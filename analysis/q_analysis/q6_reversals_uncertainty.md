## Synthesis (300 words)

Uncertainty reversals — meetings where FOMC minutes score higher on UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED than the underlying transcript — reveal a systematic asymmetry in how the two document types process epistemic content. The reversal is not random noise; it follows predictable mechanisms.

The dominant mechanism, present in at least seven of ten cases, is what might be called boilerplate uncertainty amplification. Minutes are crafted as institutional documents that must represent the collective epistemic state of the Committee. In practice, this means that minority hedges and acknowledged unknowns get systematically weighted more heavily in the synthesis than they were in the original discussion. The minutes writer faces an incentive to catalog uncertainty rather than suppress it — recording that "the outlook was subject to considerable uncertainty" is the institutionally safe formulation, even when the meeting discussion was dominated by confident voices. The 1996-12 case is the clearest illustration: Meyer explicitly noted the GDP forecast range was "remarkably tight" at 2.0–2.4%, yet the minutes produced "a great deal of uncertainty" on the inflation outlook.

The second mechanism is minority amplification. Transcripts preserve the full distribution of views: Broaddus declaring overheating imminent sits alongside Parry noting forecast uncertainty. The transcript score reflects this full distribution, including the confident tail. The minutes suppress the confident tail while preserving the hedging language. This is not distortion; it is institutional filtering. Minority uncertainty views survive the synthesis process better than minority confidence views, because confidence is subsumed in the consensus action while uncertainty survives as the stated rationale for patience or conditionality.

These reversals cluster heavily in 1993–1999 because the Greenspan-era FOMC faced a genuine structural puzzle — the missing inflation at tight labor markets — that invited the uncertainty-amplification mechanism to operate strongly. The transcript district reporters experienced real local tightness (vivid, confident); the minutes writers faced the structural question (uncertain). The same data produced different uncertainty readings depending on the vantage point of the document.

The practical implication for empirical researchers is clear: uncertainty reversals are not measurement error in either source. They are evidence that transcript-based and minutes-based uncertainty measures capture different things — the former the distribution of individual views at a moment, the latter the Committee's institutionalized representation of its own epistemic state.
