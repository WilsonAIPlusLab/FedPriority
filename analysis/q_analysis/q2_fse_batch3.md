## 300-Word Synthesis

Across the ten meetings in this batch, three mechanisms of FSE compression recur with striking consistency, regardless of era, context, or gap size.

**Mechanism 1: Speaker-to-aggregate substitution.** In every meeting, the transcript records two to seven named participants devoting substantive remarks to financial stability — often multiple paragraphs, structured analyses, or explicit scenario discussions. The minutes replace this with "a few participants noted" or "a couple of participants expressed concern." This substitution is not merely cosmetic: it erases the identities of senior officials who raised alarms. Greenspan calling the 1995 market "the edge of a bond and stock bubble," Prell describing the 2000 equity market as "a speculative bubble of extraordinary proportions," Yellen in 2012 saying financial stability was "flashing red," Rosengren in 2011 naming a specific SIFI by stock price and CDS spread — all of these collapse into anonymous aggregate counts.

**Mechanism 2: Naming-to-neutralizing.** The transcript often contains named institutions, named countries, and named products as the subject of concern. The minutes systematically generalize: BofA at $7/share and >200 CDS becomes "financial markets were volatile"; Greece's election as a tail risk becomes "downside risks to foreign growth"; the volatility ETF episode becomes "investment strategies predicated on a low-volatility environment." The minutes preserve the category (financial stability) while eliminating the specificity that gives the concern analytical weight.

**Mechanism 3: Forward-looking alarm suppressed.** The transcript recurrently contains explicit forward scenarios — a possible equity correction if profits disappoint (2002-01-30), a wave of insolvencies (2020-04-29), leverage buildup from QE (2012-12-12), bubble downside risk understated by the Greenbook (2000-05-16). The minutes retain backward-looking characterizations of current conditions while stripping forward-looking alarm. This is the mechanism most directly relevant to crisis foreshadowing: concerns about trajectories rather than states are nearly always cut.

The pattern across all three mechanisms is uniform: the minutes record that financial stability was discussed; they do not record what was said.

## Batch-Level Observations

**Era distribution**: This batch spans five distinct eras — mid-1990s expansion (1995 meetings), late-1990s dot-com peak (2000), early-2000s post-recession (2002 meetings), QE era (2011–2015), late-cycle expansion (2018), and pandemic (2020). The -22 to -23 FSE compression gap is consistent across all eras, strongly suggesting the compression mechanism is structural rather than context-dependent.

**Pre-crisis foreshadowing eliminated in 5 of 10 meetings**:
- 1995-12-19 and 1995-03-28: Bond and equity bubble language cut as markets were approaching their pre-1996 peak
- 2000-05-16: Staff chief economist's explicit bubble call cut at the precise peak of the dot-com bubble
- 2002-09-24: Housing speculation warnings and equity-extraction mechanics cut — the earliest internal FOMC signals of what became the housing bubble
- 2012-12-12: QE-era leverage-buildup concern cut at the meeting that doubled the monthly purchase pace

**Special-topic go-rounds not reflected**: The 2018-05-02, 2020-04-29, and 2015-01-28 meetings all had formal dedicated financial stability agenda items (go-rounds, Day 1 structured presentations). In each case, the minutes reflect less FSE emphasis than a standard meeting would suggest, much less a meeting with an explicit FSE go-round.

**Absolute vs. relative suppression**: The 2020-04-29 meeting is notable because the FSE gap is -22 even though minutes FSE score is 60 (well above baseline). The transcript score is 82 (near-dominant). This demonstrates that even when the minutes record meaningful FSE content, the compression mechanism still operates — the absolute level of suppression in a crisis meeting is larger than in a normal meeting, even if the relative suppression rate appears similar.
