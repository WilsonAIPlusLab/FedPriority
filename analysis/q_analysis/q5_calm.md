## Dimension-Specific Patterns in Calm Periods

### DISSENT_INTENSITY: Systematic and Consistent

Dissent compression is **the most consistent baseline pattern** across all 12 calm-period meetings. Every meeting except 2014-04-30 shows meaningful compression. Gaps range from 5 to 26 points. The mechanism is identical across all eras and chairs: the minutes report only the formal vote outcome (unanimous or named single dissenter), while the transcript records the full spectrum of voiced reluctance, alternative preferences, and analytical disagreements.

The pattern holds even when there are no formal dissents. Meetings with zero formal dissents but 4–6 reluctant holders (1996, 1997, 1999, 2005, 2017) show gaps of 10–26 points because the minutes render the outcome — "all members supported" — while the transcript records the deliberation. This is not selective compression; it is the structural difference between a record of decisions and a record of deliberation. But the effect is indistinguishable from editorial choice.

### FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS: Systematically Compressed

FSE compression is the **second most consistent pattern**. Of the 12 meetings, 10 show meaningful FSE compression (MILD or higher). The two exceptions are 2006-03-28 (unusual convergence overall) and 2006-09-20 (where FSE scores converge because the housing discussion dominates both sources).

The compression is qualitatively specific: anecdotal detail disappears. In 1997, Jordan's "no standards" lending and Boehne's "30 percent more than a new acquisition is worth" quotes are replaced by a single sentence noting increased speculative nonresidential building "but not reminiscent of the 1980s." In 2006, Warsh's "euphoria" and Bies's subprime ARM warnings are reduced to "still-supportive financial conditions." In 2017, Fischer's multi-segment FSE briefing covering shadow banking, FHLB maturity transformation, and asset valuations across markets collapses to two sentences about regulatory resilience.

The consistent direction of FSE compression is **toward dismissal**: the minutes invariably reframe financial stability concerns as monitored but not alarming. In no case does the minutes convey a higher FSE score than the transcript.

### UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED: Asymmetric and Era-Dependent

UE compression is directionally inconsistent across meetings, which distinguishes it from DISSENT and FSE compression. In some meetings (1996-05-21, 1999-05-18), the minutes inflate uncertainty above the transcript level — adding "considerable" and "substantial" qualifiers to aggregate the range of participant views. In other meetings (2017-05-03, 2018-06-13), the minutes reduce uncertainty by removing named participants' strong uncertainty statements and replacing them with "roughly balanced" risk assessments.

This asymmetry reflects a distinct editorial mechanism: the minutes do not selectively suppress uncertainty but instead **normalize** it. Named participant statements that convey strong personal uncertainty get aggregated into collective language that often falls at the median rather than conveying the distributional range. Whether this inflates or deflates uncertainty depends on whether the transcript's mode is more or less uncertain than its range.

## Synthesis: Baseline Compression vs. Crisis Compression (400 words)

The twelve calm-period meetings document that editorial compression is not a crisis response — it is an institutional default. Three compression patterns operate continuously, regardless of economic conditions, and can be distinguished by their consistency, direction, and magnitude.

**Dissent compression is the most structurally embedded baseline pattern.** Across all four chairs and across all twelve meetings, the minutes consistently reduce the visible spectrum of disagreement. When there are no formal dissents, the minutes render outcomes as unanimous while the transcripts record five or six reluctant holders whose analytical disagreements were substantive. The 1996 "revolt of the nonvoters," the 1997 meeting where at least six participants preferred immediate tightening, and the 1999 meeting where roughly half the room wanted a 25bp hike that day — all appear in the minutes as smooth consensus. The compression magnitude in calm periods (gaps of 5–26 points) is not clearly smaller than in crisis periods; this is primarily a structural feature of the decision-record format rather than a crisis-specific choice.

**FSE compression is directional and consistent: stability concerns are systematically softened.** Ten of twelve calm meetings show FSE compression, and in every case the direction is the same — vivid anecdotes, named warnings about speculative excess, and explicitly stated concerns about frothy markets are replaced by brief contextual mentions or omitted entirely. The 1997 "no standards" lending anecdotes, the 2006 "euphoria" language, the 2017 Fischer FSE briefing, and the 2018 leveraged-loan concerns all disappear or become unrecognizable in the minutes. This pattern predates the post-GFC era of formal FSE briefings and appears consistently throughout the Greenspan period. Crisis-period FSE compression would be larger in absolute magnitude because the transcript concerns are more severe, but the directional mechanism — compress stability risks toward dismissal — is unchanged from calm periods.

**Uncertainty compression is asymmetric and less predictable.** Unlike dissent and FSE, uncertainty can be inflated or deflated depending on whether the minutes aggregate upward or downward from the participant distribution. This makes uncertainty the least reliable baseline compressor and suggests it is driven less by institutional editorial policy and more by how the summary writer handles distributional information.

**The key implication for crisis comparison** is that crisis-period compression is not qualitatively different from calm-period compression — it is the same mechanisms operating on more extreme underlying material. What changes during crises is not the direction or type of compression but its potential consequence: compressing a warning about "some speculative activity" in 1997 has lower information cost than compressing "imminent systemic risk" in 2008. The baseline rate of dissent and FSE compression in calm periods means that the crisis-period record is systematically less alarming than the deliberation, but it would have been similarly sanitized even if the crises had been milder.
