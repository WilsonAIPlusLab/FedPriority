## Synthesis (300 words)

Across all ten meetings in Batch 4, a single structural mechanism drives the FSE compression: the minutes transform named-speaker stability analysis into either economic-context boilerplate or silence. The transcript consistently records two to five participants devoting substantive remarks to financial stability as an independent topic — with named data, historical comparisons, and explicit concern language. The minutes consistently compress this into a passing clause at best, or omit it entirely.

Four compression modes appear with regularity. First, **analytical elimination**: rich speaker analysis (Ferguson on risk appetite tensions, Olson on CLO B-tranche accumulation, Tarullo on ON RRP systemic risks, Rosengren on $1.6 trillion in MMF European exposure) is dropped without residue. Second, **reframing as economic context**: equity markets, credit spreads, and leverage trends that speakers analyze as stability threats are relabeled in minutes as conditions "supporting growth" or "reflecting improved expectations." Third, **attribution removal**: minutes use "some participants" for concerns that were individually named, specific, and data-driven in transcripts, reducing their apparent weight. Fourth, **superlative deletion**: the most alarming language — Greenspan's "downright scary," Ferguson's "fundamental tension...most unusual," Minehan's "increasingly vulnerable" — is systematically absent from the minutes record.

The crisis-foreshadowing finding is the most notable: in the 2000-02-02 meeting, three senior participants independently identified dot-com bubble fragility, yet the minutes contain no stability concern whatsoever. In 2010-12-14, Rosengren's precise calculation of $1.6 trillion in MMF European exposure was cut from the minutes just as the European sovereign crisis was intensifying. In 2018-09-26, multiple participants documented the leveraged loan buildup and nonbank credit loosening as pre-crisis indicators; the minutes reduced this to a single subordinate clause.

The pattern is consistent with an institutional minutes-writing norm that treats financial stability discussion as expert commentary rather than policy-relevant content, regardless of how forcefully it was raised in the room.
