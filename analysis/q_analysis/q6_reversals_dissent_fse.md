## Synthesis: What Reversals Reveal About the Editorial Process

The ten reversal cases collectively expose three distinct mechanisms by which FOMC minutes can record higher DISSENT_INTENSITY or FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS scores than the underlying transcripts.

**The dominant mechanism, accounting for six of the eight FSE reversals, is structural format mismatch.** Four meetings (2012-03-13, 2007-06-28, 2006-10-25, 2008-06-25) had their scored transcripts drawn from special-topic subsessions — communications workshops, inflation-targeting framework debates, or liquidity facility reviews — rather than from the standard economic go-around and policy deliberation. In each case, the special-topic session had no occasion for substantive financial stability or dissent content. The minutes, covering the complete multi-day meeting, routinely included the suppressed economic go-round. The score reversal does not reflect editorial choices so much as it reflects a mechanical documentation asymmetry: the scored transcript file was pages 134–186 of 213, not pages 1–213. This is the most important finding: when FSE minutes > transcript by a large margin (gaps of +30 or more), the proximate cause is almost always a transcript that covers a non-standard session.

**The second mechanism, accounting for most DI reversals and smaller FSE gaps, is editorial aggregation and taxonomization.** The FOMC minutes are not a summary of what was said but a synthetic document structured around analytical categories. When scattered participant comments address a topic obliquely — as brief asides in a taper debate or passing references to equity valuations in a growth discussion — the minutes' synthesizing editors collect them under a labeled heading, assign quantifiers ("many participants," "a number expressed concerns"), and create a paragraph with structural weight that the individual transcript moments lacked. The 1993 DI reversal illustrates this cleanly: the transcript shows near-total consensus, while the minutes introduce a "some members concerned about firming" versus "others who could not rule out easing" typology that inflates the apparent disagreement. Similarly, the 2013-03-20 FSE gap (+15) arises because a distributed taper-debate discussion, touching financial stability as a secondary argument, was reassembled in the minutes as a dedicated formal review section.

**The third mechanism — operating residually in the small-gap cases — is measurement noise.** Four FSE reversals have gaps of +7 or less (2000-08-22, 2006-10-25, 2015-07-29, 2016-11-02). These all fall within or close to the combined uncertainty bands of the two scores. Even without any editorial choice or structural mismatch, sampling variance and scorer judgment at the margins would generate gaps of this magnitude. They should not be interpreted as evidence of systematic editorial emphasis.

The overall pattern carries a methodological implication: large FSE reversals (gaps > 20) are almost always STRUCTURAL_FORMAT artifacts and should be flagged as incomparable meeting pairs. Small reversals (gaps < 10) are probably MEASUREMENT_NOISE. Moderate reversals in the +10 to +20 range are the zone where EDITORIAL_EMPHASIS genuinely operates — where editorial decisions about how to label, aggregate, and weight participant discussion produce a different impression than the transcript record would alone.

This is directionally consistent with the broader finding from cross-method convergence work: the minutes are not a compressed version of the transcripts. They are a reconstructed document whose editorial choices systematically shape the apparent salience of topics. For financial stability and dissent specifically, those choices tend — when they operate — to amplify rather than suppress.
