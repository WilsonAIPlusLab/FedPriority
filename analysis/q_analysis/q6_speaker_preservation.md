## Part 4: Synthesis — 400 Words

### Who Gets Preserved vs. Cut, and What It Reveals About the Editorial Process

The most striking finding across all ten meetings is absolute: **no named individual is attributed by name in the participant discussion sections of the minutes, in any meeting, for any dimension**. The transformation from transcript to minutes is not a partial suppression of names — it is a total elimination of named attribution, without exception, across more than 90 speaker-meeting instances examined.

Within this universal anonymization, a clear hierarchy of preservation quality emerges. **The Chair's institutional voice survives best**. Bernanke's economic assessments, Yellen's accommodation framing, and Powell's "mid-cycle adjustment" language survive, but as anonymous statements of committee consensus rather than attributed views. The Chair's role in these documents is as the voice that translates into institutional prose — yet the transcripts reveal that every Chair here explicitly acknowledged lacking full committee support, and those acknowledgments disappear entirely.

**Governors fare materially worse than their institutional weight suggests**. This is counter-intuitive. Governors are permanent voting members, physically proximate to the Board's communications apparatus, and often the most technically sophisticated analysts in the room (Brainard on CCyB, Warsh on CDO mechanics, Tarullo on leveraged lending, Duke on banking credit quality). Yet their detailed analytical contributions are consistently the first casualties of compression. The minutes do not distinguish between a Governor's carefully constructed empirical case and a brief District-condition observation from a non-voting Regional President — both disappear into "several participants noted."

**Non-voting Regional Presidents are systematically erased**. Plosser's would-dissent declaration, Kocherlakota's would-dissent declaration, Evans's "woefully inadequate" position, Rosengren's seven-year arc of detailed financial stability analysis across CDOs, WeWork, ETFs, and bank dividends — all absent. The transcripts show that non-voting Regional Presidents often provide the most specific, data-rich, and analytically distinctive contributions precisely because they feel free to stake out positions without the constraint of voting accountability. The minutes receive none of it.

**Formal dissents appear only as bare vote tallies**. Hoenig's "I think this is a mistake," Fisher's "way too much," Lacker's "I will dissent," George's "seize the day" — these appear in the transcripts as substantive, reasoned positions with articulated justifications. In the minutes, they appear as "one member preferred" or appear implicitly in the vote count. The reasoning, the urgency, the framework disagreement: erased.

What this reveals about the editorial process is institutional consensus manufacturing at scale. The minutes are not a summary of what participants said — they are a reconstruction of what the institution concluded. Individual views are inputs to a synthetic collective output, and the synthesis systematically narrows the distribution: outlier concerns are compressed, intensity is reduced, and attribution is dissolved. The minutes present a committee speaking with a single, modulated voice that no individual participant actually used.
