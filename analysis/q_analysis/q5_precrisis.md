## Synthesis: How the Editorial Process Handles Pre-Crisis Early Warning Signals (400 words)

Across twelve pre-crisis meetings spanning five major crises, the evidence establishes a consistent and consequential pattern: the FOMC editorial process systematically compresses the specificity, intensity, and attribution of early warning signals while preserving the institutional consensus outcome.

**The mechanism is not suppression but dissolution.** Named, vivid, data-grounded warnings in the transcript are translated into collective attribution in the minutes. Bies's technically detailed ARM/option-ARM supervisory briefing — naming payment shock, negative amortization, and subprime products deteriorating to the point of triggering accounting disclosure requirements — became "a few participants voiced concerns." Bernanke's statement that "some conduits of credit are simply closed or frozen" became "markets for securities backed by subprime and other non-traditional mortgages had become illiquid." Rosengren's explicit judgment that coronavirus was "a greater risk than currently priced in by markets" became "a new risk... which participants agreed warranted close watching." In each case, the warning survived but lost the specific voice, specific data, and specific urgency that made it actionable.

**The FSE dimension is the primary casualty.** Financial Stability Emphasis scores show the largest and most consistent transcript-to-minutes compression of any dimension across all twelve meetings, with gaps ranging from 5 to 27 points. The 2005 Bies briefing (Δ27), the 2007-08-07 GFC moment (Δ25), the 2019-12-11 valuation warning (Δ13), and the 1998-08-18 LTCM warning (Δ13) all show the same structure: individual participants naming specific mechanisms, with collective synthesis removing the mechanism while retaining a vague acknowledgment that concerns existed.

**Dissent compression is a secondary but structurally related pattern.** When participants disagree about crisis severity — as they did sharply at 2007-08-07 (named camps on whether financial stress was serious), at 2000-11-15 (four named participants preferring balanced risks), at 2000-12-19 (Gramlich and Poole explicitly wanting to cut) — the minutes record the consensus outcome without the intensity of the disagreement. This matters for early warning because the presence of a serious minority view is itself a signal that something unusual is being perceived. Collapsing "everyone supported the decision" obscures when "supporting the decision" required explicit persuasion.

**The reassurance pattern is real but limited.** Several meetings show stabilizing language added in the minutes not present in the transcript — most starkly the 2005 and 2008 cases where "current financial conditions consistent with sustained growth" appeared alongside or instead of supervisory warnings. But the more dominant pattern is omission rather than addition: the minutes do not fabricate optimism so much as they remove the pessimism.

**The net effect on the public record is systematic understatement of pre-crisis fragility.** In every case where the transcript contained a named, specific, data-grounded financial stability warning, the minutes conveyed a softer, collectively attributed, less specific version of the same concern. This is not evidence of bad faith — the minutes are a consensus document, not a verbatim transcript. But it means the published record of what the Federal Reserve knew and said is structurally biased toward the median institutional view and away from the early-warning outlier view that, in retrospect, identified the crisis correctly.
