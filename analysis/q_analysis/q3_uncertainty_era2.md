### 2005-03-22 | UE gap: +10 (REVERSAL)

**Uncertainty expressed — Transcript (38):**
The transcript is notable for the presence of Poole's explicit counterweight: "This expansion is about as surprise-free as we ever see." The baseline is a well-understood expansion on steady footing. Moskow: "Quantifying the extent of the inflation risk is difficult," but this is standard risk management language. Pianalto: "I can find myself being either hopeful or worried." Overall, uncertainty is below the era average — the committee is managing a hiking cycle in a strong, relatively legible economy.

**Uncertainty expressed — Minutes (48):**
"Many participants indicated that their uncertainty about the intensity of inflation pressures had risen." "Considerable uncertainty persisted about longer-run prospects for productivity growth, unit labor costs, and cost pressures." "They had become less certain of that outlook for the next few quarters."

**WHY THE REVERSAL OCCURS:**

This case illustrates a selection effect in minutes drafting. The minutes summarize the distribution of views across participants, and minority views expressing concern are given disproportionate weight in the summary relative to their weight in the full discussion. The transcript contains:
- Poole's "surprise-free" statement (explicitly low uncertainty, fully confident)
- Ferguson's "long-term inflation expectations continue to be well anchored" (confidence signal)
- Multiple participants presenting their baseline as clear even while noting risks

The minutes omit or underweight the confident baseline and overweight the uncertainty qualifications. The collective quantifier "many participants indicated their uncertainty had risen" applies to the vocal concerned minority, not to the full room. In the transcript, the uncertainty expressions exist alongside confident baseline views; in the minutes, the confident baselines largely disappear while the uncertainty clauses are retained and amplified.

This is **asymmetric uncertainty selection**: the minutes drafting process preferentially retains hedges and uncertainty language, even when the dominant transcript register is confident. In normal (crisis-era) meetings, this effect operates additively (uncertainty is already high, but the minutes still cut it). In calm-era meetings, it can operate inversely — amplifying minority uncertainty language over the confident majority baseline.

**Confidence projection flag:** INVERTED. Minutes project less confidence than the transcript.

## Section 3: Synthesis — Uncertainty Compression Patterns in the 2005–2011 Era

The dominant structural pattern in this sample is what could be called **epistemic laundering**: the conversion of explicit, named, first-person uncertainty — including direct confessions of ignorance, statements about broken analytical tools, and acknowledgments of Knightian non-calculability — into routinized institutional risk language ("subject to downside risks," "uncertainty remained elevated," "risks roughly balanced"). The laundering operates along several consistent channels.

**The most common type cut is personal hedging erasure.** In nine of ten meetings (2005-03-22 being the exception), the minutes systematically remove first-person uncertainty language. Bernanke's "walking on eggshells" (2009-12), his "I think uncertainty is greater than average right now" (2010-01), and Lockhart's threefold "we simply don't know" structure (2011-08) all disappear. The institutional voice of the minutes cannot sustain first-person vulnerability, so it converts individual epistemic confessions into depersonalized collective descriptors. This is not dishonest — the sentiment is real — but it materially changes the register from lived uncertainty to managed risk.

**The second most common cut is model and tool uncertainty.** The GFC-era meetings (2007-08, 2009-06, 2009-12) are distinctive because participants explicitly question whether standard analytical frameworks apply. Bullard (2009-12) says econometric models from the last 25 years may not apply; Geithner (2007-08) says available information is "stale and uneven"; Warsh (2007-08) admits inability to assess second- and third-order consequences. None of these appear in the minutes. The minutes document uncertainty about outcomes; they systematically omit uncertainty about the reliability of the tools used to generate those outcome assessments — a qualitatively deeper form of epistemic humility that the institutional form cannot retain.

**Tail risk language undergoes partial rather than full erasure.** Kohn's "tails lot fatter" (2007-08), Warsh's "nonlinear outcomes" (2010-04), and Lockhart's "fat tails on the downside" (2010-12) do not appear in their respective minutes. This is structurally significant: fat-tail language is rhetorical evidence that participants are assigning meaningful probability to scenarios outside the Gaussian uncertainty range described by standard "upside and downside risks" framing. The minutes' default form — "risks to the outlook were roughly balanced" — implicitly symmetrizes and normalizes the distribution in a way the transcripts explicitly do not.

**The 2005-03-22 reversal is diagnostic, not anomalous.** In that meeting, the minutes score higher on uncertainty than the transcript because of asymmetric retention — the drafting process preferentially preserves hedges from the concerned minority over the confident baselines of the majority. Poole's "expansion is as surprise-free as we ever see" and Ferguson's "expectations well anchored" do not appear in the minutes; the minority's concerns about inflation uncertainty are amplified via "many participants." This reveals the selection mechanism operating in reverse: when uncertainty is genuinely low, the mechanism produces a positive UE gap by amplifying minority concerns. When uncertainty is high, the same mechanism still operates but is overwhelmed by the overall level, producing the negative gaps seen in the GFC-era meetings.

**The three-dissent constraint at 2011-08-09 is a natural experiment.** The smallest gap in the sample (−5 or −8 depending on scoring) occurs in the meeting with the most formal dissents. Three dissents force the minutes to record genuine disagreement — the institutional text cannot project consensus where the vote record refutes it. This implies that the uncertainty compression mechanism operates partly through the social function of projecting Committee coherence. Disagreement-as-uncertainty (the widest spread of views in the room) is the category most strongly protected from erasure when formal votes make it visible, and most strongly erased when disagreement remains conversational.

**Era-level implication.** The 2005–2011 sample lies in the transitional window between the low-uncertainty pre-GFC environment and the systematically high-gap post-2012 era (where the overall compression reaches −9.4). The compression here is already substantial during the GFC core (gaps of −15 to −17), but is partially offset by calmer meetings where the reversal mechanism operates. The era-average gap of approximately −5 to −7 reflects this mixture. What is consistent across the full era is the mechanism: minutes strip personal hedging, model doubt, and tail-risk quantification; retain outcome-uncertainty language in depersonalized form; and add "roughly balanced" or similar confidence-projecting qualifiers that the transcripts do not support.
