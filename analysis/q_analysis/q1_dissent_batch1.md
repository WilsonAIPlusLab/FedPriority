## Synthesis: Dominant Compression Mechanisms (~300 words)

Across all 10 meetings, five compression mechanisms recur, but they operate with different weights depending on meeting type.

**Anonymization combined with downgrade quantifiers** is the most pervasive mechanism, appearing in every case. Named individuals who express explicit non-support — Plosser saying "I cannot support alternative B," Fisher saying "75bp is way too much," Poole saying "I would, with respect, dissent" — are dissolved into vague collectives: "a few members," "some participants," "two members." The numerical shrinkage is often dramatic: a 5-person hawk camp in 1999-03-30 and 1999-05-18 becomes "all members agreed"; 3 named non-supporters in 2014-12-17 become "two members." The direction of compression is almost always toward the majority position, not toward a midpoint.

**Omission of quasi-dissents and near-dissents** is the second dominant mechanism. The minutes record formal voting dissents inconsistently (2008-03-18 omits both Fisher and Hoenig), but they are nearly silent on quasi-dissents — participants who state they "would dissent if voting," reach a straw-vote threshold, or explicitly acknowledge their position is "too far outside the consensus." The 2003-08-12 floor vote (7/18 favoring removal of "considerable period") is perhaps the clearest example: an internal vote that nearly reached majority status leaves no trace in the minutes.

**Reframing active opposition as shared uncertainty** is the most structurally consequential mechanism. In 2008-03-18, two formal voting dissents and a vigorous framework debate become "appropriately calibrating the stance of policy was difficult" — transforming individual opposition into collective epistemic humility. In 2011-01-26, Fisher's "I could argue for alternative C or D" becomes "a few members remained unsure of the likely effects." The dissenter's agency — the expression of a different preferred policy — is converted into doubt, which is a far more deniable and less legible form of disagreement.

**Vote/discussion gap suppression** operates quietly but consistently. In 2010-08-10, 2011-11-02, and 2014-12-17, participants vote with the majority while expressing strong reservations in discussion; the minutes record the vote outcome but not the conditional, reluctant, or cost-benefit character of the support. "All members agreed" papers over agreement achieved through chairmanship deference, not genuine consensus.

**The structural asymmetry** across all cases: minutes reliably report unanimous or near-unanimous consensus language even when transcripts reveal two-sided splits. The 1999 meetings are especially instructive because the disagreements were directional but non-dissenting — everyone voted the same way, yet the transcript reveals that 5 of 18 participants explicitly preferred a different action. This pattern suggests the minutes' consensus framing serves an institutional legitimacy function independent of any intent to deceive: the published record performs unity while the transcript preserves deliberation.
