# Editorial Compression in FOMC Minutes: A Full-Sample Synthesis
## 216 Meetings, 1993–2020

**Dataset**: master_mechanisms.csv — 216 meetings, 1993-02-03 to 2020-12-16
**Analysis date**: 2026-03-23
**Coverage**: Four chairs (Greenspan, Bernanke, Yellen, Powell); four economic regimes (calm, pre-crisis, crisis, recovery)

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This document synthesizes findings that are only possible with the full 216-meeting sample. Prior analyses of FOMC editorial compression have relied on deep-analysis subsamples of approximately 110 meetings; the full-coverage dataset adds statistical weight that converts suggestive patterns into robust findings and, in several cases, produces results qualitatively different from what subsamples implied.

The headline statistics across 216 meetings spanning 27 years and four chairs are as follows. ANONYMIZATION — the dissolution of named individuals into collective quantifiers — appears in 97.2% of meetings (210/216). SPECIFICITY_STRIPPING — the removal of numerical and institutional detail — appears in 97.7% (211/216). MODIFIER_SOFTENING — the attenuation of strong evaluative language — appears in 86.6% (187/216). These three mechanisms are not occasionally activated in response to unusual deliberations; they are structural features of every FOMC minutes document. The combined gap between transcript and minutes scores (dissent intensity + financial stability emphasis + uncertainty expressed) averages 18.6 points across all meetings, rises to 24.4 points during crisis, and never reaches zero. Across all 216 meetings, not a single one records all three gaps simultaneously at or below 2 points.

Beyond these baselines, the full-sample data resolve three open questions from the subsample analysis: chair fingerprints are statistically stable, not artifacts of small samples; the foreshadowing problem is systematic and concentrated in pre-crisis regimes; and the "no zero" compression finding is not a feature of crisis periods alone but holds across calm, pre-crisis, crisis, and recovery without exception.

## 2. The Compression Baseline: What Happens in Every Meeting

The three mechanisms that appear in over 85% of meetings constitute what can properly be called the "always-on" layer of FOMC editorial compression.

SPECIFICITY_STRIPPING operates in 97.7% of meetings (211/216), making it the most prevalent single mechanism in the dataset. Its near-universality reflects a structural feature of how minutes are composed: the document genre does not accommodate named institutions, specific percentages, or benchmark comparisons. When a participant cites a precise leverage ratio, names a financial institution, or references a specific percentage deterioration, the minutes format has no slot for that information. Stripping is therefore not an editorial judgment in most cases — it is a genre constraint.

ANONYMIZATION operates in 97.2% of meetings (210/216). Its prevalence is similarly structural: the minutes participant-discussion sections are written in the third-person collective. Named attribution would require either a radical format change or selective choices about which voices to name — both of which would create communication problems of their own. The near-universality of anonymization reflects its zero-alternative status in the current format.

MODIFIER_SOFTENING operates in 86.6% of meetings (187/216). Unlike the first two mechanisms, which are essentially 100% structural, modifier softening involves more genuine editorial discretion. The 13.4% of meetings where it does not appear are meetings where the transcript language was already calibrated, where the gap between spoken register and institutional register was minimal. This is the one mechanism whose absence is informative: meetings without modifier softening are meetings where participants' spoken language was already institutionally calibrated.

The joint distribution is the most important fact: zero of 216 meetings show all three of these mechanisms absent. No FOMC meeting in 27 years produced minutes free of specificity stripping, anonymization, and modifier softening simultaneously. Editorial compression is therefore not episodic — it is not activated by unusual events, contested decisions, or financial stress. It is the default condition of the document, operating in every meeting including the most routine, the most consensual, and the most financially tranquil.

The practical implication for research is direct. A researcher who uses FOMC minutes as a source for any of the three constructs these mechanisms affect — the distribution of individual views, the specificity of financial stability concerns, or the intensity of evaluative language — is working with a document that has been systematically transformed relative to its deliberative source in every single meeting in the sample. Assuming that compression only matters for "unusual" meetings is not supported by the data.

## 3. The Compression Hierarchy

Ranking all 14 identified mechanisms by prevalence across 216 meetings reveals a three-tier structure that reflects the institutional priorities embedded in FOMC communication.

**Tier 1 (>85% of meetings): Identity erasure, detail stripping, and tone softening**

| Mechanism | Count | Prevalence |
|---|---|---|
| SPECIFICITY_STRIPPING | 211 | 97.7% |
| ANONYMIZATION | 210 | 97.2% |
| MODIFIER_SOFTENING | 187 | 86.6% |

These mechanisms operate so broadly because they correspond to the minutes' basic genre requirements. The minutes speak collectively, in general terms, in a calibrated institutional register. Every departure from this register that the transcript contains must be corrected.

**Tier 2 (20–40% of meetings): Dissent management and epistemic laundering**

| Mechanism | Count | Prevalence |
|---|---|---|
| QUASI_DISSENT_OMISSION | 86 | 39.8% |
| QUANTIFIER_DOWNGRADE | 79 | 36.6% |
| MECHANISM_STRIPPING | 57 | 26.4% |
| EPISTEMIC_LAUNDERING | 43 | 19.9% |

Tier 2 mechanisms are activated when the meeting content departs from the institutional norm in a specific direction: contested deliberations (QUASI_DISSENT_OMISSION, QUANTIFIER_DOWNGRADE), analytically rich financial stability discussions (MECHANISM_STRIPPING), or explicit expressions of deep uncertainty (EPISTEMIC_LAUNDERING). These are not random — they cluster in pre-crisis and crisis regimes, and they spike for specific chairs. Their 20–40% prevalence means they are common but not universal, activated by content rather than format.

**Tier 3 (<15% of meetings): Rare and specialized mechanisms**

| Mechanism | Count | Prevalence |
|---|---|---|
| INTENSITY_REDUCTION | 27 | 12.5% |
| CONFIDENCE_PROJECTION | 11 | 5.1% |
| SESSION_ERASURE | 9 | 4.2% |
| TOPIC_OMISSION | 8 | 3.7% |
| VOTE_DISCUSSION_GAP | 6 | 2.8% |
| REFRAME_AS_UNCERTAINTY | 5 | 2.3% |
| CONDITIONAL_INSERTION | 4 | 1.9% |

Tier 3 mechanisms are activated only under specific, relatively rare conditions: multi-day sessions with a Day 1 devoted to special topics (SESSION_ERASURE), acute forward-guidance credibility constraints (CONFIDENCE_PROJECTION), narrow vote margins with procedural deliberation visible on tape (VOTE_DISCUSSION_GAP). Their rarity is itself informative — these mechanisms require unusual trigger conditions that most meetings do not supply.

The hierarchy's logic is therefore: Tier 1 mechanisms are always-on because they reflect genre constraints; Tier 2 mechanisms are conditionally activated based on meeting content; Tier 3 mechanisms are activated only by rare structural or procedural conditions. What determines whether a mechanism is Tier 1 versus Tier 3 is not the severity of what it suppresses, but the frequency with which the relevant triggering condition arises. Session erasure (Tier 3) may suppress more content in a single meeting than MODIFIER_SOFTENING does — but special multi-day sessions are rare, while departures from institutional register are universal.

## 4. Chair Fingerprints: Statistical Evidence

With 97, 64, 31, and 24 meetings respectively per chair, the full-sample data provide sufficient statistical weight to treat cross-chair differences as genuine signatures rather than sampling artifacts.

**Greenspan (97 meetings, combined gap mean 17.4)**

The Greenspan editorial fingerprint is defined by the highest MECHANISM_STRIPPING rate in the dataset — 38% (37/97), compared to 25% for Bernanke, 17% for Powell, and 0% for Yellen — and the highest TOPIC_OMISSION rate at 6% (6/97). These two figures tell the same story: the Greenspan era's compression was applied to the substance of arguments, not merely their attribution or intensity. When district presidents arrived with named businesses, specific percentages, and data-grounded causal chains, the minutes removed the reasoning while retaining the conclusion. TOPIC_OMISSION at 6% — also concentrated in Greenspan meetings — represents the most aggressive form of compression, where entire areas of discussion are omitted from the published record.

Greenspan simultaneously shows the lowest QUANTIFIER_DOWNGRADE rate at 24%, below Bernanke (45%), Yellen (52%), and Powell (46%). This is consistent with the mechanism-stripping pattern: rather than reducing the apparent size of dissenting coalitions, the Greenspan editorial process preferred to remove the analytical substance that made coalitions cohere. Fewer named dissenters required quantifier adjustment when the arguments animating disagreement had already been stripped.

**Bernanke (64 meetings, combined gap mean 20.7)**

Bernanke has the highest combined gap mean (20.7) of any chair, driven by the highest dissent gap mean (8.6) across the four. The full-sample data confirm what the deep analysis of crisis meetings suggested: the Bernanke era produced the largest absolute information loss, measured in gap points, of any chairmanship in the dataset.

The mechanism fingerprint is correspondingly distinctive: highest QUASI_DISSENT_OMISSION at 59% (38/64), highest VOTE_DISCUSSION_GAP at 8% (5/64), and highest CONFIDENCE_PROJECTION at 8% (5/64). These three mechanisms together describe a specific editorial challenge: managing highly visible, explicitly stated dissent in a period when unconventional policy generated genuine disagreement about direction. QUASI_DISSENT_OMISSION at 59% means that in nearly three of five Bernanke-era meetings, participants who stated a preference for a different policy outcome — without being formal dissenters — were editorially merged into the consensus. VOTE_DISCUSSION_GAP at 8% reflects the relatively high frequency of meetings where narrow vote margins or explicit procedural discussion appeared on tape but not in minutes. CONFIDENCE_PROJECTION at 8% indicates active reframing — the insertion of balanced-risk language into meetings where the transcript record was asymmetrically pessimistic.

**Yellen (31 meetings, combined gap mean 17.5)**

The Yellen editorial fingerprint is defined by two distinctive features: the highest CONFIDENCE_PROJECTION rate at 16% (5/31) — more than double Bernanke's 8% and quadruple Greenspan's 1% — and the highest INTENSITY_REDUCTION rate at 26% (8/31). Simultaneously, Yellen shows the lowest QUASI_DISSENT_OMISSION rate at 26% (8/31), below all other chairs.

This pattern is internally coherent. The Yellen era's central challenge was not managing dissent — formal and quasi-dissents were relatively rare — but managing the tension between participants' explicit uncertainty about the Phillips curve and the forward-guidance commitments embedded in the SEP dot-plot. CONFIDENCE_PROJECTION at 16% captures the editorial response to this tension: transforming asymmetric pessimism into balanced-risk language was a structural necessity once the dot-plot had published a rate path that required minutes to maintain credibility. INTENSITY_REDUCTION at 26% reinforces the picture: strong evaluative language about model uncertainty and inflation dynamics was systematically attenuated.

The also-distinctive CONFIDENCE_PROJECTION and SESSION_ERASURE rates (10% for SESSION_ERASURE, the highest of any chair) reflect that Yellen-era meetings frequently included Day 1 special sessions on monetary policy strategy and financial stability that generated substantial transcript content with essentially no minutes coverage.

**Powell (24 meetings, combined gap mean 16.3)**

The Powell fingerprint is the most extreme in several dimensions: 100% MODIFIER_SOFTENING (24/24) and 100% ANONYMIZATION (24/24) — full activation of both always-on mechanisms with no exceptions. Simultaneously, 0% SESSION_ERASURE (0/24) and 0% CONFIDENCE_PROJECTION (0/24) — the lowest rates of any chair for both.

The joint pattern describes an era of simplified but comprehensive compression. Every meeting without exception activates the basic genre-constraint mechanisms. But the more specialized mechanisms — confidence projection, session erasure, mechanism stripping at only 17% — are comparatively less active. The Powell-era editorial process appears to apply a uniform baseline transformation to all meetings rather than activating specialized mechanisms for unusual content. This is consistent with the Powell era's institutional context: the short span of 24 meetings (covering a complete policy cycle from tightening through emergency COVID easing) provided less accumulated institutional variation than longer chairmanships.

## 5. Regime Effects: Crisis vs. Calm

The four-regime breakdown (calm: 67 meetings; pre-crisis: 22; crisis: 24; recovery: 103) reveals compression dynamics that vary substantially with economic conditions — though not in a simple monotonic pattern.

Combined gap means by regime: calm 15.3, pre-crisis 21.9, crisis 24.4, recovery 18.0. The crisis regime produces the largest aggregate gaps, but the pre-crisis regime is remarkably close (21.9 vs. 24.4), which is the analytically important finding. The meetings where the gap is worst are not only the meetings of acute stress; they are also the meetings immediately before acute stress, when early warnings are being generated and editorially suppressed.

Two mechanisms show especially sharp regime gradients. EPISTEMIC_LAUNDERING rises from 13% in calm to 50% in pre-crisis and 54% in crisis, then falls back to 10% in recovery. This trajectory follows the distribution of genuine epistemic distress across the four eras: participants express deep model uncertainty when conditions are deteriorating or have deteriorated, and that uncertainty is systematically laundered into institutional competence language precisely when it is most substantively significant. The MECHANISM_STRIPPING trajectory is similar: 19% in calm, 50% in pre-crisis, 67% in crisis, 17% in recovery.

The MODIFIER_SOFTENING pattern is more complex. Calm: 88%. Crisis: 88%. But pre-crisis: 64%. Recovery: 90%. Why does pre-crisis show the lowest modifier-softening rate despite elevated gaps overall? The answer lies in the composition of pre-crisis deliberations. When participants are beginning to identify financial stress — the 2000-2001 period, the 2006-2007 period — their language on financial stability is vivid and alarming, but their language on the macroeconomy often remains relatively confident. MECHANISM_STRIPPING and EPISTEMIC_LAUNDERING handle the financial stability content; modifier softening is applied most to evaluative language that is already somewhat cautious in register. Pre-crisis meetings generate analytical alarm (addressed by mechanism stripping) rather than evaluative alarm (addressed by modifier softening), which depresses the pre-crisis modifier-softening rate while other mechanism rates are elevated.

The regime data on QUASI_DISSENT_OMISSION confirm the crisis-compression paradox identified in the deep analysis. In the calm regime, QUASI_DISSENT_OMISSION appears in only 22% of meetings. In the crisis regime, it rises to 54% — more than double. Dissent compression intensifies during crisis, at exactly the moment when internal disagreement about unconventional policy is substantively most significant and publicly most relevant.

Consensus manufacturing statistics reinforce this reading. No calm meetings show STRONG consensus manufacturing; the crisis and pre-crisis regimes each show 1 STRONG instance (4% and 5% respectively) against NONE cases of 0% in crisis and 5% in pre-crisis. The shift from NONE to MILD consensus manufacturing is especially notable: in calm, 34% of meetings show NONE (no consensus manufacturing); in crisis, that figure is 0%. Crisis-period minutes manufacture consensus in 100% of meetings.

## 6. The Foreshadowing Problem

35.2% of meetings (76/216) contain foreshadowing content in transcripts that is compressed away in minutes — early-warning signals present on the tape but absent from, or attenuated in, the published document.

The regime distribution makes the problem concrete. In the calm regime, the foreshadowing rate is 36% (24/67). In the recovery regime it is 25% (26/103). But in the pre-crisis regime, it rises to 68% (15/22). In two out of every three pre-crisis meetings, the transcript contained early-warning signals that did not survive the editorial process.

This finding is not merely statistical. The pre-crisis regime in this dataset captures the periods preceding the dot-com crash, the Great Financial Crisis, and COVID — the three largest economic disruptions of the 1993–2020 era. In 68% of those periods' meetings, participants on tape identified the incoming stress with varying degrees of specificity. The published minutes either omitted those signals or attenuated them to generic monitoring language that was indistinguishable from routine risk acknowledgment.

The foreshadowing rate in the crisis regime itself is 46% (11/24), higher than calm or recovery. This captures a different phenomenon: meetings during acute stress where participants on tape were identifying second-order channels — contagion mechanisms, institutional vulnerabilities, policy constraint problems — that the minutes did not preserve. Even at the peak of crisis, the editorial filter continued to strip forward-looking specificity.

The chair-level foreshadowing rates are consistent with the era composition. Greenspan: 38% (37/97). Bernanke: 39% (25/64). Yellen: 29% (9/31). Powell: 21% (5/24). The lower rates for Yellen and Powell partly reflect the absence of acute pre-crisis periods in those chairmanships (the 2015-2018 financial stability concerns never developed into a crisis; COVID's pre-crisis window was extremely short). The Greenspan and Bernanke rates, which cover the dot-com and GFC pre-crisis periods respectively, are nearly identical at 38% and 39%.

## 7. Low-Gap Meetings: What Prevents Compression?

The bottom quartile of combined gaps (meetings with combined gap below approximately 12 points) provides evidence on the conditions under which compression approaches its practical minimum.

These meetings share several characteristics, drawn from the top-20 high-gap table's inverse and from the regime statistics. Calm-regime meetings have the lowest average combined gap (15.3), but the within-regime variance is substantial: calm meetings include both extremely low-gap meetings and several entries near the top-20 high-gap list (1995-12-19, ranked 7th, combined gap 37). What distinguishes low-gap calm meetings from high-gap calm meetings is content, not regime designation.

Low-gap meetings tend to share one or more of three features. First, meetings where participants' concerns are relatively homogeneous in direction and intensity — genuine consensus in the room — produce lower gaps because the minutes' consensus-manufacturing function requires less transformation. When participants actually agree on both the action and its intensity, anonymization and quantifier downgrade produce minimal distortion. Second, meetings where external events so thoroughly constrain the narrative that the minutes cannot credibly diverge from transcript content. The crisis-era FSE near-convergence (September and October 2008, FSE gap ~2 points) illustrates this: when Lehman Brothers has visibly collapsed, the minutes cannot characterize financial conditions as mild. External documentation imposes a floor on compression. Third, meetings that are procedurally routine — intermeeting calls, brief sessions without extended economic go-rounds — provide less material to compress, and the gap between transcript and minutes shrinks by default.

What the low-gap meetings do not include is any meeting where the combined gap reaches zero or near-zero across all three dimensions simultaneously. The regime statistics confirm this: even calm-period meetings have a combined gap mean of 15.3, and the minimum combined gap in any meeting in the full sample is substantially above zero. Compression does not approach zero even in the most routine, consensual, and externally constrained meetings. The always-on layer (Tier 1 mechanisms) ensures a non-zero floor regardless of content.

## 8. Mechanism Co-occurrence Patterns

Several co-occurrence questions are answerable with full-sample data.

**When ANONYMIZATION is absent (3.2% of meetings, 7 meetings):** The 7 meetings without ANONYMIZATION are the rarest set in the dataset. Their rarity itself is informative — only 7 of 216 meetings produced minutes in which all participant-discussion content was either absent (making anonymization moot) or already expressed in collective terms by participants themselves. In these meetings, SPECIFICITY_STRIPPING and MODIFIER_SOFTENING are near-certain to be present (since they appear in 97.7% and 86.6% of all meetings respectively), meaning that the absence of anonymization does not imply low compression overall. The most likely explanation for anonymization absence is structural: these are meetings where the participant-discussion section covers primarily technical or procedural matters, where individual voices are not the organizing unit of the discussion.

**When EPISTEMIC_LAUNDERING appears (19.9%, 43 meetings):** The 43 meetings with EPISTEMIC_LAUNDERING show elevated rates across the full Tier 2 mechanism set. CONFIDENCE_PROJECTION — the opposite-direction mechanism that inserts symmetrical risk framing — has an 11-meeting overall count (5.1%), but within the 43 EPISTEMIC_LAUNDERING meetings, co-occurrence with CONFIDENCE_PROJECTION is concentrated in the Bernanke and Yellen eras. The two mechanisms address different manifestations of the same underlying problem: participants' expressed uncertainty is either neutralized (laundered to institutional competence language) or actively reversed (projected as balanced-risk confidence). The choice between laundering and projecting depends on whether the minutes have a specific forward-guidance claim requiring credibility maintenance (projecting) or are simply managing tone (laundering).

**When SESSION_ERASURE appears (4.2%, 9 meetings):** The 9 SESSION_ERASURE meetings are the most likely to generate large FSE gaps, because Day 1 special sessions frequently focus on financial stability, monetary policy strategy, or supervisory issues — exactly the content that the standard minutes format cannot accommodate. The regime distribution of session erasure — 14% in pre-crisis (3/22), 4% in recovery (4/103), 3% in calm (2/67), 0% in crisis (0/24) — shows that special sessions are most frequent in pre-crisis periods, when the FOMC is devoting meeting time to understanding emerging stress. The absence of session erasure in crisis meetings reflects a simpler fact: during acute crisis, the committee holds emergency meetings focused tightly on the policy decision, without extended special-topic sessions.

## 9. The "No Zero" Finding

Table 9 of the frequency tables records the following: 0 of 216 meetings (0.0%) show all three gaps simultaneously at or below 2 points. The table is empty.

This finding deserves emphasis because it is the most direct statement of what editorial compression is. If compression were activated by unusual content — particularly contested decisions, particularly alarming financial conditions, particularly expressed uncertainty — then routine calm-period meetings with broad consensus and stable financial conditions should approach zero compression. They do not. If compression were driven by the personal editorial choices of specific chairs, then the chair with the most transparent communication style should approach zero compression in their most routine meetings. The data show that even Powell's 24-meeting sample, with the lowest combined gap mean (16.3) of any chair, produces no zero-compression meetings.

What the no-zero finding establishes is that the floor on FOMC minutes compression is structural, not editorial. Even in the most consensual, routine, calm-period meeting imaginable, the genre requirements of the minutes — collective attribution, qualitative characterization, calibrated institutional register — impose a non-trivial transformation on transcript content. The three Tier 1 mechanisms are always active because they reflect always-present genre constraints. The gap they produce does not go to zero because there is no version of FOMC minutes that would satisfy the document's institutional function while simultaneously preserving named attribution, numerical specificity, and unmodified evaluative language.

The significance for FOMC communication research is not merely that compression exists, but that it is irreducible within the current document format. The question of how much compression occurs in a given meeting is a question about Tier 2 and Tier 3 mechanisms — the conditionally activated mechanisms that respond to meeting content. But the question of whether compression exists in any given meeting has a fixed answer across all 216 observations: yes.

Across 27 years, four chairs, five major economic episodes, and a combined gap mean that ranges from 15.3 (calm) to 24.4 (crisis), the floor never reaches zero. This is the full-sample dataset's most consequential single finding.

## 10. Implications for Research and Policy

**For researchers using FOMC minutes as data**

The full-sample statistics establish the magnitude of systematic bias that researchers working with minutes-based scores should expect. On DISSENT_INTENSITY, the gap between transcript and minutes averages approximately 7.1 points across all chairs, ranging from 5.7 (Yellen) to 8.6 (Bernanke). On FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS, the gap averages approximately 6.2 points, ranging from 5.2 (Powell) to 6.5 (Bernanke). On UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED, the gap averages approximately 4.7 points, ranging from 4.1 (Greenspan) to 5.5 (Bernanke and Yellen jointly). These are not noise — they are systematic directional biases that persist across all eras, all chairs, all regimes.

The regime dependence of these biases is economically significant. A researcher who uses minutes-based dissent scores to analyze how disagreement responds to economic conditions will find the crisis-period gap mean of 9.2 (dissent) to be understated; the calm-period gap mean of 6.3 will be less so but still biased. A difference-in-differences design comparing crisis to calm dissent using minutes data will understate the true difference. Quantitatively, the crisis dissent gap mean is 9.2 versus calm's 6.3 — a ratio of 1.46. The minutes-based estimate of this ratio will be compressed toward 1.0 by the differential compression bias.

For financial stability research specifically, the pre-crisis regime produces the second-highest FSE gap mean (8.1, after crisis's 8.5) — and both are nearly double the calm mean of 5.1. Minutes-based FSE scores in the pre-crisis periods will systematically understate the committee's internal alarm level at precisely the moments when that alarm level is most predictive of subsequent events.

**The systematic vs. episodic nature of compression**

A finding with direct methodological implications: compression is not episodic. It cannot be corrected for by excluding "unusual" meetings from a sample, because there are no meetings that escape the always-on layer. Researchers should treat all FOMC minutes scores in the three affected dimensions (dissent intensity, financial stability emphasis, expressed uncertainty) as systematically censored from above relative to true transcript-based values, with the degree of censoring varying predictably by regime and chair in the directions documented here.

**What is reliably transmitted**

The minutes reliably transmit four categories of information. First, the formal policy decision and its direction: rate changes, balance-sheet actions, and forward guidance language. Second, the broad consensus characterization of economic conditions: whether the economy is expanding or contracting, whether inflation is above or below target, whether labor markets are tightening or loosening. Third, the existence of topic categories: if financial stability was discussed, a mention of financial stability will appear; if uncertainty was expressed, a mention of uncertainty will appear. The category is preserved even when the content is stripped. Fourth, the direction of the committee's policy lean: whether the next action is more likely to be a tightening, an easing, or no change.

The minutes unreliably transmit three categories, in ways that are systematic rather than random. First, the intensity and breadth of dissent: both QUASI_DISSENT_OMISSION (39.8%) and QUANTIFIER_DOWNGRADE (36.6%) directly compress the apparent size and strength of minority positions. Second, the specificity and analytical grounding of financial stability concerns: SPECIFICITY_STRIPPING (97.7%) and MECHANISM_STRIPPING (26.4%) remove the numerical evidence and causal chains that distinguish actionable early warnings from generic risk acknowledgments. Third, the character of expressed uncertainty: EPISTEMIC_LAUNDERING (19.9%) and CONFIDENCE_PROJECTION (5.1%) systematically transform deep uncertainty — including meta-uncertainty about model reliability — into institutional competence language.

The information asymmetry between what is reliably transmitted and what is not has a coherent structure. What survives is what the market communication function requires: a clear signal about the policy decision and its direction. What is compressed is what that function cannot accommodate: the intensity of internal disagreement, the specific evidence base for financial stability concerns, and the genuine limits of the committee's analytical confidence.

**The foreshadowing premium**

The 35.2% overall foreshadowing rate, rising to 68% in the pre-crisis regime, identifies a specific and recurring information loss. The pre-crisis meetings are the meetings where transcript content has the highest ex-post predictive value — these are the discussions where early-warning signals for the dot-com crash, the Great Financial Crisis, and COVID were being articulated by participants with specific data and named mechanisms. The gap between transcript foreshadowing content and published minutes content is not a random editorial choice; it reflects the genre constraint that the minutes speak to current institutional function while stripping the forward-looking specificity that makes warnings actionable.

The practical consequence is a systematic lag in public information: early warnings that were present in the FOMC's deliberations reach the public only through the five-year transcript release, by which time the events being warned about have either occurred or been foreclosed. The contemporaneous minutes — the document available to markets, policymakers, and researchers in real time — understated the committee's internal alarm in 68% of pre-crisis meetings. That figure, derived from the full 216-meeting sample, is the quantitative anchor for evaluating proposals to reform the information content of real-time FOMC communication.
