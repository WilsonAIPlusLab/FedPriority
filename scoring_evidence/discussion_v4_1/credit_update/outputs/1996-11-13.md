# Blind check, 1996-11-13: CREDIT_SPREADS and EQUITY_VALUATION

The whole file was read to the last line. The vote read-out shows 12 Yes. This is a normal meeting with a policy vote and a stance discussion.

## EQUITY_VALUATION

Participants state valuation and concern views, so the score is read on the table (higher = more stress or concern, with overvaluation concern in the low range).

- Rivlin: "the clearly overvalued stock market might head down, perhaps sharply."
- Lindsey, who gave the longest treatment: "the biggest risk relates to the current stock market valuation based on 13 percent growth in profits." He also said "the fact that stock prices have risen as fast as profits implies an expectation of another five years of 13 percent growth in profits. But that would be inconsistent with the numbers in the Greenbook." He added that people are "putting valuations on financial assets ... that pose a problem for the future." On policy he said it "is going to be our toughest decision in the years ahead" whether to "make it stop by injecting a little risk into the process."
- Minehan: "We are risking a major asset price bubble unless some sense of caution ... is injected into these financial markets."
- Hoenig: "I am concerned about signs of a potential financial bubble."
- Yellen: "I also share Governor Lindsey's and President Minehan's concern about euphoria in asset markets." She also said "a significant stock market correction, which cannot be ruled out, adds downside risk."
- Melzer: "evidence of some speculative excesses in financial markets"; "By moving now we might be able to avoid a bigger accident in those markets later."
- Greenspan: "I gave him the worry about the stock market."

Six participants (Rivlin, Lindsey, Minehan, Hoenig, Yellen, Melzer) hold an overvaluation, bubble or froth view. Several of them link it to the policy decision. The Guynn, Broaddus, Phillips and Yellen remarks on stock-market wealth are wealth-effect mentions only and are not used. The words "bubble", "euphoria" and "clearly overvalued" sit in the table's 1-15 and 16-30 bands. Nobody disputes the view. The concern is serious but described as a risk, not a present crash, so I place it at the stretched-to-bubble boundary.

Score: 18, uncertainty +-6, confidence High.

## CREDIT_SPREADS

No participant says anything about credit spreads, risk premiums or corporate or other yield spreads. Remarks on Treasury yields are about inflation expectations (Broaddus, Stern, Melzer), not spreads, so they do not count. Under clarification section 3 the dimension can only be scored from lending standards, terms, availability or issuance language. I use that route and say so: **this score rests on availability and lending language, not spread wording.**

Evidence that points to tighter credit (above 50):
- Yellen: "consumer spending stalled, the growth of consumer credit plummeted, and lending standards tightened ... it is not very surprising that lenders would tighten standards."
- Jordan, on bankers in his District: "they had tightened up enough"; "charge-offs would be much greater in 1997"; "very concerned about the credit quality of their loans on the consumer side ... increasingly ... on the commercial side, too."
- Guynn: "the regional decline in loan quality suggests the possibility of tighter loan standards and weaker loan demand"; "Banks in our region ... are beginning to pay closer attention to credit quality."
- Lindsey: a concern that "we as regulators and the banks will make things worse."

Evidence that points to easy credit (below 50):
- Phillips: "Financing is generally available"; "the cost of external capital is low as a result of low interest rates and the rise in stock prices. Banks are continuing to provide financing"; "businesses are issuing new debt and equity and utilizing increased bank financing."
- Minehan and Lindsey: markets behave as if "interest rates only go in one direction", and perceived recession risk is very low. This is risk-appetite language, not credit availability.

The tightening is confined to consumer credit, while business financing is described as ample, so the two sets of evidence offset. Four participants support the tightening side and one supports the ample side. The mixed picture sits near the middle, with a small lean to the easy side because Phillips's cost-of-capital wording is the closest thing in the file to a pricing statement. Under the clarification this is Low confidence with uncertainty of at least +-10.

Score: 48, uncertainty +-12, confidence Low.
