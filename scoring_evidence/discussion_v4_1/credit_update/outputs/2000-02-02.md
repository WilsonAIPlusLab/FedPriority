# Blind check, FOMC 2000-02-02 (CREDIT_SPREADS, EQUITY_VALUATION)

Read the whole input to the last line, including the vote read-out. Only participants' words were used.

## CREDIT_SPREADS

No participant says anything about credit spreads, risk premiums on corporate debt, or a level of spreads. The only credit-side material is about availability and pricing of bank lending, so I applied the clarification for availability or lending language without spread wording (Low confidence, UNC at least +-10, evidence rests on availability).

- Stern: "bankers report that they are continuing to compete aggressively for loans but--and the 'but' is that they are tightening credit standards. And they report that they are encountering examiners who are encouraging them to tighten credit standards." This is the main evidence. It points to somewhat tighter availability, partly offset by "compete aggressively for loans".
- Jordan: bankers are "putting through a round of loan repricings during this quarter" because the "cost of funds [is] rising" and margins are "under pressure". He also says "corporate bond yields and mortgage rates at 8 percent" are not "at the wrong levels". The bond-yield remark is a yield level, not a spread, and he calls it consistent with the outlook, so I give it no weight.
- Minehan: corporate debt is "growing rapidly and hitting rather high levels--at least relative to GDP" but "certainly still affordable". She also mentions a "buildup of imbalances in terms of credit". This is leverage language, not spread or availability language.

Greenspan's "equity premium" and "risk premiums" comments concern equities and the price-stability argument, so they are not credit spread evidence. Gramlich's TIPS and nominal yield discussion concerns Treasury inflation compensation, not credit spreads. Net reading: modest tightening of bank credit standards and loan pricing (one clear participant, Stern, with Jordan's repricing as weak support) against a backdrop of competitive lending. This is slightly above normal, so I score 55, UNC +-10, Low confidence.

## EQUITY_VALUATION

Participants state explicit valuation and concern views.

- Greenspan: "our latest evaluation of the equity premium in the stock market is that it is downright scary! I haven't seen anything like this before." He adds "the market is at extremely high valuation levels", says the discount rate in the stock market "now is virtually zero as best I can judge", compares the situation to 1987 ("We are in a far worse situation now than we were then"), and fears that being too aggressive "we could crack the market and end up with a very severe problem of instability."
- Ferguson: "a placid surface may disguise some underlying turmoil... this market is subject to some volatility, including significant downside corrections". He repeats later that "there is likely to be some underlying turbulence" in asset markets.
- Broaddus: "the big risk is that we might get a market break that could lead to what I would refer to as a disorderly plunge in the stock market."
- Meyer: the equity price rise is partly a partial-equilibrium result of "a low equity premium", and "we are now going to see how equity prices evolve in a general equilibrium world", which implies a stretched valuation.
- Minehan: "growing liabilities and debt service burdens that are reasonable only when one factors in growing market values of assets"; the economy "increasingly buys into the idea that things go only in one direction"; and the stock market "might even level off nicely in an atmosphere of a little less euphoria". Later she refers to "the buildup of imbalances in terms of credit, all related to rising asset values".
- Moskow and Kelley mention "high levels of the stock market" and "record highs", mostly as a spending channel, so they add little on valuation.

Rule applied: clarification section 2, stated valuation or concern view, scored on the table. Greenspan's "downright scary" and "extremely high valuation levels" land in the extreme overvaluation concern band, tempered slightly because he also says he is "leaving aside the issue of whether or not the stock market is appropriately priced" in an earlier passage. At least five participants (Greenspan, Ferguson, Broaddus, Meyer, Minehan) support the reading that equities are richly valued and vulnerable. Score 14, UNC +-5, High confidence.
