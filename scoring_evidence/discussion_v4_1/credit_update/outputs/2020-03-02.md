## EQUITY_VALUATION

Two participants say something about equity or asset prices, and one of them gives a valuation view.

- Bullard: "this policy move will be perceived and understood as a reaction to last week's selloff in U.S. and global equity markets ... equity pricing is notoriously volatile, and some corrections are warranted, as pricing sometimes moves well away from what seems to be consistent with market fundamentals. Many will, no doubt, argue that the current selloff is consistent with a move toward more rational pricing of the value of the U.S. corporate sector."
- Brainard: "The sharp declines in asset valuations and long-term yields reflect a notable increase in tail risk."
- Powell mentions only "a tightening of financial conditions", with no equity view.

Rule applied: clarifications section 2. A stated valuation or concern view is scored on the table. Bullard does state one. He treats the selloff as a correction toward fundamentals and not as undervaluation or panic, so his view sits at the "roughly fair valued, no particular concern" end or slightly on the "modestly above fair value" side. His "many will argue" phrasing is partly attributed to others, so the view is somewhat hedged. Brainard reads the fall in valuations as a sign of stress and tail risk, which pulls the other way but is a price-and-risk reading and not a claim that stocks are undervalued. Net: close to neutral, nudged slightly below 50 by Bullard's "corrections are warranted / more rational pricing" view and pulled back toward 50 by Brainard's stress reading. Score 47. Uncertainty is +-10 because only two speakers touch the topic, one is hedged, and the two lean in opposite directions. Confidence is Low.

Polarity check: higher means more equity stress or undervaluation concern. The evidence says the selloff is a partly warranted correction, and that does not imply more stress. A score slightly below 50 fits.

## CREDIT_SPREADS

The only spread wording is Kashkari's: "I can imagine a scenario in which the Treasury yield curve is basically at zero, and the federal funds rate is basically at zero, and credit spreads are wide. What do we do in that type of situation?" This is a hypothetical downside scenario, and he does not say spreads are wide now. Other relevant words are Brainard's "ease financial stress among indebted businesses and cash flow constrained households" and Powell's "counter a tightening of financial conditions". Neither is a statement about credit spreads, lending standards, terms, availability or issuance. Brainard's "long-term yields" refers to the general rate level and not to a credit premium. No participant gives a current spread level, a spread direction, or a statement about credit availability.

Rule applied: clarifications section 3. A stated level governs the side of 50, but this level is a scenario assumption about a possible future. No spread direction is reported, and no availability or issuance language is present. Scoring from the single hypothetical would put a number on a future scenario and not on what participants see now. One participant, one clause, no present-tense reading. I score N/A. If a reader treats Kashkari's clause as a stated level, the most the text could support is about 55 to 58 with Low confidence and +-12 or more. I judge that this reading is not supported.
