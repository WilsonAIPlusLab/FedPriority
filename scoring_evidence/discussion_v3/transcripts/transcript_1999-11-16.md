# FOMC Scoring: 1999-11-16

## Part 1: Thinking Process

**Document type**: Transcript. Individual speakers identified by name in a go-around format, followed by a policy discussion. This is a segmented transcript with clear participant attributions.

**Era context**: Late 1999 -- the peak of the late-1990s technology/productivity boom. The economy had been growing rapidly, labor markets were historically tight, productivity was accelerating, and the Fed had already raised rates in June and August 1999. Y2K concerns loomed over near-term policy timing. The Asian crisis had receded and global growth was recovering, removing a disinflationary cushion.

**Speaker count and views**:
- Go-around participants: Jordan, Minehan, McTeer, Parry, Moskow, Broaddus, Hoenig, Guynn, Stern, McDonough, Poole, Gramlich, Ferguson, Meyer, Kelley, Greenspan (Chairman)
- Policy round: Nearly all support 25bp hike. Parry prefers hold with asymmetric bias. Stern accepts but with reservations. McTeer supports but unsure if further tightening needed. Broaddus would prefer retaining asymmetry.

### Dimension-by-dimension reasoning:

**INFLATION_PRESSURE**: Participants see inflation as modestly below worrisome levels currently but with clear upside risks. Core inflation is still low (1-1.5% PCE, ~2% CPI), but nearly every participant sees risks tilted to the upside. Jordan: "the suggestion that inflation over the next year or two might remain the same...is simply not credible." Minehan: "all of the risks to the forecast seem to me even more firmly planted on the upside." Moskow: "we are even more pessimistic than the Greenbook about inflation." McTeer is more sanguine: core deflators "stable in the 1 to 1.5 percent range." Meyer: inflation "definitively continues to point toward an increase in core inflation." The center of gravity is that current inflation is contained but pressures are building -- pipeline prices (PPI), health costs, energy, import prices reversing, labor costs potentially accelerating. This is not yet elevated inflation, but it is above the "no concern" level. Direction: modestly above the "at target" midpoint due to building pressures, but actual readings still low. Score: ~53.

**OUTPUT_GAP**: The economy is clearly running above potential. Jordan: "supply-constrained economy." Minehan: GDP "growing without undue strain" but "resource pressures." Gramlich: "even more than the upgraded estimate of potential GDP growth...a little too much demand pressure." Ferguson: "the economy is still at this stage exceeding that limit." Meyer: "the degree of imbalance today between supply and demand." Greenspan: "growth of demand exceeds the growth of supply." The bright-line rule requires >50 for "above potential" language, which is ubiquitous. This is the late-90s boom; anchor suggests 57-63. Score: ~60.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS**: Extremely tight. Jordan: "supply-constrained economy...more warm bodies." Minehan: New England unemployment at 2.9%, "series low since 1969." Moskow: "labor markets are still very, very tight." Broaddus: pressures "more common and intense...10 to 15 percent increases for skilled workers." Hoenig: "Labor markets are tight everywhere." Ferguson: unemployment "clearly ticked down again...supply of available workers is indeed nearly exhausted." Greenspan: pool of unemployed continuing to decline. Anchor for 1999-2000 is 65-75. Score: ~70.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT**: Strong but with early signs of moderation. Consumer spending decelerating somewhat; autos and housing softening. But overall still very robust. McDonough: "forecast growth at a 4 percent rate in the fourth quarter." Broaddus: "overall spending growth is currently still very strong." Moskow: auto sales at 16.5M still "very high." Kelley: "momentum remains impressive." Some softening in housing and autos but business investment remains strong (capital deepening, equipment/software). Net: above trend. Score: ~60.

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS**: Conditions have tightened somewhat through rising long-term rates. Greenspan: "Rates on U.S. Treasuries have risen somewhat less than a percentage point...the spread on BBB-rated obligations versus Treasuries has widened." Housing affected by higher mortgage rates. McDonough: "rise of long-term interest rates...has caused housing construction and sales to decline." Guynn: "mortgage loan originations are off as much as 50 percent." But stock market recovered, consumer confidence high, credit still available. McDonough also notes banks "tightening in credit standards." Overall: somewhat tighter than neutral but not restrictive. Score: ~55.

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN**: Clear consensus for 25bp hike. Nearly all participants see upside risks and support tightening. Hoenig: "a good case for acting now." Gramlich: "all indicators do point in one direction." Meyer, Broaddus, Moskow, Minehan, Guynn all favor moving. Greenspan: "benefits of moving outweigh those of standing pat." Only Parry dissents (prefers hold). Stern accepts "although there is some chance that this action may be unnecessary." The move is symmetric (not hawkish bias going forward) because of Y2K constraints, not because they feel balanced. Many would prefer asymmetry but accept symmetry for practical reasons. This is a moderate hawkish meeting -- consensus for tightening with many wanting more but constrained by Y2K. Score: ~65.

**DISSENT_INTENSITY**: Broad agreement on 25bp hike. Parry preferred no change but didn't formally dissent from the vote (he was a voter and his preference was noted but he could accept). Stern lukewarm. McTeer uncertain about further hikes. Some disagreement on the statement language (third paragraph debate). But the vote was unanimous on the rate action. Modest differences on forward outlook. Score: ~28.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS**: Relatively limited direct financial stability discussion. Jordan mentions bankers doing deals others walk away from ("troubling"). Stock market mentioned as context (wealth effect) but not as a stability concern per se. Greenspan discusses wealth effect and stock market extensively but in an analytical framework, not as a stability alarm. McDonough mentions banks tightening standards and credit quality. Guynn probed for credit quality problems and Y2K liquidity issues. Some attention but not a prominent topic. Score: ~25.

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE**: The directive is symmetric, and Y2K constrains December action. Greenspan: "we effectively are saying that we are not going to move in December." However, many participants signal further tightening will likely be needed. Meyer: worried symmetric doesn't convey that "rates would have to rise more." Broaddus: wanted asymmetry. Moskow: "I do feel asymmetric." The forward signal is mildly hawkish -- they expect to need to tighten more, but are pausing for Y2K. Score: ~62.

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED**: Moderate uncertainty. Greenspan: "We really do not know how this system works. It's clearly new. The old models just are not working." Stern: "we are a bit at sea here." Ferguson: "a great deal of uncertainty about where policy is going to go next year." The productivity question creates genuine uncertainty -- how long it will last, what the true NAIRU is, what the speed limit is. Gramlich cites wide NAIRU confidence intervals. But participants are fairly confident about the direction of risks (upside). Score: ~48.

### Cross-dimension coherence check:
- OUTPUT_GAP (60) and LABOR_TIGHTNESS (70): Both above 50, consistent with a booming economy. Labor tightness higher because the labor market is the binding constraint; output gap is moderated by strong productivity.
- HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN (65) and FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE (62): Aligned -- moderate hawkish action with a mildly hawkish forward lean, both tempered by the symmetric directive and Y2K pause.

### Polarity checks:
- INFLATION_PRESSURE: Higher = more pressure. Evidence says building but not yet realized. 53 = modestly above neutral. Matches.
- OUTPUT_GAP: Higher = more above potential. Evidence says above potential. 60 > 50. Matches.
- LABOR_TIGHTNESS: Higher = tighter. Evidence says very tight. 70. Matches.
- FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS: Higher = tighter. Evidence says somewhat tighter conditions. 55. Matches.

## Part 2: Evidence and Scores

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE
**Score: 53 +/-8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- MR. JORDAN: "The suggestion that inflation over the next year or two might remain the same as it has been over the last year or two is simply not credible. The expectation that inflation is moving up just does not have any dissenters."
- MR. MCTEER: "Our broadest measures of inflation, the deflators for personal consumption expenditures and gross domestic purchases, have been stable in the 1 to 1.5 percent range all of this year."
- MR. MEYER: "the balance of risks...definitively continues to point toward an increase in core inflation over the forecast horizon."

**Center-of-gravity**: Current core inflation readings remain contained (~1-2% PCE), but virtually all participants see upside risks from tight labor markets, rising pipeline prices (PPI), health costs, energy, and fading disinflationary forces. The score sits just above the midpoint because pressures are building but have not yet materialized in core measures.

**Minority views**: McTeer and Stern are more sanguine about inflation, emphasizing productivity gains as a buffer. This widens the band modestly.

---

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP
**Score: 60 +/-6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- MR. GRAMLICH: "Starting from a position where labor markets are already very tight, this demand growth is even more than the upgraded estimate of potential GDP growth. It represents a little too much demand pressure even for our new economy."
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "the growth of demand exceeds the growth of supply. That gap between the two has not been closing."
- MR. FERGUSON: "even if the speed limit is somewhat higher, the economy is still at this stage exceeding that limit."

**Center-of-gravity**: Nearly unanimous view that the economy is operating above potential. GDP growth of ~4.8% in Q3 exceeds even the revised-up potential growth estimate. Demand-supply imbalance is persistent and possibly widening.

**Minority views**: Stern notes uncertainty about the true speed limit. McTeer emphasizes productivity could accommodate more growth. Narrow minority.

---

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 70 +/-5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- MS. MINEHAN: "New England's unemployment rate fell slightly in September to 2.9 percent, equaling the series low since data began to be collected on a regional basis in 1969."
- MR. JORDAN: "it still basically looks and feels like a supply-constrained economy. Business people complain that they could be doing more if only there were more warm bodies."
- MR. BROADDUS: "stories about pressures in labor markets seem to have become more common and intense...one person talked about 10 to 15 percent increases for skilled workers."

**Center-of-gravity**: Universally reported as very tight. National unemployment at 4.1%, regional rates even lower. Every district reports difficulty finding workers. Wage pressures intensifying. Health care costs rising sharply. The pool of available workers being exhausted.

**Minority views**: None -- all participants agree labor markets are very tight. Narrow band.

---

### 4. CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT
**Score: 60 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- MR. BROADDUS: "Overall spending growth is currently still very strong despite the recent leveling off in home sales."
- MR. MOSKOW: "16.5 million light vehicles sold in October still represents a very high sales level."
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "interest-sensitive areas of the economy are beginning to slow, at least at the margin."

**Center-of-gravity**: Consumer spending and business investment remain above trend despite emerging softness in housing and autos. GDP grew 4.8% in Q3. Capital spending on equipment and software extremely strong. Some deceleration from peak levels but still well above normal.

**Minority views**: Some participants emphasize deceleration more (McDonough, Greenspan note housing/auto softening), while others see continued strength (Broaddus, Kelley). Moderate spread.

---

### 5. FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 55 +/-7 [Moderate confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "Rates on U.S. Treasuries have risen somewhat less than a percentage point since before the Asian crisis, but the spread on BBB-rated obligations versus Treasuries has widened."
- VICE CHAIRMAN MCDONOUGH: "the rise of long-term interest rates...has caused housing construction and sales to decline...mortgage refinancing activity to a virtual halt."
- MR. GUYNN: "mortgage loan originations are off as much as 50 percent from six months ago."

**Center-of-gravity**: Financial conditions have tightened from earlier levels via higher long-term rates, wider corporate spreads, and reduced mortgage activity. But stock market remains supportive, consumer confidence is high, and credit is still available. Somewhat tighter than neutral.

**Minority views**: Some participants see conditions still as broadly supportive (stock market resilient, consumer confidence high); others emphasize the tightening from rate increases.

---

### 6. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN
**Score: 65 +/-6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "My bottom line for today, frankly, is that the benefits of moving outweigh those of standing pat...I would like to put on the table a 25 basis point increase today and a symmetric directive."
- MR. HOENIG: "I still think there is a good case for acting now."
- MR. MOSKOW: "I think the risks of not moving are much higher. I do feel asymmetric."

**Center-of-gravity**: Near-unanimous support for 25bp hike. Many participants would prefer asymmetric (hawkish) bias but accept symmetry due to Y2K timing constraints. This is the third hike in the cycle. The tone is clearly hawkish -- tightening with most expecting more to come -- but moderated by the forced Y2K pause.

**Minority views**: Parry preferred no change. Stern lukewarm. McTeer unsure about further tightening.

---

### 7. DISSENT_INTENSITY
**Score: 28 +/-5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- MR. PARRY: "I would prefer to leave the funds rate unchanged at the present time and to retain a bias toward a tighter policy."
- MR. STERN: "Although I think there is some chance that this action may be unnecessary, I doubt it will be harmful."
- Significant debate over statement language (Meyer, Broaddus, Minehan, Moskow vs. McDonough, Ferguson, Boehne on third paragraph).

**Center-of-gravity**: Broad agreement on the 25bp action (only Parry dissenting in preference, not formally). Moderate disagreement on communication/statement language. Minor differences on forward outlook intensity. No formal dissent in the vote.

**Minority views**: Parry's preference for no change and Stern's lukewarm acceptance represent the main outliers.

---

### 8. FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS
**Score: 25 +/-7 [Moderate confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- MR. JORDAN: "some do admit that they know of deals that others are doing that they have walked away from...I find that very troubling."
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: Extended discussion of wealth effect and stock market as analytical mechanism, not as stability alarm per se.
- MR. GUYNN: Probed for "credit quality problems" and Y2K liquidity issues; found none significant.

**Center-of-gravity**: Financial stability is not a major focus. The stock market and wealth effect are discussed analytically (as a driver of demand imbalance), but not as a bubble concern or systemic risk. Credit quality mentioned briefly as good. Y2K liquidity discussed and dismissed. Limited attention overall.

---

### 9. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE
**Score: 62 +/-8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- MR. MEYER: "I don't particularly want to convey to the markets that we now feel that we've done as much as is necessary...What I really want to avoid is turning a tightening into what appears to be an easing."
- MR. MOSKOW: "I do feel asymmetric."
- MR. BROADDUS: "I think an economic case could be made for retaining asymmetry."

**Center-of-gravity**: The directive is symmetric, but the underlying sentiment is mildly hawkish going forward. The symmetry is largely tactical (Y2K constraints, not wanting to roil markets near year-end), not reflective of a genuinely balanced outlook. Most participants expect further tightening will be needed. The forward signal is a mild tightening bias masked by a symmetric directive.

**Minority views**: McTeer "not sure that move won't be the last in a series of tightening actions." Stern uncertain about models and direction. Ferguson genuinely prefers symmetry to avoid "building in momentum."

---

### 10. UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED
**Score: 48 +/-8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "We really do not know how this system works. It's clearly new. The old models just are not working."
- MR. STERN: "I think we are a bit at sea here...many of the conventional models...have simply been wrong on the critical issue of the acceleration of inflation."
- MR. PARRY: "a forecast that so far has been on the high side compared with the more favorable actual results."

**Center-of-gravity**: Elevated uncertainty driven by the productivity puzzle -- participants genuinely do not know whether the "new economy" dynamics change the inflation calculus. Old models have been wrong. But the direction of risk is fairly clear to most (upside). The uncertainty is about magnitude and timing, not direction.

**Minority views**: Some like Gramlich and Meyer are more confident in standard models. Others like Stern and Greenspan express deeper model uncertainty.
