# FOMC Transcript Scoring — 2018-03-21

## Part 1: Thinking / Reasoning

### 1. Document Type
This is a **transcript** from the March 20–21, 2018 FOMC meeting. It contains individual participant statements from both the economic go-around (Day 1) and the policy discussion (Day 2). Speakers are identified by name (e.g., "MR. HARKER.", "MS. BRAINARD.", "CHAIRMAN POWELL."). The transcript is segmented with a clear "=== Policy Discussion ===" marker. Staff attribution is distinguished from participant remarks; I score participant views only.

Participants represented include: Harker, Bullard, Rosengren, Brainard, Barkin, Quarles, Mester, Evans, Kaplan, George, Bostic, Williams, Kashkari, Dudley (Vice Chair), and Powell (Chair). That is approximately 15 participant-level speakers — a full complement for a regular meeting. This is Chairman Powell's first meeting as Chair (succeeding Yellen).

### 2. Era Context
March 2018: The U.S. expansion was mature and well above-trend. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act had passed in December 2017, and the Bipartisan Budget Act (BBA) adding $300 billion in government spending was freshly enacted in February 2018. The hiking cycle was well underway; the Committee was expected to raise the federal funds rate 25bp at this meeting (liftoff was December 2015, and several hikes had already occurred). Core PCE was running around 1.5% year-over-year (below target) but short-term measures like 6-month annualized were near 2%. Unemployment was around 4.1%. The broad backdrop is: economy near/above full employment, inflation still slightly below target but firming, policy in gradual tightening mode, and fiscal stimulus adding to demand.

### 3. Core Dimensions — Directional Assessment

**INFLATION_PRESSURE**
Participants broadly see inflation as **below target but firming and approaching 2%**. Nearly every speaker notes core PCE at 1.5% year-over-year but improving short-term measures (6-month annualized at 2%). Barkin: "inflation is effectively at our target." Mester: "modal projection is that inflation will gradually rise over the forecast horizon, reaching our 2 percent goal on a sustained basis sometime in the first half of next year." Kashkari: "core PCE is at 1.5 percent...we should not declare victory prematurely." Williams: "prices have been rising as expected...core inflation will gradually move toward the 2 percent target." Bullard is concerned about under-shooting, citing trimmed mean at 1.7%. Evans prefers to hold rates to confirm inflation is actually rising. Brainard worried about underlying inflation below target for years.

Direction: Slightly below target — leaning toward the 41–49 range. Multiple speakers see near-target short-term readings, but the year-over-year measure is still below, and several participants (Bullard, Evans, Kashkari, Brainard) explicitly flag downside risk. Center of gravity: ~44. Most see firming but not yet at target. Not deflationary but also not clearly above target.

**OUTPUT_GAP**
Participants broadly see the economy as **operating above potential** or at the very least at/near potential. Key language: Williams: "All signs point to an economy operating above full employment...real GDP growing much more rapidly than potential GDP." Rosengren: "my forecast is for real GDP to exceed potential by almost 1 percentage point this year." Barkin: economy is "running hot" (implied by policy discussion). Brainard: "close to full employment and growing above its trend rate." Bostic: "near-complete closing of resource slack." Powell (Chair summary): "the strong job market seems to have become stronger still...the economy is very likely in the vicinity of maximum sustainable employment."

BRIGHT-LINE RULE: Multiple speakers explicitly say "above potential" or "above trend" → score MUST be > 50. Center of gravity: approximately 57-62. Several place output ~1 percentage point above potential; others more moderate. This is clearly in the "modestly above potential" range, perhaps approaching "clearly above potential" given fiscal stimulus projections.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS**
Consensus view is that the labor market is **tight and getting tighter**. Unemployment at 4.1%, monthly payroll gains at 240,000 (above trend). Multiple speakers cite "labor shortages," "tight and getting tighter" (Barkin). Williams: "economy operating above full employment...employment rates have reached lows that would have been dismissed as unattainable just a few years ago." Kashkari is the notable outlier — argues the gauge is broken, there may be more slack than visible in the 4.1% headline. Key debate: wage growth puzzlingly modest despite tightness.

Direction: Tight. Unemployment forecasts as low as 3.1–3.5% over horizon. Anchors suggest 53–65 range for "tight" conditions with low unemployment. Given unemployment at 4.1% already and forecasts of 3.5–3.7%, center of gravity is ~60–63. Kashkari's skepticism provides some uncertainty band but is a clear minority.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT**
Mixed: Q1 2018 data showed softness ("pothole"), but underlying fundamentals and fiscal stimulus support strong forward outlook. Powell: "consumption appears to have slowed significantly from a very strong 4¼ percent annualized growth in the fourth quarter to an estimated 1½ percent in the current quarter." However: nearly all participants attribute Q1 weakness to residual seasonality, tax refund timing, and temporary factors. Strong business sentiment, manufacturing surveys at highs, cap-ex plans elevated by tax legislation. Global strength also supportive.

Direction: Near-trend to somewhat above trend, given the soft Q1 being viewed as temporary and forward expectations robust. Score: ~52–55. Not boom-like (the Q1 softness prevents a higher score), but clearly not recessionary or below trend in participants' assessment.

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS**
Participants describe financial conditions as **accommodative but with some recent modest tightening**. Brainard: "despite the recent choppiness in financial markets, financial conditions remain supportive of economic growth." Mester: "financial conditions remain accommodative." Evans: "both the overall [CFNAI] index and the volatility subindex remain well below their long-run historical averages." Brainard: equity prices down 4% since last meeting but up 25% since end of 2016; corporate bond spreads widened "somewhat" but "still remain quite compressed." Powell: "financial conditions remain accommodative despite a modest net tightening since the Committee's January meeting."

Direction: Accommodative. Score: ~35–40 (somewhat loose to neutral range). Conditions are described as accommodative, spreads compressed, equity still elevated. The slight recent tightening (VIX rise, equity pullback) prevents a score lower than 30.

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN**
The overwhelming majority support a 25bp hike at this meeting (alternative B). Williams: four hikes this year and four next year. Rosengren: considers more than 4 hikes if needed, concerned about overheating. Mester: rate path above 3% into 2020. George: four hikes in 2018. Dudley: four 25bp hikes this year. Quarles: three in 2018, three in 2019, three in 2020.

Notable doves: Bullard (one reluctant hiker, but concerned about going above 2%), Evans (preferred to hold today but accepted consensus for alt-B), Kashkari (preferred alt-A but accepted B for continuity). Harker: three hikes this year. Kaplan: three hikes in 2018.

Direction: Moderate to strong hawkish. Nearly all support the hike; most support continued gradual tightening; several suggest the path may need to be steeper. Anchors: 70–75 range is "consensus for continued tightening / further increases needed." This is clearly in that range. Center of gravity: ~70. The presence of 2–3 reluctant or less hawkish participants brings it down from 75.

**DISSENT_INTENSITY**
No formal voting dissents are recorded. The transcript shows unanimous support for alt-B among voters. Evans preferred to hold but accepted the consensus. Kashkari preferred alt-A but deferred. Bullard also expressed reservations about the forward path but supported alt-B for continuity.

Direction: Low-moderate disagreement. There are philosophical differences (Bullard vs. Williams on r*; Rosengren vs. Kashkari on labor market slack; Evans vs. majority on timing), but no formal dissents, and the policy vote was unanimous. Anchor: typical one-dissent meeting is 25–35; this has zero formal dissents but 2–3 "reluctant" supporters. Score: ~28–32.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS**
Several participants discuss financial stability dimensions. Brainard: asset valuations elevated but system risks moderate; warns against banks releasing capital buffers; mentions financial imbalances. Dudley: concerned about equity market outlook in 1–2 years, overheating risk. Kaplan: concerned about excesses and imbalances. Evans: contacts "sanguine" about equity market correction; CFNAI well below long-run average. Bullard: mentions equity valuations as downside risk.

Direction: Moderate discussion of financial stability — not a dominant topic but meaningfully present across multiple speakers. Score: ~35–42 (limited-to-moderate discussion; a few address meaningfully but it's not the meeting's focus).

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE**
The Committee is clearly in tightening mode and almost all participants signal further gradual increases. Multiple speakers note paragraph 4 of the statement (which says funds rate will remain "below levels expected to prevail in the longer run") is becoming stale and should be changed soon. Williams, Bullard, Barkin, Mester, Brainard, Dudley all discuss removing this language. Williams: "the expiration date is nearing for the forward-guidance language." SEP median has 4 hikes in 2018, overshoot of neutral in 2019–2020.

Direction: Clearly hawkish forward signal. A 25bp hike with signal of continued gradual tightening and likely 3–4 more hikes this year. Score: ~75–80. This is "further rate increases or balance sheet reduction ahead." The moderate hesitation on exactly how many hikes (3 vs. 4) and Bullard's caution about overshooting prevents a score above 80.

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED**
Multiple sources of uncertainty mentioned: r* (natural rate of interest) is highly uncertain and debated at length; natural rate of unemployment highly uncertain; fiscal multiplier uncertain (no prior experience with procyclical fiscal stimulus of this scale at full employment); trade policy trajectory uncertain; Q1 data weakness interpretation uncertain; NAFTA/Mexico political uncertainty (Kaplan). Rosengren: "relatively small changes in the underlying economic relationships or changes in key conditioning assumptions can cause forecasts to miss in significant ways." Powell: "considerable uncertainty about just how strong that impetus is." Brainard: "we don't have a lot of experience with a procyclical fiscal experiment of this nature."

Direction: Somewhat elevated uncertainty. The r* debate alone shows real uncertainty among experts. But the economic outlook is broadly positive and well-telegraphed; participants don't describe "uncharted territory" panic. Score: ~42–48. Above normal (25–35 is standard caveats) but not dramatically elevated. Key sources: r*, fiscal multiplier, trade, labor slack measurement.

### 5. Cross-Dimension Coherence

- OUTPUT_GAP (~58) and LABOR_TIGHTNESS (~62): Both above 50, consistent with an economy near/above full employment. Coherent. Labor slightly higher because the unemployment level discussion is more direct than the output gap language.
- HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN (~70) and FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE (~76): Both in hawkish territory. Dovish current action is not the case — they are hiking. Forward signal (76) is slightly more hawkish than current meeting lean (70) because participants also signal the neutral rate may need to be exceeded. Coherent and expected.
- INFLATION_PRESSURE (~44, below target) vs. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN (~70, hawkish): This divergence is consistent with the meeting's narrative: policy is hawkish not because inflation is high, but because of labor market tightness, output above potential, and fiscal stimulus risk. Participants are hiking preemptively/gradually to avoid falling behind the curve even as inflation is still below target.

### 6. Speaker Count for Center of Gravity

- On policy: ~12–13 support continued gradual hiking; Evans preferred hold but accepted; Kashkari preferred alt-A but accepted. Essentially unanimous support for alt-B.

## Part 2: Evidence and Scores

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE
**Score: 44 ±7 | Confidence: High**

- **Evidence**: Kashkari: "core PCE is at 1.5 percent, and we should not declare victory prematurely." Evans: "inflation expectations currently appear to be stubbornly below our 2 percent target." Mester: "reflecting the recent firming in the inflation readings...my modal projection is that inflation will gradually rise over the forecast horizon, reaching our 2 percent goal on a sustained basis sometime in the first half of next year." Barkin: "I believe for now it's effectively at our target. Monthly inflation has picked up over the past half-year."
- **Center of gravity**: Participants see inflation as slightly below the 2 percent target on a 12-month basis but firming, with the 6-month measure having reached 2%. Most expect gradual convergence to target over 2018–2019.
- **Minority views**: Barkin (already at target on short-term measures); Bullard (worried about downside risk if policy becomes too restrictive; Dallas trimmed mean at only 1.7%); Evans/Kashkari more cautious, seeing persistent below-target readings.

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP
**Score: 58 ±6 | Confidence: High**

- **Evidence**: Williams: "All signs point to an economy operating above full employment...real GDP grew much more rapidly than potential GDP last year, and I expect a repeat of this pattern this year." Rosengren: "my forecast is for real GDP to exceed potential by almost 1 percentage point this year." Brainard: "close to full employment and growing above its trend rate." Powell: "the economy is very likely in the vicinity of maximum sustainable employment, and that momentum in the job market...will further tighten labor market conditions."
- **Center of gravity**: Clear consensus that the economy is operating above potential, with fiscal stimulus expected to push it further above trend in 2018–2019.
- **Minority views**: Bullard argues neutral rate may be very low so the output gap may not require aggressive tightening. Kashkari expresses uncertainty about effective unemployment measurement. But neither disputes an above-trend assessment.

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 62 ±8 | Confidence: High**

- **Evidence**: Barkin: "Labor markets are tight and getting tighter as you can see in the recent jobs report and comments given by our contacts." Williams: "employment rates, both in aggregate and in the case of various demographic groups, have reached lows that would have been dismissed as unattainable just a few years ago." Kaplan: "Labor shortages are the biggest constraint we see to energy production growth in 2018, and labor shortages are also, we think, the biggest constraint to growth across industries." Powell: "Monthly job gains averaged 240,000 over the past three months...more than twice the pace required to keep the unemployment rate stable."
- **Center of gravity**: The labor market is clearly in tight territory — widespread reports of labor shortages, unemployment at 4.1% and expected to fall to 3.5–3.7% over the horizon. Wage growth puzzlingly modest despite tightness.
- **Minority views**: Kashkari argues the gauge is broken and there may be more hidden slack; believes 4.1% headline may overstate tightness because prime-age LFP is still recovering.

### 4. CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT
**Score: 53 ±7 | Confidence: Moderate**

- **Evidence**: Powell: "Consumption, in particular, appears to have slowed significantly from a very strong 4¼ percent annualized growth in the fourth quarter to an estimated 1½ percent in the current quarter." Rosengren: "high-frequency data related to consumption and investment for the first quarter have been slower than I expected." Brainard: "payrolls have strengthened from an already expansionary pace...consumer sentiment and business surveys remain strong." Mester: "Incoming data suggest a moderation in consumer and business spending early in the year from the strong level seen last quarter. I expect the recent softness to be temporary."
- **Center of gravity**: Q1 spending data weak, but participants nearly unanimously attribute this to temporary/seasonal factors; underlying fundamentals (sentiment, tax cuts, global growth) are strong. Net assessment is at or slightly above trend.
- **Minority views**: Bostic notes some contacts shelving projects due to trade uncertainty. Bullard points to Q1 weakness as a risk factor.

### 5. FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 37 ±7 | Confidence: High**

- **Evidence**: Mester: "financial conditions remain accommodative." Brainard: "despite the recent choppiness in financial markets, financial conditions remain supportive of economic growth...corporate bond spreads have widened somewhat over the intermeeting period, they still remain quite compressed by historical standards." Evans: "both the overall [CFNAI] index and the volatility subindex remain well below their long-run historical averages." Powell: "financial conditions remain accommodative despite a modest net tightening since the Committee's January meeting."
- **Center of gravity**: Near-unanimous view that financial conditions remain accommodative/loose, with only a modest net tightening since January. Conditions supportive of growth.
- **Minority views**: None. This is one of the few dimensions where participants essentially agree.

### 6. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN
**Score: 70 ±7 | Confidence: High**

- **Evidence**: Williams: "my SEP submission calls for three additional rate increases this year and another four next year." Dudley: "four 25 basis point hikes are likely to be appropriate this year." George: "I maintain four rate increases as appropriate in 2018." Rosengren advocates for possible faster pace if economy performs as Tealbook predicts.
- **Center of gravity**: Near-universal support for the 25bp hike at this meeting, with most participants signaling 3–4 more hikes in 2018 and a path that likely goes above the long-run neutral rate by 2019–2020. Clearly in "strong hawkish" territory by the rubric standards.
- **Minority views**: Evans preferred to hold today but accepted consensus (reduced weight); Kashkari preferred alt-A (hold) but accepted alt-B for continuity; Bullard supports alt-B but is more concerned about over-tightening past 2%.

### 7. DISSENT_INTENSITY
**Score: 28 ±8 | Confidence: High**

- **Evidence**: Multiple speakers: "I support alternative B as written" (Rosengren, Williams, Bostic, Harker, Mester, Barkin, Quarles, Kaplan, George, Brainard, Dudley, Powell). Evans: "I support alternative B" (accepted consensus despite preference for hold). Kashkari: "I support alternative B in the context of continuity...just looking at the data, I would prefer alternative A."
- **Center of gravity**: Zero formal dissents. Two participants expressed reservations (Evans, Kashkari) but voted with the majority. Philosophical differences about r*, labor slack, and inflation dynamics present but not breaking into dissent.
- **Minority views**: The debate on r* (Bullard vs. Williams) represents real intellectual disagreement but both supported the same policy action.

### 8. FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS
**Score: 38 ±8 | Confidence: Moderate**

- **Evidence**: Brainard: "asset valuations are currently elevated, overall risks to the financial system remain moderate because household borrowing is still moderate...I would be reluctant to see our large banking organizations releasing the capital and liquidity buffers." Kaplan: "I worry much more...about excesses and imbalances building in the economy and in the financial markets because of an overshoot of full employment and the other effects of historically low interest rates." Dudley: "risks of economic overheating have increased...there's a risk of a much bumpier ride a couple of years down the road."
- **Center of gravity**: Financial stability is discussed by 3–5 participants, primarily as a monitoring concern rather than a current crisis. Elevated asset valuations and potential imbalances mentioned, but overall financial system risk described as moderate.
- **Minority views**: Evans' contacts were "sanguine" about equity correction; viewed it as healthy. No one expressed acute financial stability alarm.

### 9. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE
**Score: 76 ±6 | Confidence: High**

- **Evidence**: Williams: "my SEP submission calls for three additional rate increases this year and another four next year...my projection...leads to the funds rate exceeding the longer-run neutral level a year from now." Mester: "continuing a gradual removal of accommodation is appropriate...I now anticipate that the funds rate will need to stay above 3 percent—my estimate of its longer-run level—into 2020." Multiple speakers note the paragraph 4 language (funds rate to remain below neutral) is becoming obsolete and should be removed at upcoming meetings.
- **Center of gravity**: Strong forward signal of continued tightening well into 2019–2020, with median SEP path likely overshooting the neutral rate. The imminent removal of "below neutral" language signals the tightening path will transition to restrictive territory.
- **Minority views**: Bullard argues caution about going above 2%; Evans wants a shallower path. These are minority views and reduce the score somewhat from 80.

### 10. UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED
**Score: 45 ±8 | Confidence: High**

- **Evidence**: Powell: "considerable uncertainty about just how strong that impetus is at the present...In view of the state of our knowledge about various unobservables, such as the natural rate of unemployment and the neutral rate of interest, we need to consider the two-sided risks." Brainard: "we don't have a lot of experience with a procyclical fiscal experiment of this nature, so it's hard to know with any precision how the economy is likely to respond." Kashkari: "our gauge is broken. We just don't know how much slack is in the labor market." Bullard: full session devoted to r* uncertainty.
- **Center of gravity**: Multiple, distinct sources of uncertainty acknowledged: r*, NAIRU, fiscal multiplier, trade policy. More than standard caveats but not "uncharted territory" alarm. Elevated but not extreme.
- **Minority views**: Williams describes risks to outlook as "balanced and not unusually large relative to the past 20 years"; this is a more confident assessment.
