# FOMC Meeting Scoring: 1998-08-18

## Part 1: Thinking Process

### Document type
This is a **transcript** with individual speaker statements from the go-around and a policy discussion. Speakers identified by name include: McTeer, Moskow, Parry, Hoenig, Stern, Guynn, Minehan, Boehne, McDonough, Jordan, Poole, Gramlich, Rivlin, Ferguson, Meyer, Kelley, and Chairman Greenspan.

### Era context
August 1998: The U.S. economy is in a late-1990s expansion with strong domestic demand, very tight labor markets, and low unemployment. The Asian financial crisis that began in 1997 is deepening, now spreading to Russia and Latin America. Oil and commodity prices are falling. The stock market has begun correcting. The FOMC has maintained an asymmetric tilt toward tightening since March 1998 but has not actually moved the funds rate.

### Core Dimension Reasoning

**INFLATION_PRESSURE**: Participants see inflation as below the midpoint -- currently very low. Multiple speakers note outstanding/subdued inflation. Parry: "The news on inflation certainly continues to be outstanding." GDP price index "under 1 percent for the second straight quarter." Boehne: "U.S. inflation remains subdued." Meyer notes "few signs of building inflation pressure." However, multiple participants note wage acceleration (ECI rising, labor cost increases of 4-6%), and some (Minehan, Guynn, Jordan) see upside inflation risks from tight labor markets. The CENTER OF GRAVITY is that current inflation is low/benign, but wage pressures and tight labor markets create forward risk. Current readings are well below 2% target by some measures. Score should be in the 35-42 range -- below target with slack keeping prices in check through favorable supply shocks, but wage pressures preventing a lower score. Direction: below midpoint.

**OUTPUT_GAP**: The economy is clearly running above potential. Domestic demand is very strong. Growth was 3.5% in the first half. Labor markets are extremely tight. Multiple speakers describe "strong" domestic demand. However, the external drag from Asia is pulling GDP growth down toward or below trend. Poole: "we probably have overshot full employment." Meyer: growth was 3.5% first half despite 2.5 ppt subtraction from net exports. Greenbook projects below-trend growth ahead. The current assessment is that the economy has been running above potential but is moderating. Bright-line rule: "above potential" language dominates -- Poole explicitly says overshot full employment; construction "soars to unsustainable levels" (Jordan); domestic demand "very strong." Score must be >50. The moderation from Asia pulls it back somewhat. Score around 55-58.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS**: Nearly every speaker emphasizes extremely tight labor markets. Moskow: "Labor markets remain very tight" with Manpower survey "at the highest levels in the survey's 22-year history." Minehan: "labor markets remain extremely tight." Stern: "Labor markets are tight... labor is becoming more militant." Jordan: union halls empty, training prisoners/parolees. Hoenig: "Labor markets remain very tight... more reports of wage pressures." Poole: "overshot full employment." Greenspan: labor markets are "sufficiently tight." This is unambiguously very tight -- the center of gravity is overwhelmingly tight. Score in the 68-72 range, consistent with era anchors (65-75 for 1999-2000).

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT**: Domestic demand is very strong. Consumer spending repeatedly described as strong/surprising on the upside. Moskow: "Retailers indicate that consumer spending continues to surprise them on the upside." Construction robust everywhere. Investment strong. Meyer: "domestic final sales remain strong." However, some manufacturing weakness from Asia. Net assessment is clearly above trend. Score around 62-65.

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS**: Financial conditions are mixed. On one hand, long-term rates have declined, credit is freely available, and lending competition is intense (Minehan notes bank lending growth double the national rate, "competition among lenders reportedly remains intense"). On the other hand, the stock market has corrected, some widening of spreads is noted, liquidity is reducing in some markets (McDonough: "considerably less liquidity as large financial institutions... react to the uncertainty by reducing their trading positions"). Jordan notes banks doing deals at "ever thinner margins, longer terms, less collateral." The balance is still on the loose side -- conditions remain generally accommodative despite some recent tightening from market volatility. Score around 38-42 -- somewhat loose to neutral.

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN**: Unanimous support for no change (alternative B). The key shift is from asymmetric (toward tightening) to symmetric directive. Most participants see risks shifting toward more balanced or downside. McTeer explicitly calls for easing. Gramlich says time may come "perhaps very soon" to lower rates. But Minehan, Meyer, Jordan, Guynn, Kelley, and Moskow still see upside inflation risks; some prefer to keep asymmetry. The center of gravity is: hold steady, shift to symmetric (balanced), with a mild residual hawkish lean among several. Score around 48-50 -- lean dovish to balanced, moving from prior hawkish stance. The move TO symmetry FROM asymmetric tightening tilt is itself a dovish shift. Score: 48.

**DISSENT_INTENSITY**: No formal dissents -- unanimous vote for B symmetric. But notable range of views: McTeer wants easing now; Jordan argues for tightening; several (Kelley, Guynn, Moskow, Hoenig) preferred to keep asymmetric tilt; Gramlich says easing may come soon. Wide range of economic views but convergence on "hold and wait." Score around 35-40 -- moderate differences with no dissents but significant spread in views.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS**: Substantial discussion of financial conditions and stability. Jordan gives extended remarks on speculative imbalances, credit excesses, "absence of consumer price inflation is not a sufficient condition for financial stability." McDonough discusses market liquidity drying up. Minehan discusses financial market speculation risks. Stock market correction discussed by many. Asian financial contagion discussed extensively. This is a prominent topic -- not dominant but clearly significant. Score around 55-62.

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE**: The shift to symmetric directive is the key signal. This communicates that the next move is equally likely to be up or down. Several participants (McDonough, Rivlin) explicitly state the next move is equally likely in either direction. This is a neutral forward signal. Gramlich hints easing may come soon but this is a minority view. Score around 50 -- neutral, data-dependent.

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED**: Multiple participants emphasize high uncertainty. Moskow: "there is a great deal of uncertainty." Stern: "the risks look to me to be very large." McDonough: "I do not recall a time when there has been more uncertainty in financial markets." Ferguson: "a delicate balance... fraught with uncertainties." Kelley: risks in both directions. Greenspan discusses data problems extensively -- employment data diverging, inflation measures diverging, "our data have deserted us." Very elevated uncertainty. Score around 58-65.

### Cross-dimension coherence
- OUTPUT_GAP (57) and LABOR_TIGHTNESS (70): Labor tightness is higher than output gap. This makes sense because: (1) the economy has been running hot for years so labor tightness is a cumulative/level measure reflecting years of expansion, (2) the output gap is being pulled down by the external drag from Asia even though domestic demand remains very strong. The divergence is explained by the two-track economy (strong domestic, weak external).
- HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN (48) and FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE (50): These align well -- the shift to symmetric from asymmetric tightening reflects a roughly balanced/slightly dovish lean with a neutral forward signal.

### Speaker count by view direction
- Risks balanced/shifting downside: McDonough, Rivlin, Gramlich, Boehne, Parry, Ferguson, Stern, Meyer, Kelley, McTeer, Poole -- ~11 participants
- Risks still on upside: Minehan (somewhat), Guynn, Moskow, Jordan, Hoenig (somewhat) -- ~5 participants
- On policy: All support hold. Most support symmetry. Kelley, Guynn, Moskow, Hoenig, Jordan prefer asymmetry but can accept symmetry.

## Part 2: Evidence and Scores

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE
**Score: 38 +/-8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Parry: "The news on inflation certainly continues to be outstanding. The GDP price index came in under 1 percent for the second straight quarter."
- Meyer: "on balance they are showing few signs of building inflation pressure"
- Boehne: "U.S. inflation remains subdued, and in my estimation the threat of a breakout on the upside has lessened"
- Moskow: "competition constrains their ability to raise prices" but notes "labor cost increases of 4 to 6 percent"

**Center-of-gravity**: Participants see current inflation as clearly below target, with benign price readings driven by favorable supply shocks (Asia, oil, strong dollar), but they recognize rising wage pressures that could translate to price inflation.

**Minority views**: Minehan projects CPI inflation rising to 3% by Q4 1999. Guynn sees upside inflation risk. Jordan cites median CPI at 2.8-3.0%. These views widen the uncertainty band upward.

---

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP
**Score: 57 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Poole: "we probably have overshot full employment and are seeing evidence of that in the upcreep of wages"
- Meyer: "The economy grew at a 3-1/2 percent rate in the first half despite a subtraction of 2-1/2 percentage points stemming from the decline in net exports"
- Gramlich: "Real GDP according to the Greenbook is already below the trend growth rate in this half year"

**Center-of-gravity**: The economy has been running above potential with very strong domestic demand, but the external drag is pulling growth toward or below trend. The accumulated above-potential position is still evident in tight labor markets and resource constraints.

**Minority views**: Gramlich and Rivlin emphasize the economy slowing below trend; McTeer sees downside risks as greater. These push toward a lower score.

---

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 70 +/-5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Moskow: "Labor markets remain very tight, and the Manpower survey... will show strong hiring plans... at the highest levels in the survey's 22-year history"
- Minehan: "labor markets remain extremely tight... Hiring bonuses are commonplace even for fairly low-level jobs"
- Stern: "Labor markets are tight... labor is becoming more militant"
- Jordan: "union halls were empty, so they are now providing training to prisoners and parolees"

**Center-of-gravity**: Near-universal agreement that labor markets are very tight with widespread shortages, rising wages, and increasing worker militancy. This is among the tightest labor markets in recent memory.

**Minority views**: None dissent from the assessment of very tight labor markets. Narrow band.

---

### 4. CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT
**Score: 63 +/-6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Moskow: "Retailers indicate that consumer spending continues to surprise them on the upside... Economic fundamentals for the consumption and investment sectors continue to look strong"
- Jordan: "Construction projects continue to be announced at a rapid pace... Retail sales were reported to be surprisingly strong"
- Guynn: "considerable momentum in domestic consumption and investment"
- Meyer: "domestic final sales remain strong"

**Center-of-gravity**: Consumer spending is very strong and surprising on the upside. Construction is robust. Business investment remains solid. Manufacturing is weakened by Asia but service sector is booming. Net domestic demand is clearly above trend.

**Minority views**: Some note manufacturing weakness from Asia (Minehan, Moskow). The Greenbook projects slowing ahead.

---

### 5. FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 40 +/-10 [Moderate confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Minehan: "Commercial and industrial lending at the District's large banks grew at rates about double that of the nation... competition among lenders reportedly remains intense"
- Jordan: "banking directors... report that the deals are being done at ever thinner margins, longer terms, less collateral, or no collateral"
- McDonough: "there is considerably less liquidity as large financial institutions... react to the uncertainty by reducing their trading positions"
- Minehan also notes: "some widening of spreads related to credit quality and the beginnings of some slight restraint in credit markets"

**Center-of-gravity**: Financial conditions remain broadly accommodative domestically -- credit is freely available, lending standards are loose, and competition intense. However, international financial turmoil and the stock market correction have introduced some tightening at the margins. The net position is still somewhat loose.

**Minority views**: McDonough emphasizes reduced liquidity and market uncertainty, pulling toward tighter. Jordan emphasizes excess credit accommodation.

---

### 6. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN
**Score: 48 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Greenspan: "alternative B, meaning 'no change' in the rate structure, is the appropriate position"
- McDonough: "I very strongly prefer that we move to a symmetric directive... I think our next rate move is equally likely to be up or down"
- Gramlich: "the time is coming, perhaps very soon, when the right thing to do may be to lower the funds rate a bit"
- Minehan: "I continue to believe that our next move should be to raise the federal funds rate" but acknowledges "a lot of worrisome downside risks"
- Meyer: "I still believe... the next move for the funds rate will be up rather than down" but supports symmetric posture

**Center-of-gravity**: Unanimous support for holding rates. The shift from asymmetric (tightening bias) to symmetric directive represents a mild dovish shift. Most see risks as more balanced, with the downside risks from the international sector offsetting domestic upside inflation risks. This is a balanced-to-slightly-dovish lean.

**Minority views**: McTeer wants easing now (dovish outlier). Jordan argues the more costly mistake would be not tightening (hawkish outlier). Several participants prefer to keep the tightening tilt.

---

### 7. DISSENT_INTENSITY
**Score: 38 +/-6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- No formal dissents -- unanimous vote for alternative B with symmetric directive
- But wide range: McTeer calls for easing; Jordan implies tightening needed; Minehan/Meyer/Guynn/Kelley/Moskow preferred asymmetric tilt
- Kelley: "I would prefer to stay with asymmetry because the basic structure of the risks... is still intact"
- Jordan: "the more costly mistake to correct will be the one that occurs if it turns out that excess demand growth does not in fact slow down"

**Center-of-gravity**: Broad agreement on holding rates, but notable differences on the directive tilt and risk assessment. About 5-6 participants preferred to retain the asymmetric tightening tilt. The spread of views on the economic outlook is substantial (from easing bias to tightening bias) but does not produce dissents.

**Minority views**: The hawkish minority (Jordan, Kelley, Guynn, Moskow) and the dovish minority (McTeer, Gramlich) widen the band.

---

### 8. FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS
**Score: 58 +/-8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Jordan (extended): "the absence of consumer price inflation is not a sufficient condition for financial stability... When there is a favorable productivity surprise, it brings with it an acceleration of the growth of business earnings and in equity prices"
- McDonough: "I do not recall a time when there has been more uncertainty in financial markets... large financial institutions... react to the uncertainty by reducing their trading positions"
- Minehan: "financial markets were fueling speculation that, even in the absence of inflation, could present real risks"
- Jordan: banking directors note "overbuilding of hotels, motels, and retail space... deals being done at ever thinner margins"

**Center-of-gravity**: Financial stability is a moderately prominent topic. The Asian financial crisis, stock market correction, credit conditions, speculative excesses, and market liquidity are discussed at length by multiple participants. Jordan devotes a large portion of his remarks to financial stability concerns.

**Minority views**: Some participants barely mention financial stability per se, focusing more on the real economic effects of Asia.

---

### 9. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE
**Score: 50 +/-8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- McDonough: "our next rate move is equally likely to be up or down"
- Rivlin: "we are unlikely to change monetary policy anytime soon and that, when we do, we may move the fed funds rate up or down"
- Gramlich: "the time is coming, perhaps very soon, when the right thing to do may be to lower the funds rate a bit"
- Meyer: "I still believe... the next move for the funds rate will be up rather than down"

**Center-of-gravity**: The shift to symmetric directive is a clear signal of neutrality -- no directional lean. The committee explicitly communicates that the next move is data-dependent and could go either way.

**Minority views**: Gramlich leans toward easing soon (dovish forward). Meyer and Minehan lean toward eventual tightening (hawkish forward).

---

### 10. UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED
**Score: 62 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- McDonough: "I do not recall a time when there has been more uncertainty in financial markets"
- Stern: "the risks look to me to be very large... risks that I see on both sides are significant"
- Greenspan: extensive discussion of data problems -- "our data have deserted us" -- employment surveys diverging, inflation measures diverging, productivity data uncertain
- Meyer: "Given the uncertainty about the structure of the economy and the forecast going forward"
- Ferguson: "a delicate balance... fraught with uncertainties, none of which is of our own making"

**Center-of-gravity**: Uncertainty is elevated. Multiple participants emphasize large risks on both sides, unprecedented financial market uncertainty, and fundamental difficulty in reading the data. Greenspan's extended discussion of statistical discrepancies adds a layer of analytical uncertainty unusual for FOMC deliberations.

**Minority views**: Poole tries to put the Asia impact in perspective, suggesting magnitudes may be smaller than feared. But even he concludes uncertainties are "quite substantial."

---

### Optional Dimensions

**INFLATION_GOODS**: Goods prices falling due to Asian imports, oil decline, commodity weakness. Jordan: "Goods prices are falling while service prices are accelerating." Parry: "falling commodity prices." Score: **30 +/-8 [Moderate confidence]** -- clear disinflationary/deflationary pressure in goods.

**INFLATION_SERVICES**: Jordan: "service prices are accelerating." Stern: "Rents are moving up significantly." Minehan: "Regional consumer prices are thought to be rising slightly faster than the average for the nation." Score: **60 +/-8 [Moderate confidence]** -- above-target and rising.

**INFLATION_HOUSING**: Stern: "markets for rental apartments have become very, very tight in the urban areas... Rents are moving up significantly." McDonough: "Office rents surged at a 27 percent annual rate in Manhattan." Score: **65 +/-10 [Moderate confidence]** -- clearly rising rents/shelter costs.

**INFLATION_EXPECTATIONS**: Parry: "diminished inflation expectations" as an offset to wage pressure. Greenspan discusses market measures. Hoenig notes market pricing of deflation risk. Score: **42 +/-8 [Moderate confidence]** -- expectations declining, somewhat below target/anchored low.

**CREDIT_STRESS**: Jordan: deals at "ever thinner margins, longer terms, less collateral." Minehan: "some widening of spreads" but "competition among lenders reportedly remains intense." Score: **35 +/-10 [Moderate confidence]** -- below normal stress; credit conditions loose with some early signs of widening.

**EQUITY_STRESS**: Ferguson: "the recent so-called near correction in the stock market." Kelley: "if the stock markets still have the stamina for one or more bull runs." Multiple participants note the correction. Some (Jordan) express overvaluation concern. Score: **38 +/-10 [Moderate confidence]** -- mild concern about overvaluation/correction, not panic.

**BANKING_STRESS**: N/A -- no prominent discussion of banking system stress per se. Jordan mentions loose lending standards but frames it as excess risk-taking, not impairment.

**TREASURY_STRESS**: N/A

**DOLLAR_CONCERN**: McTeer: "the stronger dollar" hurting exports. Parry: "the high dollar" as a restraining factor. Stern: paper industry "talking a lot about... the need for a lower dollar." Score: **62 +/-8 [Moderate confidence]** -- concern about dollar strength hurting competitiveness and reinforcing deflationary pressures from Asia.

**PRODUCTIVITY**: Greenspan discusses productivity data extensively. Parry: "somewhat higher trend productivity growth." Minehan: "greater agnosticism... as to whether the higher rates of productivity growth we have seen are cyclical or more structural." Score: **62 +/-8 [Moderate confidence]** -- participants perceive above-trend productivity growth, though uncertain about its permanence.
