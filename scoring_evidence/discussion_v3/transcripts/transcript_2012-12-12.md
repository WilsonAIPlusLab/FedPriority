# FOMC Transcript Scoring — December 11–12, 2012

---

## Part 1: Thinking Process

### 1. Document Type

This is a **transcript** of the December 11–12, 2012 FOMC meeting. It is fully segmented: individual speakers are identified by name (MR. WILLIAMS, VICE CHAIRMAN DUDLEY, CHAIRMAN BERNANKE, etc.). The document covers two sessions: Day 1 (economic go-around + Chairman Bernanke's policy framework presentation) and Day 2 (policy go-round vote). This is a long, richly detailed transcript with 17+ identified participant speakers making substantive remarks. High confidence is warranted across most dimensions.

### 2. Era Context

December 2012 is in the heart of the post-GFC slow recovery period. The unemployment rate stood at 7.7%, the economy was growing at roughly 1.5–2% annualized, inflation was running around 1.6–1.7% on a trimmed mean basis — well below the 2% PCE target. The dominant macro concerns were: (1) the "fiscal cliff" (expiring tax cuts and spending sequestration), (2) whether to replace the expiring Maturity Extension Program (MEP/Twist) with outright Treasury purchases (QE3 extension), and (3) whether to adopt numerical unemployment/inflation thresholds in forward guidance in lieu of date-based guidance. This meeting culminated in the decision to proceed with $45B/month Treasury purchases on top of the existing $40B/month MBS purchases, and the adoption of the 6.5%/2.5% Evans Rule thresholds. The era anchor for OUTPUT_GAP is approximately 35–42 (recovering but slack remains).

### 3. Directional Analysis — Core Dimensions

**INFLATION_PRESSURE:** Participants universally describe inflation as running below the 2% target. Williams: "both headline and core PCE inflation will remain below 2 percent for the next several years." Fisher (Dallas trimmed mean): "12-month run rate for October is about 1.7 percent, and the 6-month run rate is about 1½ percent." Kocherlakota: "PCE inflation will be at about 1.6 percent in 2013." Rosengren: "we've continued to undershoot our inflation target for the entire forecast horizon...core PCE has been below 2 percent for four years." Bernanke summary: "inflation remains relatively tame, likely running at or below the Committee's target over the medium term. Trimmed mean inflation is 1.7 percent...unit labor costs have fallen." Even hawkish voices (Plosser, George) discuss inflation risks to the upside only as a concern for the future, not a current reality. There is essentially no participant who argues inflation is at or above target now. Direction: well below target. Score: approximately 33–37 (below-target; disinflation range). This is consistent with the 2013–19 below-target anchor of 38–42; December 2012 is at the softer edge of that range given the explicit multi-year undershooting trajectory.

**Polarity check:** Higher = more inflation pressure. Evidence says low/declining pressure → score below 50. Score 35 is consistent.

**OUTPUT_GAP:** Participants consistently describe an economy "below potential" with significant slack. Bernanke: "we are taking significant risks with an economy that is obviously underperforming." Williams: "stiff headwinds holding back aggregate demand." Yellen: "no significant improvement in the outlook for the labor market to date." Evans: "second-half GDP growth seems to be around 2 percent as the familiar host of uncertainties and headwinds continue to take their toll." Tarullo: "the case for insufficient aggregate demand is still a pretty strong one." However, there is also recognition of some underlying positives (housing improvement, household deleveraging nearing completion, balance sheet strength). The consensus is clearly "below potential," not a deep crisis. The economy is at the 2010–14 recovery level. Direction: below potential, moderate-to-significant slack remains. Score: approximately 37–40.

**Polarity check:** Higher = more above potential. Evidence shows below potential → score below 50. Score 38 consistent.

**BRIGHT-LINE RULE check:** Multiple "below potential," "slack remains," "underperforming" references → score MUST be < 50. Score 38 satisfies this.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS:** Unemployment at 7.7% (headline) and 14.4% (U-6). Participants widely acknowledge the labor market remains weak. Yellen: "the unemployment rate has declined more than I had anticipated, but...those measures are not reassuring...hiring rate remains far below its pre-recession level." Tarullo: "U-6 still up at 14.4 percent, very high historically...no sign of real wage pressures." Williams: "involuntary part-time employment has grown considerably this year." Raskin: "flat real wages." However, there is also acknowledgment that unemployment dropped from 9.9% to 7.7% over three years, and some (Plosser, Lacker) suggest the labor market may be closer to a "balanced growth path" than the majority thinks. Lacker explicitly argues the 2.1 percentage point drop suggests the economy may be near a balanced growth path. But the center of gravity firmly views labor markets as weak/slack-ridden. Direction: clearly below full employment. Score: approximately 34–38 (consistent with 2011–14 anchor of 35–40).

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT:** Participants describe consumption and business investment as "sluggish" and below trend. Williams: "Households continue to trim debt...stiff headwinds holding back aggregate demand." Evans: "Business fixed investment has been quite weak." Yellen: "the near-term outlook, on balance, is weaker. Last Friday's report from the Michigan survey suggests that consumers are, for the first time, focusing intently on the fiscal cliff." Raskin: "real disposable income rose just 1½ percent." Powell: "capital spending is down and weak." On the positive side, housing is showing improvement (Duke, Rosengren, multiple speakers), autos are relatively strong, and business balance sheets are solid. But overall consumption and investment are below trend. Direction: below trend but not contracting. Score: approximately 37–41.

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS:** Conditions are generally described as accommodative/loose, not tight. Dudley: "financial conditions are accommodative right now." Bernanke notes "there are no signs of market dysfunction." Williams: "Many potential borrowers still face tight credit" — but this is about specific borrowers, not overall conditions. Lacker: "bankers routinely talk of weak loan demand and fierce competition." Fisher: "only 6 percent of small- and medium-sized businesses felt that their credit needs were not being met." Duke: "Earnings pressure seems, to me, to point to continued easing of credit for all loan types." The FOMC itself is executing QE3 (MBS purchases at $40B/month) and is about to add $45B/month in Treasuries — this is a highly accommodative financial conditions environment. The discussion of financial stability risks is about risks from *excessive* ease (search for yield, leverage buildup), not from tight conditions. Direction: loose to somewhat loose; financial conditions are supportive. Score: approximately 25–32 (loose; "accommodative conditions").

**Polarity check:** Higher = tighter financial conditions. Evidence shows loose/accommodative conditions → score below 50. Score 28 consistent.

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN:** The vast majority of participants support Alternative B — continuing $40B/month MBS purchases PLUS replacing the expiring MEP with $45B/month Treasury purchases, and adopting 6.5%/2.5% Evans Rule thresholds. The QE debate is clearly about "how much to buy" and "how to communicate the exit," not about whether to tighten. Only Lacker explicitly "opposes" the asset purchase expansion. Stein, Powell, Duke, and Pianalto support Alternative B with "serious reservations" and would have preferred a smaller purchase amount or more exit signaling. Plosser and Fisher support the thresholds version but have concerns about the Treasury purchase size. Bullard would prefer $35B in Treasuries (less than proposed). But all votes (except Lacker's dissent) go to Alternative B. This is a deeply dovish meeting: open-ended QE continuation, new threshold-based accommodation commitments, widespread language about "below target on both sides of mandate." The rubric anchor for "QE era 2010-12" is 15–25. This meeting is squarely in that range, but slightly above 2010 QE2 (where there was more consensus for expansion) given the reservations. Direction: strongly dovish, QE expansion + new threshold accommodation. Score: approximately 18–22 (strong dovish, majority favors continued/expanded QE, only 1 dissent).

**Polarity check:** Higher = more hawkish. Evidence is strongly dovish (QE expansion, threshold accommodation) → score well below 50. Score 20 consistent.

**DISSENT_INTENSITY:** There is one formal dissent (Lacker), plus several "reluctant supporters" with reservations (Stein with "fairly serious reservations," Powell with "misgivings," Duke preferring reduced Treasury purchases, Pianalto with attenuated efficacy concerns, Plosser supporting thresholds but skeptical of Treasury purchases). The substantive disagreement concerns: (a) whether to expand balance sheet, (b) the size of Treasury purchases ($45B vs. $35B vs. nothing), (c) quantitative vs. qualitative thresholds, and (d) the risk of exit problems. This is more than a "minor emphasis differences" meeting but less than a "multiple formal dissents" meeting. The presence of multiple strong reservations and significant policy disagreements about balance sheet size and threshold design puts this in the 35–48 range. Direction: moderate disagreement. Score: approximately 42.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS:** Financial stability is a recurring and substantive theme in this meeting. Yellen devotes multiple paragraphs to the risk of "excessive search for yield and dangerous buildup of leverage." Stein makes it a central concern ("C(x) convexity"), devoting substantial remarks to fiscal risk and balance sheet costs. Powell calls his "other" cost category "flashing red: Financial stability." Pianalto raises it multiple times. Bernanke explicitly addresses it in his framework. Raskin discusses it in the context of forward guidance credibility. Multiple participants invoke the term "financial stability" explicitly. However, the meeting is not overwhelmingly dominated by financial stability concerns — the primary focus is on the economic outlook, fiscal cliff, and QE/threshold policy design. Direction: notable but not dominant. Score: approximately 48–55 (moderate, several participants address meaningfully).

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE:** This meeting is the one where the committee transitions from calendar-date guidance (mid-2015) to the Evans Rule thresholds (6.5% unemployment / 2.5% inflation). The thresholds are calibrated to be *consistent* with the mid-2015 date guidance — i.e., they are not intended to be more hawkish. Bernanke explicitly says "the statement is very explicit that the thresholds are consistent with our earlier date guidance." The forward signal is: ultra-low rates until unemployment reaches 6.5% (then still data-dependent) AND "considerable time after the asset purchase program ends." This is among the strongest possible easing commitments — threshold-based guidance promising near-zero rates, satisfying the rubric anchor of 3–8 ("strongest easing commitment: threshold-based or date-based guidance promising near-zero rates"). Score: approximately 4–7.

**Polarity check:** Higher = more hawkish forward signal. The Evans Rule thresholds at 6.5%/2.5% with rates low "at least until" those thresholds are met is an extremely dovish forward signal → score well below 10. Score 5 consistent.

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED:** Uncertainty is pervasive in this meeting, primarily from three sources: (1) the fiscal cliff outcome (unknown), (2) the efficacy of QE (debated, "very difficult to assess"), and (3) the exit risk (acknowledged as "legitimate concern" by multiple speakers). Multiple participants express genuine uncertainty: Williams warns of larger-than-expected fiscal multipliers; Dudley notes a very wide range of Q4-over-Q4 GDP outcomes ("minus 2 percent" to "plus 4 percent"); Plosser stresses large forecast errors and uncertainty about the Phillips curve; Stein constructs a "Groundhog Day" scenario explicitly framed as "a little bit less likely" but very relevant; Powell warns of "vast uncertainties." However, the uncertainty is not of the "uncharted territory / cannot bound the range" variety of March 2020 — there is a modal forecast, and the main uncertainties are about fiscal outcomes and policy efficacy, not about the fundamental state of the economy. Direction: elevated uncertainty, above typical for stable expansion. Score: approximately 55–62 (elevated, multiple participants emphasize unusual uncertainty; "particularly uncertain" tone on fiscal cliff).

### 4. Cross-Dimension Coherence Check

- OUTPUT_GAP (38) and LABOR_TIGHTNESS (36): Both below 50, consistent direction. The slight divergence reflects: output gap is partially "known" (GDP growing at modest pace) while labor market has structural mismatch concerns that create ambiguity in tightness reading. Both in the 35–42 recovery range — coherent.
- HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN (20) and FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE (5): Both strongly dovish — coherent. The FORWARD_GUIDANCE is even more dovish than the HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN score because the threshold commitment itself is the strongest form of dovish signaling.
- INFLATION_PRESSURE (35) and OUTPUT_GAP (38): Both below 50, consistent with below-target inflation driven by slack — coherent.

## Part 2: Evidence and Scores

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE

**Score: 35 ±5 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Rosengren: "the four-quarter moving average of the change in core PCE has been below 2 percent for four years, and we expect it to remain below our target for another three years."
- Bernanke (summary of go-round): "inflation remains relatively tame, likely running at or below the Committee's target over the medium term. Trimmed mean inflation is 1.7 percent over the past 12 months; median inflation was also quite tame."
- Williams: "both headline and core PCE inflation will remain below 2 percent for the next several years. Uncertainty...putting downward pressure on inflation."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** The overwhelming consensus is that inflation is running below 2% PCE, with trimmed mean measures around 1.6–1.7%, unit labor costs flat or falling, and no wage pressures evident — consistent with multi-year below-target inflation.

**Minority views:** Plosser warns that the estimated coefficient on the output gap in the Phillips curve "is now quite small" and cautions that relying on resource slack to keep inflation subdued may be wrong; George expects inflation "to begin to rise above 2 percent in 2014 and 2015" under continued accommodation. These create upward uncertainty in the band.

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP

**Score: 38 ±6 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Bernanke: "we are taking significant risks with an economy that is obviously underperforming, and we are missing on both sides of our mandate."
- Tarullo: "the case for insufficient aggregate demand is still a pretty strong one and is likely to be strong regardless of the resolution of the fiscal cliff."
- Evans: "second-half GDP growth seems to be around 2 percent as the familiar host of uncertainties and headwinds continue to take their toll."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Participants broadly see the economy operating below potential, with GDP growth around 2% (near or modestly below trend potential), significant labor market slack, and consumption/investment being held back by fiscal uncertainty and headwinds. The economy is recovering but the output gap has not closed.

**Minority views:** Lacker argues the economy may be near "a balanced growth path right now" given the 2.1 percentage point unemployment decline with modest GDP growth; Plosser is "considerably more optimistic than the Tealbook about labor market conditions." These push the lower bound of the uncertainty range upward.

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS

**Score: 36 ±6 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Yellen: "The JOLTS data for October...showed that the hiring rate remains far below its pre-recession level, and the quit rate remains very depressed. Such readings on turnover suggest no substantial improvement in the labor market outlook thus far."
- Tarullo: "U-6, my favorite broadest measure of capacity underutilization in the labor market...is still up at 14.4 percent, very high historically...the number of people employed part-time for economic reasons is still about double the level that prevailed pretty consistently for a decade before the crisis."
- Bernanke (summary): "conditions in the job market remain generally sluggish. The loss of UI benefits may be one reason for lower unemployment, as people are leaving the labor force. Hiring is still weak."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Despite the unemployment rate declining to 7.7%, participants broadly view the labor market as substantially below full employment, with broad-based measures (U-6 at 14.4%), weak hiring and quit rates, growing involuntary part-time employment, and flat wage growth all signaling significant slack.

**Minority views:** Lacker argues the 139K/month job gain trend looks like a "balanced growth path"; Plosser projects unemployment reaching 7% by year-end 2013 and is "considerably more optimistic than the Tealbook." These are outlier views.

### 4. CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT

**Score: 39 ±6 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Evans: "BFI has been quite weak, and the forward indicators and anecdotal reports from my business contacts don't suggest a change in this anytime soon. In addition, consumption has been softer than expected. Real income growth seems weaker."
- Yellen: "the near-term outlook, on balance, is weaker. Last Friday's report from the Michigan survey suggests that consumers are, for the first time, focusing intently on the fiscal cliff."
- Powell: "corporate profits...have been down; guidance has been down across the board; capital spending is down and weak. My industrial contacts report nothing but weakness and significant layoffs in the coming months."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Business fixed investment is weak and declining amid fiscal uncertainty; consumer spending is softer than expected with real disposable income constrained. Housing is the notable bright spot (Duke, Rosengren highlight it), but overall consumption and investment are below trend, dragged by fiscal uncertainty and household deleveraging.

**Minority views:** Duke and Dudley are more optimistic about housing momentum and business balance sheet strength as latent drivers of spending recovery; Bullard notes "relatively upbeat households" in the Eighth District. Housing is an explicit positive counterweight.

### 5. FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS

**Score: 28 ±7 | Confidence: Moderate**

**Evidence:**
- Dudley: "financial conditions are accommodative right now."
- Bernanke (on fiscal risk framing): "The banking system is stronger than it was a few years ago, so credit availability is gradually improving."
- Fisher: "only 6 percent of small- and medium-sized businesses felt that their credit needs were not being met" — loose/improving credit availability broadly.

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Financial conditions are broadly accommodative, with QE3 driving mortgage rates to historically low levels, credit availability gradually improving, and no signs of market dysfunction. The FOMC is in the process of further expanding accommodation ($85B/month total). Some specific borrowers (certain mortgage applicants, small businesses in some sectors) still face constraints, but the general conditions are loose.

**Minority views:** Williams notes "Many potential borrowers still face tight credit" and Rosengren discusses the primary-secondary mortgage spread (capacity constraints in mortgage origination), which creates some upward pressure on the estimate. Duke discusses regulatory headwinds to mortgage credit. These push conditions slightly above extreme-loose levels.

### 6. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN

**Score: 20 ±5 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Dudley: "we've essentially said that we'd do whatever it takes to generate a more rapid improvement in labor market conditions consistent with our price stability goal. To dial back on that commitment now...would, to me, seem to be reneging on our commitment."
- Rosengren: "I support alternative B...Additional asset purchases are needed to shorten the time we continue to miss on our inflation and employment targets...Even if we just had one mandate and that was an inflation mandate, I would argue that we should be doing more."
- Bernanke (summary): "we are missing on both sides of our mandate...it is reasonable to follow through on that commitment and to continue the accommodation that we discussed in September."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** The overwhelming center of gravity is strongly dovish — the meeting results in continuation and expansion of QE (from $40B/month MBS to $85B/month combined), adoption of threshold-based forward guidance promising ultra-low rates until 6.5% unemployment, and explicit statements that further accommodation is warranted given below-mandate performance on both inflation and employment. One formal dissent (Lacker) opposing all asset purchases.

**Minority views:** Stein, Powell, Duke, Pianalto, Bullard, and Plosser would have preferred a smaller or differently structured purchase program, pushing the center slightly hawkward from the extreme. Lacker's formal dissent anchors the minority view firmly.

### 7. DISSENT_INTENSITY

**Score: 42 ±7 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Lacker: "It will come as no surprise that I oppose expanding our asset purchases for the same reason I opposed them in September. I view them as unlikely to aid economic growth without unacceptably increasing the risk of inflation."
- Stein: "I support alternative B, though I do so with fairly serious reservations...I would have preferred an option that kept the MBS purchases going at their current $40 billion clip but began to dial back the Treasury purchases."
- Powell: "I'm not supportive of a program of that size, and I am concerned that we may be sitting on bad news in the form of a disconnection between the expectations of 12 of the 19 participants and those of the market."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** One formal dissent (Lacker) plus five or more participants expressing substantial reservations (Stein, Powell, Duke, Pianalto, Bullard wanting $35B instead of $45B, Plosser skeptical of Treasury purchase efficacy). There is also a significant sub-debate about quantitative vs. qualitative thresholds. The disagreements extend to economic assessment (Lacker and Plosser argue the economy is near balanced growth) and policy design, warranting a score in the moderate-disagreement range.

### 8. FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Financial stability receives moderate-to-prominent discussion across multiple speakers (Yellen, Stein, Powell, Pianalto, Bernanke, Raskin). The dominant theme is the risk of excessive search for yield, leverage buildup, and potential sharp rate spike at exit. While not the primary agenda item (which was the QE design and threshold mechanics), financial stability permeates the cost-side analysis of LSAPs throughout both days.

### 9. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE

**Score: 5 ±3 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Bernanke (introducing the threshold): "the Committee views its current low-rate policy as likely to be appropriate at least until the specified thresholds are met" — specifically a 6.5% unemployment rate threshold with an inflation ceiling of 2.5%.
- Dudley: "Thresholds are better than date guidance because they remove us from the equation on a meeting-by-meeting basis. I don't think we'd want to change our threshold values very often, if at all."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** This meeting adopts the strongest form of threshold-based forward guidance — the Evans Rule — promising near-zero rates until a specific unemployment threshold is met, with ongoing QE purchases besides. The rubric anchor for this type of commitment (3–8, "strongest easing commitment: threshold-based or date-based guidance promising near-zero rates") is directly applicable. This is about as dovish as forward guidance gets without an explicit rate cut.

**Score: 58 ±8 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Dudley: "I can imagine minus 2 percent, on a Q4-over-Q4 basis, if we fall off the fiscal cliff. But I can imagine plus 4 percent if everything goes swimmingly. It seems to me that the fiscal cliff has been impeding hiring and investment, so there has been some delay in activity."

Multiple participants use explicit "considerable uncertainty," "very difficult to assess," and "cannot be quantified" language. The range of GDP scenarios offered by Dudley alone spans 6 percentage points, which is unusual.
