## Part 1: Thinking Process

### 1. Document Type
This is a **transcript** of the December 13, 2011 FOMC meeting. Speakers are identified individually by name (MR. LOCKHART., MS. YELLEN., CHAIRMAN BERNANKE., etc.). The document covers: (a) the economic go-round (pages 33–86), (b) the policy go-round (pages 90–113), and (c) an afternoon communications session on SEP enhancements and a consensus statement of longer-run goals. Scoring focuses on participant economic assessments and policy preferences, ignoring staff-attributed content.

### 2. Era Context
December 2011 is the post-GFC recovery era, approximately 2.5 years after the June 2009 trough. The funds rate has been at zero since December 2008. The Committee is running Operation Twist (MEP). Europe's sovereign debt crisis is the dominant macro risk. U.S. growth is moderate (~3¼% in Q4 2011 by some estimates), but 2012 baseline growth is projected around 2¼–2½%. Unemployment is ~8.6% and declining slowly. Inflation has moderated from a mid-2011 spike. The Tealbook is projecting PCE inflation of ~1¼–1½% over the next several years.

### 3. Directional Signals — Core Dimensions

**INFLATION_PRESSURE**
The dominant message from participants is that inflation pressure is abating. Williams: "Inflation pressures seen earlier this year have clearly abated... Six-month annualized changes in overall, core, and trimmed mean PCE prices are now all below 2 percent and heading lower." Pianalto: "I now expect both core and headline inflation to dip below 2 percent in the first half of next year." Yellen: "inflation now appears likely to drift substantially below the 2 percent level." Rosengren: "PCE inflation rate from undershooting 2 percent inflation over the medium term." Evans: "inflation seems most likely to run below 2 percent." Most participants see current PCE inflation still somewhat elevated (headline near 3% on 12-month basis per Lacker: "on a 12-month basis, we are still much closer to 3 percent than to 2 percent"), but on a declining trajectory that will go below 2%. Bernanke's summary: "Inflation looks to be continuing its recent moderation and may drift below the 2 percent implicit target." The consensus is: inflation is above target NOW but heading to or below target, with downside risks. A minority (Plosser, George, Lacker, Fisher) are alert to medium-term upside risks. Score reflects currently above but declining to below—center of gravity is that inflation is near or slightly above 2% currently but seen as moving below 2%, not alarming. Given 12-month headline still near 3% but momentum heading down, participants see pressure as modestly above target currently. Score: ~48 — centered just below target directionally, capturing the view that current readings are still close to or slightly above 2% but outlook is decisively below-target, with balance of risk to downside.

Direction check: Participants see inflation as slightly above target currently but declining below target in the forecast. The balance of concern is downside. Score should be below 50. The rubric 41-49 range is "slightly below target but not concerning." Given 12-month is still near 3% (Lacker's statement) but 6-month readings are below 2% (Williams), and most see inflation going below 2%, I place this at **45** — slightly below the neutral 50, reflecting that current readings still have some residual pressure from earlier energy shocks but the trajectory and outlook are below target. This is consistent with 2011-era disinflationary momentum.

**OUTPUT_GAP**
Near-unanimous view that a substantial negative output gap exists. Yellen: "still appears to be a massive output gap." Evans: "there still appears to be a massive output gap." Tarullo: "I think the aggregate demand shortfall is still the story." Rosengren: "extended period of only moderate growth that is insufficient to significantly reduce the unemployment rate." Bernanke: "expected pace of growth may be insufficient to reduce unemployment by very much." Even Bullard (the relative hawk) argues the gap is overstated but still acknowledges unemployment at 8.6% is historically high. No participant says the economy is operating at or above potential. Score must be below 50 per bright-line rule.

Direction: Participants see economy far below potential. Range of views: majority see large gap (Yellen, Evans, Tarullo, Williams, Rosengren, Raskin, Bernanke — all below 50, leaning 30-38). Bullard argues for smaller gap but still below potential. Score: **32** — clearly below potential, recovering but gap persists; consistent with 2011-era recovery characterization in anchor table (35-42).

Polarity check: Higher = more above potential. Evidence says below potential. Score 32 < 50. Consistent.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS**
Unemployment is 8.6% and declining, but all participants describe slack remaining. Raskin: "24 million underemployed people." Yellen: "exceptionally prolonged period of unacceptably high unemployment." Rosengren: "insufficient to significantly reduce the unemployment rate." Bernanke: "hiring remains slow, participation rates are down, labor income is stagnant." Even the more optimistic Plosser acknowledges "two steps forward and one step back in the labor market." Williams notes unemployment decline partly due to labor force participation drop. Bullard is more optimistic that unemployment declining faster than expected, and Kocherlakota discusses metric preferences, but neither argues for tight labor markets.

Direction: Participants see labor market as substantially below full employment, elevated unemployment, significant slack. Score anchor for 2011: 35-40.

Score: **33** — unemployment elevated at 8.6%, "slack remains," slow improvement, consistent with rubric range 31-45.

Polarity check: Higher = tighter labor market. Evidence says loose/slack. Score 33 < 50. Consistent.

Cross-check OUTPUT_GAP and LABOR_TIGHTNESS: both in low 30s. Appropriate — both reflect sub-potential, post-recession economy with substantial slack.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT**
Mixed but net below-trend picture. Recent data showed upside surprises (Q4 holiday spending, auto sales). Fisher: "sales paid by credit and debit cards were up 7.4 percent year over year in November... highest year-over-year gain in five years." Williams: "real GDP growth looks to be around 3¼ percent this quarter." But participants widely doubt sustainability. Raskin: "I'm not convinced that we've actually seen much sustainable improvement." Yellen: "the incoming data confirm the fundamental picture of an economy that is recovering only gradually." Bernanke: "Household spending has been somewhat stronger recently, but consumers remain uncertain." Income growth weak, saving rate drop potentially unsustainable. Business investment held back by uncertainty. Housing remains deeply depressed.

Direction: Participants see consumption/investment as below trend but with some near-term improvement impulse that is not expected to be durable. Net assessment: sluggish, below trend.

Score: **38** — below trend; "sluggish" with pockets of strength, consistent with rubric 31-45 range "below trend; sluggish; cautious consumers."

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS**
Conditions described as loose for large U.S. banks but with specific areas of stress. Plosser: "U.S. financial institutions have been able to access short-term funding markets on mostly normal terms." Dudley: "funding for the major U.S. banks and their broker-dealer subsidiaries has held up surprisingly well." Duke: "banks are actually seeing some increases in commercial lending." Fisher: "capital is widely available and attractively priced." But Rosengren details asset-backed commercial paper stress, money market fund vulnerabilities, elevated CDS spreads on Jefferies and Morgan Stanley. Williams: "tight credit" for small businesses. Pianalto: "tight financing conditions are hampering hiring at small businesses." Bernanke summary: "Some saw credit for small businesses as remaining tight, especially from weaker community lenders. Stresses have increased in the ABCP market."

Direction: Overall, financial conditions somewhat loose to neutral for large corporates, but with specific pockets of tightening (small business credit, ABCP, European bank stress). Net: modestly tight due to European overhang, not severely restrictive.

Score: **52** — near neutral with slight tightening bias; conditions "generally supportive" for large firms but specific stress areas create mild tightening, consistent with rubric 46-54 "neutral; normal credit, typical spreads" with a touch above due to ABCP and European bank CDS spreads.

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN**
This is the key policy dimension. The vote was for Alternative B (hold, no change). Most participants supported B. However, the distribution of views:
- Strong Alternative A advocates (want more easing): Evans (explicit), Yellen (compelling case, attracted to A), Tarullo (favors more accommodation), Rosengren (pushes for extending date or Option A language), Raskin (becoming persuaded need more accommodation but supports B for now), Vice Chairman Dudley (inclined toward A in January), Williams (supports B but notes dismal outlook).
- Alternative B supporters comfortable with current stance: Bernanke, Bullard, Lockhart, Pianalto, George, Fisher, Plosser, Kocherlakota, Lacker, Duke.
- Notably Plosser: "I do not recommend changing anything" and warns of inflation risk from accommodation.
- Fisher: Would vote against extending date language.

The QE2 era policy anchor is 15-25. This is a hold meeting but the dominant discussion is about whether more easing is needed. The majority (10+ participants) voted for B (hold), but 4-5 participants explicitly favor more accommodation (A-equivalent), and B supporters mostly frame it as a pause before potential future action, not as satisfaction with current stance. Very few participants (perhaps Plosser, Fisher, Lacker, Kocherlakota) oppose more accommodation on principle.

This places the meeting in the "strong dovish" range — committee is holding but most lean toward or accept more easing as likely coming. The rubric anchor 15-25 for QE era applies: "majority favors easing or maintaining max accommodation." Current accommodation is QE-level (MEP in place, zero rates). The discussion is about doing MORE.

Score: **22** — majority supports maintaining maximum accommodation; significant minority explicitly advocates more; this is clearly in the "strong dovish" range. The presence of QE-era discussion with majority comfortable at zero rates and many pushing for additional action is the dovish signal per rubric instruction.

Polarity check: Higher = more hawkish. Evidence says dovish (most favor hold with easing bias). Score 22 < 50. Consistent.

**DISSENT_INTENSITY**
One formal dissent was anticipated (Evans dissented in November). At this meeting Evans explicitly does not support B. Plosser flags he would vote against extending the date. Fisher says he would "vote against" extending the date language. The actual vote for Alternative B was broad but not unanimous — Evans clearly dissented. There are 4-5 participants with reservations or minority views. Tarullo, Yellen, Rosengren all want more but support B reluctantly. Bullard has a distinct intellectual framework from most. Kocherlakota has reservations about paragraph 5 language. Strong debate exists about the right policy.

The dissent pattern: 1 formal dissent (Evans), several expressed reservations about going further (Plosser: would dissent on date extension; Fisher: same). Substantive disagreement about economic framework between Bullard/Kocherlakota (smaller gap camp) and Yellen/Evans/Tarullo (larger gap, more accommodation needed camp). This is moderate to notable disagreement.

Score: **45** — notable disagreement: distinct camps (doves vs. those comfortable with current stance), 1 formal dissent, several reservations; extends to assessment (output gap magnitude). Consistent with rubric 46-60 range, slightly below given the final vote was broadly consensual for B.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS**
European financial stability is a major topic for almost every participant. Rosengren devotes substantial time to ABCP stress, broker-dealer funding risk, money market fund vulnerabilities. Dudley gives a long analysis of European sovereign debt and ECB role. Williams warns about Treasury curve if Europe blows up. Fisher discusses stock market correction risk (S&P to 800 scenario). Bernanke's summary extensively covers European financial stability. Multiple participants discuss swap lines, CDS spreads for U.S. and European banks, counterparty risk. This is a prominent but not dominant topic — economic assessment and policy debate receive comparable attention.

Score: **62** — prominent topic; most participants discuss European financial risks at length; consistent with rubric 65-75 minus a few points since it competes with economic and policy discussion rather than dominating.

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE**
Current guidance: "mid-2013" for exceptionally low rates. The discussion extensively debates extending this to end-2013 or 2014. Rosengren explicitly advocates extending to "2014" or at least "end of 2013." Yellen supports shifting to "late 2014." Dudley: "extending the date to the end of 2014 makes sense." But the decision was to hold mid-2013 language. The forward signal that emerges from this meeting is: (a) current guidance at mid-2013, (b) broad expectation that this will be extended, (c) possibility of additional MBS purchases in January, (d) no tightening bias whatsoever.

The meeting is QE-era with commitment to zero rates into 2013 with discussion of extending further. This is strongly in the easing/accommodative forward guidance range.

Score: **8** — strong easing bias: signal further/extended accommodation ahead, near-zero rates commitment for multi-year horizon, active discussion of extending date further; consistent with rubric 6-15 "strong easing bias: signal further/extended accommodation ahead."

Polarity check: Higher = more hawkish forward signal. Evidence says very dovish forward guidance. Score 8 < 50. Consistent.

HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN vs. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE check: Both low (22 and 8). Consistent — both reflect strong dovish orientation in the QE era. The slight divergence (22 vs. 8) captures that the current action is hold (slightly less dovish) while the forward signal is very dovish.

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED**
Elevated uncertainty is a near-universal theme. Europe is the dominant source. Lockhart devotes substantial attention to business uncertainty about demand and policy. Williams conducted VAR analysis on uncertainty shocks. Pianalto, Raskin, Bullard, Yellen, Bernanke all discuss uncertainty. Bernanke: "We clearly have an enormous amount of uncertainty tied to the European situation." Range of outcomes for unemployment (7.4% to 11.8% in Tealbook scenarios) is very wide. Participants explicitly note uncertainty about: Europe outcomes, output gap measurement, potential GDP, whether current consumption is sustainable, whether payroll data is being underestimated.

This is elevated but not "uncharted territory" extreme — participants have models, scenarios, and analysis. The uncertainty is real and discussed extensively but in a structured way.

Score: **58** — elevated: multiple participants emphasize unusual uncertainty about European outcomes and output gap; "particularly uncertain" about several factors; consistent with rubric 46-60.
