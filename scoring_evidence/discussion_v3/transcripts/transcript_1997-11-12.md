# FOMC Meeting Scoring: 1997-11-12

## Part 1: Thinking Process

### Document Type
This is a **transcript** with individual speakers identified. It covers the go-around discussion of regional/national conditions and a policy discussion. Speakers include: Hoenig, Parry, Jordan, Broaddus, Moskow, Minehan, McTeer, Guynn, Stern, Boehne, Meyer, McDonough, Rivlin, Kelley, Ferguson, Greenspan, and others.

### Era Context
November 1997: late-1990s expansion. The economy is growing strongly, unemployment is very low, yet measured inflation remains subdued -- the "new economy" puzzle. The Asian financial crisis is unfolding (Southeast Asia, Korea, Hong Kong), creating financial market turbulence and uncertainty. The Fed last hiked in March 1997 (+25bp to 5.50%). This meeting takes place at a moment of strong domestic fundamentals colliding with external financial risk.

### Core Dimension Reasoning

**INFLATION_PRESSURE**: Participants are in a puzzling position. Nearly all report extremely tight labor markets, rising wages/compensation (ECI up, anecdotal reports of 25% programmer wage gains, $14-15/hr entry-level tellers, rising health costs). Yet multiple speakers note that final goods prices remain "remarkably subdued," core inflation has been declining, and unit labor costs are near zero growth thanks to rising productivity. The center of gravity: participants see growing INPUT pressures (wages, health costs) but acknowledge these have NOT yet translated to final price inflation. Several (Parry, Meyer, Stern, Broaddus, Ferguson) worry inflation will rise, while others (Greenspan, Rivlin, Minehan, Guynn) emphasize productivity offsets. Direction: inflation pressure is perceived as somewhat below the midpoint in REALIZED terms but with rising risks. The discussion focuses on potential future inflation rather than current inflation. Current measured inflation is low and declining. Score should reflect this tension -- modestly below 50 given current low inflation, but pulled upward by widespread concern about future pressures. I'll score around 43-45.

**OUTPUT_GAP**: Nearly universal agreement the economy is growing "well above potential." Broaddus: "economy currently appears to be growing at a rate well above the staff's estimate of potential longer-term growth of 2-1/2 percent." Greenspan: "extremely tight labor market." Moskow: "above-potential growth this quarter and out into 1998." Kelley: "continuing to grow very robustly, quite likely well beyond...sustainable growth." Parry: "the unemployment rate has fallen even further below most estimates of the natural rate." This is clear above-potential language from nearly every participant. Score must be > 50 per bright-line rule. Given language like "well above potential," "overheating risk," I'll place this at 58-62.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS**: This is the dominant theme. Every single regional report describes extreme labor shortages. Hoenig: "labor shortages, especially in low-paying, repetitive-type jobs." Jordan: entry-level tellers at $14-15/hr, $20/hr for teller trainees, banks getting zero newspaper applicants, using hiring bounties. Broaddus: "just about everybody who is willing and able to work is already working." Moskow: unemployment below 4% since May, salary increases of 1/3 for computer staff. McTeer: "hiring problems are now widespread." Greenspan: "extremely tight labor market" with declining pool of those wanting work. This is very tight -- multiple speakers describe conditions approaching historical extremes for the 1990s. Score should be 65-72. The anchor for 1999-2000 is 65-75, and this is getting close to that peak. I'll score 68.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT**: Strong across the board. Jordan: booming real estate, strong loan demand all categories. Moskow: retail sales improved, auto competition "brutal" but demand strong. McTeer: 4.5% employment growth in Texas. Guynn: investment spending "remained stronger than I expected." Broaddus: 3.5% GDP growth in Q3, Q4 starting strong. Multiple reports of very strong commercial and residential construction, booming tourism. Business investment very strong (capital deepening to replace scarce labor). This is clearly above trend. Score 60-65.

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS**: Multiple participants describe financial conditions as loose/accommodative despite recent Asian turbulence. Meyer: "financial conditions that are most important in underpinning real economic activity continue to be highly supportive, indeed more supportive than at the beginning of the year." Broaddus: market expectation that Fed is on hold has prevented long rates from rising, creating "de facto easing." Minehan: "very accommodative capital markets." Jordan: banks aggressively pursuing loans, 125% LTV mortgages. Hoenig: banks actively seeking borrowers. Boehne: "competition to make loans is intense...terms are very liberal." Despite Asian turbulence, domestic financial conditions are clearly loose. Score: 25-30 (loose conditions = low financial tightness).

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN**: The committee holds steady (no rate change) with asymmetric directive toward tightening. However, the key nuance: many participants explicitly state they WOULD have tightened today absent Asian turmoil. Meyer: "a 1/4 percentage point increase...would have been justified today." Broaddus: "I strongly favor a modest tightening move today" (he essentially dissents in spirit). Parry: Taylor Rule suggests 50bp tightening; willing to wait due to markets. Moskow: "were it not for the financial volatility...we would be much more likely to act." Stern: "I would want to get started with a little more restraint today" in normal circumstances. Jordan warns about speculative excesses. Only Rivlin provides clear dovish counterweight. Greenspan himself maintains asymmetric toward tightening. The lean is clearly hawkish -- they are restraining themselves from tightening only due to external events. Score: 58-63.

**DISSENT_INTENSITY**: Broaddus explicitly advocates for a rate hike today, which is a clear divergence from the consensus hold. Meyer also states a hike was justified but accepts waiting. Most others accept holding but with hawkish reservations. Rivlin is on the more dovish end, questioning whether tightening is needed. Jordan raises asset price concerns as a different framework. However, the vote appears unanimous for "B" asymmetric. The disagreement is moderate -- clear differences in urgency and analytical framework but convergence on immediate action. Score: 35-40.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS**: Significant discussion of Asian crisis, stock market volatility, and financial conditions. Jordan discusses speculative real estate lending, 1920s parallels, asset price bubbles. Minehan discusses mutual fund industry reaction to market correction. Broaddus discusses long-term rates as automatic stabilizers being short-circuited. Guynn discusses Brazil/Argentina contagion risks. Boehne discusses REIT behavior and liberal lending. Multiple participants discuss financial market turbulence as the key factor preventing policy action. This is a prominent topic but not dominant over the economic assessment. Score: 50-55.

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE**: The asymmetric directive toward tightening signals that the next move is more likely to be a hike. Multiple participants explicitly say they expect to need to tighten soon -- just not today. Meyer: "I would be concerned if we did not respond over the longer term to increases in utilization rates." Greenspan: "whatever we believe we may have to do in December or later." The forward signal is clearly toward tightening, moderated by data dependence and external uncertainty. Score: 62-67.

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED**: Multiple participants emphasize elevated uncertainty. McDonough: "the degree of uncertainty has increased." Rivlin discusses productivity uncertainty extensively. Greenspan: "I do not think we can tell" which is moving faster, productivity or wages. Minehan: unable with current forecasting methods to project both GDP and inflation. Asian crisis adds "important element of downside risk." Several discuss the puzzle of strong growth with low inflation as inherently uncertain. This is notably elevated uncertainty. Score: 50-55.

### Cross-Dimension Coherence Check
- OUTPUT_GAP (60) and LABOR_TIGHTNESS (68): Both above 50, labor tighter than output gap -- consistent with an economy running hot, with labor constraints being the most binding factor. Coherent.
- HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN (60) and FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE (65): Both lean hawkish, with forward tone slightly more hawkish since multiple participants expect to need to act soon. Coherent.
- FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS (27) is low while OUTPUT_GAP is high -- this makes sense: loose financial conditions are supporting above-potential growth.

### Polarity Verification
- INFLATION_PRESSURE: Higher = more pressure. Evidence says current inflation low but input pressures building. Score 44 reflects below-midpoint realized inflation pulled up by input concerns. Matches.
- OUTPUT_GAP: Higher = more above potential. Evidence says "well above potential." Score 60. Matches.
- FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS: Higher = tighter. Evidence says "highly supportive," "accommodative." Score 27. Matches (low score = loose).

## Part 2: Evidence and Scores

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE
**Score: 44 +/-8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- MR. HOENIG: "a core rate of inflation that continues to decline, the continued absence of pipeline inflation, persisting expectations of stable inflation"
- MR. BROADDUS: "Despite this evidence of strong demand and tight labor markets, the reports we are getting on final prices are still generally mixed...goods prices at both the wholesale and retail level generally have been flat recently."
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "unit labor cost increases for nonfinancial corporations are at an annual rate of something less than 1 percent" and "I still see it as rather modest and unit labor costs being held in check to a significant extent by what appears to be accelerating growth in productivity."
- MR. MEYER: "the unemployment rate has fallen even further below most estimates of the natural rate. This leads me to expect higher inflation."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Participants acknowledge current measured inflation is low and declining, with unit labor costs well contained by productivity growth. However, extensive input pressures (tight labor, rising wages/benefits, bottlenecks) create concern that inflation risks are building. The score reflects the tension: current inflation is benign (pulling below 50) but input pressures are clearly building (pulling toward 50).

**Minority views:** Jordan's directors see "decidedly upside" inflation risks and a "buildup of inflationary pressures." Rivlin/Greenspan are more optimistic about productivity offsetting wage growth. This spread widens the uncertainty band.

---

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP
**Score: 60 +/-5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- MR. BROADDUS: "the economy currently appears to be growing at a rate well above the staff's estimate of potential longer-term growth of 2-1/2 percent"
- MR. MOSKOW: "our forecast sees above-potential growth this quarter and out into 1998"
- MR. KELLEY: "the economy is continuing to grow very robustly, quite likely well beyond what even an optimist like me would like to think of as sustainable growth"
- MR. PARRY: "the unemployment rate has fallen even further below most estimates of the natural rate"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Near-universal agreement that the economy is operating well above potential, with 3.5% GDP growth versus roughly 2.5% estimated potential. Factory utilization rates described as above noninflationary levels. Consistent with late-1990s boom anchor of 57-63.

**Minority views:** Some (Minehan, Rivlin) suggest productivity may be higher than estimated, which would imply a smaller positive output gap. Narrows the effective range.

---

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 68 +/-5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- MR. JORDAN: "One reported $20 an hour for a teller trainee...banks that advertised in newspapers for employees got no applications at all."
- MR. BROADDUS: "just about everybody who is willing and able to work is already working"
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "we are confronted with an extremely tight labor market" with declining numbers of people outside the labor force who want work
- MR. MOSKOW: "The unemployment rate in District states has been below 4 percent since May...salary increases of one-third for [computer] skills are not uncommon"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Every participant describes labor markets as very tight. Reports of hiring bounties, inability to find workers at any advertised wage, rising compensation across sectors, and substitution of capital for labor due to worker scarcity. Unemployment well below NAIRU estimates. This is among the tightest labor markets discussed in the 1990s expansion.

**Minority views:** None meaningfully dissent on labor market tightness -- disagreement is only about whether this will translate to inflation.

---

### 4. CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT
**Score: 62 +/-5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- MR. JORDAN: "Bankers reported strong loan demand in all categories, with most getting stronger...real estate lending...was booming everywhere"
- MR. BROADDUS: "growth rate in that quarter...would have been even stronger had it not been for a slackening in inventory accumulation. And it appears that the fourth quarter is starting off on a strong note"
- MR. GUYNN: "Investment spending has remained stronger than I expected"
- MR. MOSKOW: Strong trucking demand, steel demand strong, retail sales improving

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Consumer spending is robust, business investment is very strong (driven partly by capital-for-labor substitution), commercial and residential construction booming in many districts. Auto competition is "brutal" suggesting strong demand met with competitive pricing. 3.5% GDP growth in Q3. Above trend but not unsustainably so in participants' view.

**Minority views:** Guynn notes "cautious optimism" replacing "unguarded optimism" among retailers. Some expect Asian crisis to moderate demand. Modest downside uncertainty.

---

### 5. FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 27 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- MR. MEYER: "financial conditions that are most important in underpinning real economic activity continue to be highly supportive, indeed more supportive than at the beginning of the year"
- MR. BROADDUS: "the recent stock market volatility...is being widely viewed in the markets as precluding any Fed policy tightening...one might make the case that the recent market volatility has caused a de facto easing of monetary policy"
- MR. BOEHNE: "Commercial loan demand is also very strong, but the competition to make loans is intense...bank margins are thin and the terms are very liberal"
- MS. MINEHAN: "very accommodative capital markets"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Despite Asian financial turbulence, domestic financial conditions are described as highly accommodative. Real long-term rates have declined, credit is freely available on liberal terms, banks are competing aggressively for borrowers, equity prices remain elevated. Multiple participants flag these loose conditions as a concern supporting eventual tightening.

**Minority views:** Some participants note the Asian crisis has introduced financial market volatility, but this has not yet translated to tighter domestic credit conditions. Ferguson notes "heightened reaction to any policy move" suggesting some fragility.

---

### 6. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN
**Score: 60 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "I still think that with the asymmetry of pressures in the labor markets, we have no real choice about maintaining an asymmetric directive toward tightening"
- MR. MEYER: "In the absence of developments in Southeast Asia...I believe a 1/4 percentage point increase in the funds rate would have been justified today"
- MR. BROADDUS: "I strongly favor a modest tightening move today"
- MR. PARRY: "Our version of the estimated Taylor Rule suggests that tightening policy by roughly 50 basis points by next quarter would be appropriate"
- MS. RIVLIN: "I am less sure than the Greenbook seems to be that we will need to raise interest rates in the near term"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** The committee holds rates steady with an asymmetric directive toward tightening, but this is clearly a reluctant hold driven by Asian financial turbulence rather than comfort with the current stance. Multiple participants (Meyer, Broaddus, Parry, Moskow, Stern) state they would have preferred tightening absent external events. Only Rivlin provides meaningful dovish pushback. The lean is hawkish -- hold with strong tightening bias.

**Minority views:** Broaddus explicitly advocates tightening today. Rivlin questions the need for tightening at all given productivity. This spread is notable.

---

### 7. DISSENT_INTENSITY
**Score: 38 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- MR. BROADDUS: "I strongly favor a modest tightening move today" -- clear disagreement with hold decision
- MR. MEYER: "a 1/4 percentage point increase...would have been justified" -- soft dissent
- MS. RIVLIN: Questions need for tightening, worries about Greenbook scenario speech -- dovish outlier
- MR. JORDAN: Raises fundamentally different framework (asset prices, 1920s parallels) -- framework disagreement

**Center-of-gravity summary:** No formal dissent in the vote (unanimous for "B" asymmetric), but notable spread in views. Broaddus essentially advocates immediate tightening. Several others express they would have tightened absent Asia. Rivlin is dovish. Jordan raises asset bubble concerns as a different analytical lens. Moderate disagreement with convergence on the immediate decision but divergence on underlying assessment and appropriate timing.

---

### 8. FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS
**Score: 52 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- MR. JORDAN: "The problem may not be in output markets but in asset markets...In those episodes [1920s, Japan 1980s], inflation was never apparent in consumer prices"
- MS. MINEHAN: Detailed discussion of mutual fund industry reaction to stock market correction, liquidity testing
- MR. BROADDUS: "the recent stock market volatility...has caused a de facto easing of monetary policy" -- concern about financial conditions enabling overheating
- MR. BOEHNE: "lenders, if reminded, remember the lessons of the late 1980s and early 1990s, but that influence on their current decisions is weakening"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Financial stability receives substantial attention. The Asian crisis and stock market volatility are discussed by nearly all participants as the key factor affecting today's policy decision. Jordan raises systemic asset bubble concerns drawing parallels to the 1920s and Japan. Boehne and Jordan describe deteriorating lending standards (125% LTV mortgages, speculative construction lending). Minehan reports on stress-testing of mutual funds. This is more than routine but not the dominant topic over economic assessment.

---

### 9. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE
**Score: 65 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "Whatever we believe we may have to do in December or later, I think it is quite appropriate for us to stand pat today"
- MR. MEYER: "I would be concerned if we did not respond over the longer term to increases in utilization rates"
- MR. MOSKOW: "were it not for the financial volatility stemming from events in Southeast Asia, I think we would be much more likely to act today"
- MR. STERN: "if we were in more normal circumstances...I think I would want to get started with a little more restraint today"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** The forward signal is clearly toward tightening. The asymmetric directive explicitly biases toward a hike. Multiple participants frame today's hold as temporary forbearance driven by external events, implying a move at the next meeting (December) or shortly after is likely if domestic conditions persist. Only Asian uncertainty and financial market sensitivity prevent stronger forward tightening signal.

**Minority views:** Rivlin and Minehan suggest the case for tightening is not as clear, introducing some data-dependence into the forward outlook.

---

### 10. UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED
**Score: 52 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- VICE CHAIRMAN MCDONOUGH: "the degree of uncertainty has increased"
- MS. RIVLIN: "the increasing interconnections of global economies and capital markets...have added greatly to the uncertainty about our own economic future"
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "I do not know what the actual productivity data will turn out to be. I don't think the staff can predict this; I don't think we can; I don't think anybody on the outside can"
- MS. MINEHAN: "we also have found that we are unable with our current forecasting methods to project both the strength of the GDP and the quiescence of inflation"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Uncertainty is elevated on two fronts: (1) the productivity/inflation puzzle -- standard models cannot explain the coexistence of strong growth and low inflation, creating fundamental model uncertainty; (2) the Asian financial crisis introduces unknown spillover risks. McDonough explicitly says uncertainty has increased. Multiple participants frame the situation as genuinely hard to forecast. However, there is high confidence in the current state (strong growth, tight labor) -- uncertainty is about the outlook and transmission mechanisms.
