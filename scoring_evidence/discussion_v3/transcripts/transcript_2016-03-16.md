# FOMC Transcript Scoring — March 15–16, 2016

---

## Part 1: Thinking Process

### 1. Document Type
This is a **transcript** from the March 15–16, 2016 FOMC meeting (two-day session). It is fully segmented: speakers are clearly identified by name (MR. WILLIAMS, MS. BRAINARD, MR. BULLARD, MR. EVANS, MR. FISCHER, MR. HARKER, MR. KAPLAN, MR. KASHKARI, MR. LACKER, MR. LOCKHART, MS. GEORGE, MS. MESTER, MR. POWELL, MR. ROSENGREN, MR. TARULLO, VICE CHAIRMAN DUDLEY, CHAIR YELLEN). The document covers both the economic go-round (Day 1) and the policy go-round (Day 2). Approximately 17 named participants provide substantive remarks. No unsegmented or staff-attributed content is scored.

### 2. Era Context
March 2016 is the post-liftoff phase of the current hiking cycle. The FOMC had raised rates 25 bp in December 2015 (first hike since 2006). Between December and March, there was significant financial market turbulence (equity selloff, widening credit spreads, oil price weakness, China fears) peaking around February 11, followed by a recovery. By the meeting date, markets had largely reversed; the S&P 500 and oil had recovered, spreads had partially tightened, and the dollar had depreciated ~2%. Real U.S. data (labor market, consumption) remained solid; inflation had moved modestly upward (core PCE 1.7% January). The Committee ultimately held rates steady (alternative B), with one dissent (Lacker favored a hike). Global uncertainty — China, Europe, Japan, commodity prices, Brexit — dominated risk discussions.

### 3. Core Dimension — Directional Pre-Assessment

**INFLATION_PRESSURE:**
Participants see inflation as below target but showing some recent upward movement. Core PCE is at 1.7% for January (Fischer, Williams, Mester), headline PCE at 1.3%. Multiple participants note the January reading may be partly transitory (Yellen, Dudley, Evans). However, the trend is described as moving higher, not lower. The majority see inflation returning to 2% by 2017–2018 on the modal path. Key disinflationary concerns: oil pass-through, dollar appreciation, weak global inflation environment, and possible drift down in inflation expectations. Several participants (Bullard, Evans, Tarullo, Brainard, Kashkari) express worry about inflation expectations softening. Mester and George take a more sanguine view of expectations.

Direction: Below target, approaching (not at) target. Score should be in the 35–42 range typical for this era (ongoing below-target inflation). The January uptick is noted but not viewed by most as a regime shift. Score: ~38.

**OUTPUT_GAP:**
Most participants describe growth as "slightly above trend" or "modestly above potential." Williams: "above-trend GDP growth this year," "labor market overshooting full employment." Rosengren: "we are quite likely to exceed my estimate of full employment." Mester: "growth slightly above trend." Powell: "growth continuing at around 2 percent, modestly above potential." Lacker: "solid growth." George: "output gap that is basically closed." Several note Q4 2015 GDP was weak (~1%) with Q1 2016 rebounding toward 2%.

Direction: Slightly above potential per the majority. BRIGHT-LINE: "above potential" language → must score above 50. But the margin is modest: "modestly above potential," not "running hot." Score: ~53–55.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS:**
Near-universal agreement that labor market is at or near full employment. Williams: "labor market overshooting full employment." Fischer: "We are close to the maximum sustainable level of employment." Mester: "the economy is basically at our goal of full employment." George: "economy is near full employment" (unemployment 4.9%). Lacker: "unemployment rate is at or below most estimates of the natural rate." Some participants (Brainard, Tarullo, Yellen, Kashkari) note remaining slack: elevated PTER (part-time for economic reasons), rising participation suggesting more supply, modest wage growth. But the center of gravity is firmly at/near full employment.

Direction: Near NAIRU, leaning tight. Score: ~52–54 (at-to-slightly-above full employment, not deeply tight).

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT:**
Consumer spending is described as solid but not spectacular. Williams: "labor income on the rise," upside risks to consumer spending and housing. Rosengren: "consumption-led economy." Mester: "consumer spending has held up, buoyed by gains in the labor market, low energy prices." Lockhart: "consumer spending remains at a relatively solid 2.7 percent annual growth rate." Yellen: "household spending continues to be a relative bright spot," consumer spending growing at 2.5% annualized estimated.

However, business fixed investment is consistently flagged as weak: Lockhart ("the one element that stands out as weaker is business fixed investment"), Lacker ("Business fixed investment has been soft"), Yellen ("spending and production indicators for other sectors of the economy have been weak. Orders and shipments of nondefense capital goods have been essentially flat"). Net exports also a drag.

Direction: Mixed — consumer spending at or modestly above trend, business investment below trend, net exports a drag. Net: slightly below to at trend, leaning toward at-trend given the consumer emphasis. Score: ~46–48.

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS:**
A central theme of the meeting. Financial conditions were described as having tightened sharply Jan–Feb (Evans estimated GZ spread widening equivalent to ~75 bp tightening; Brainard: "substantial financial volatility and tightening"), then easing since Feb 11. By meeting date, Mester: "financial condition indexes eased in February and March, reflecting the increase in stock prices, narrowing of credit spreads, and depreciation of the dollar... on balance, financial conditions are only a bit tighter than anticipated." Brainard: "financial conditions have eased somewhat and now look less adverse." Powell: "the tightening in financial conditions has been unwinding now for over a month, and conditions are now about where they were in December."

Direction: Net slightly tighter than December, but substantially recovered from the January–February peak. Credit spreads (BBB) up ~30 bp from December (Yellen). Corporate spreads still elevated especially in energy. Overall: somewhat tighter than "neutral" but not severely restrictive. Score: ~53–57.

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN:**
The vast majority support alternative B (hold). Only Lacker formally favors alternative C (hike 25 bp). George supports the language of B but states she favors a 25 bp hike ("I support the language in alternative B for paragraphs 1 and 2, but I do not agree with its policy prescription"). Williams and Mester support B but lean toward early follow-up and flag risk of falling behind the curve. Bullard supports B but with reservations about inflation expectations. Fischer supports B and mentions April as a possibility.

The most dovish voices (Brainard, Evans, Tarullo, Kashkari) favor caution and emphasize downside risks. Dudley supports B but is skeptical of April, sees June as the likely next move. Rosengren: "three times this year" SEP assumption, open to April if data cooperate.

Center of gravity: Hold today, lean toward gradual normalization. The overall stance is a cautious hold with mild forward tilt toward further hikes but data-dependent and more gradual than December SEP implied. Range 40–50 (lean dovish to balanced). Score: ~45 — majority supports hold at this meeting, with a mild forward tightening bias but risk-management dovish tilt dominating.

**DISSENT_INTENSITY:**
One formal dissent (Lacker favors alternative C). George also stated she disagreed with the policy prescription but voted for B (not a formal dissent). This creates a notable minority camp (Lacker, George, and Mester/Williams who are clearly leaning hawk). The dovish side (Brainard, Evans, Tarullo, Kashkari) and the middle majority (Yellen, Dudley, Powell, Rosengren, Fischer, Lockhart, Kaplan, Bullard, Harker) are broadly aligned on hold.

There is substantive disagreement about forward path: Williams believes four hikes appropriate in modal scenario, while Brainard/Evans/Tarullo/Kashkari favor cautious 1–2 hikes. Bullard expresses unique concerns about inflation expectations and the SEP framework. There are notable differences about how to weight global risks.

Direction: One formal dissent + substantive disagreement about forward path and risk weighting. Score: ~40–45. The meeting has one clear dissent and a notable minority explicitly wanting to hike, which is more than a typical one-dissent meeting (25–35), but not as divided as multi-dissent meetings (50+). Score: ~43.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS:**
Financial markets, conditions, and stability receive extensive treatment throughout both days. Rosengren, Brainard, Evans, Kaplan, Powell, Dudley, Fischer, Tarullo all discuss financial conditions, spreads, equity markets, bank CDS, global central bank policy exhaustion, and negative rate risks at considerable length. Evans dedicates most of his remarks to the VAR analysis of spread widening. Brainard's entire go-round focuses on financial conditions and their endogeneity to policy. This is a prominent topic exceeding typical levels.

Direction: Prominent. Score: ~65–70.

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE:**
The Committee explicitly holds rates but the forward signal is for further gradual hikes contingent on data. Dudley: "I would be inclined to move again in the near future, presumably at the June meeting." Rosengren: open to April if data support. Mester: "I would like us to be in a position to move rates up at any meeting this year." Lockhart: open to April. Fischer: "not say anything that takes either April or June off the table." Chair Yellen: "I do expect that further gradual increases in our target range will be appropriate if the labor market continues to improve."

But the most dovish participants (Brainard, Evans) signal only 1–2 hikes for the year. The SEP median has moved down considerably from December (4 hikes implied → now 2 hikes implied for 2016 per several participants). Forward guidance leans toward "gradual tightening, data-dependent," in the mild tightening bias zone — but less hawkish than December.

Direction: Mild tightening bias with data-dependence. Score: ~58–62.

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED:**
Elevated but not extreme. Multiple participants stress uncertainty about the global outlook (China, Europe, Japan, Brexit, commodity prices), the path of inflation (including volatility in inflation expectations measures), the Q1 GDP reading (incomplete data), and whether the January core PCE uptick is durable. Fischer: "my uncertainty about global growth has increased." Yellen: "the global economy is more fragile than I'd previously thought." Tarullo: "the probability that the global economy will fall into recession this year" — not his base case but elevated.

Several participants express "somewhat elevated" uncertainty vs. the December meeting. The language is not at the extreme ("uncharted territory") but goes beyond boilerplate. China, ECB ammunition limits, Brexit, oil path — all cited by multiple speakers as sources of genuine uncertainty.

Direction: Elevated, corresponding to the 45–55 range (multiple participants emphasize unusual uncertainty, especially regarding global developments). Score: ~52.

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE

**Score: 38 ±6 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Fischer: "The core PCE price deflator for the 12 months ending in January of this year was 1.7 percent per annum... the median 2016 increase in the core PCE deflator projected in the SEP is 1.6 percent."
- Brainard: "core inflation has remained stuck in the vicinity of 1¼ to 1½ percent for the past three years... we can't entirely rule out the risk that this lengthy underperformance of inflation may be having some depressing effect on inflation expectations."
- Tarullo: "I'm still not at the point at which I can have reasonable confidence that inflation will return to 2 percent within a reasonable period."
- Williams: "The news on inflation, in my view, has generally been good. The January jump in the core PCE to 1.7 percent... I continue to expect to see inflation gradually move back to our 2 percent target by the middle of 2018."

**Center-of-gravity:** Participants see inflation as persistently below the 2% target — core PCE at 1.7% on one month's data vs. a trend of ~1.3–1.5% — with cautious optimism about eventual return to target but significant uncertainty about whether the January uptick is durable. Inflation expectations concerns add to the below-target picture.

**Minority views:** Mester and Harker are somewhat more sanguine about inflation trajectory; George emphasizes the broad-based nature of recent core price increases. These do not shift the center of gravity above 40.

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP

**Score: 53 ±5 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Powell: "I see growth continuing at around 2 percent, modestly above potential."
- Williams: "With above-trend GDP growth this year, my projection has the labor market overshooting full employment by a substantial margin."
- Rosengren: "we are quite likely to exceed my estimate of full employment over the forecast horizon."
- Yellen: "I continue to anticipate that growth will be moderate and that labor market conditions will improve further."
- Lacker: "the economy is on a modestly above-potential growth track."

**Center-of-gravity:** The clear majority characterizes growth as slightly above potential (~2% GDP vs. estimated 1.9–2.0% potential), with the output gap essentially closed or modestly positive. No participant argues the economy is meaningfully below potential.

**Minority views:** Evans notes "a little more slack in labor markets" relative to Tealbook, but still projects above-trend growth. Brainard emphasizes remaining labor slack (prime-age e/p ratio, PTER), which could mean the output gap is smaller than the majority implies.

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS

**Score: 53 ±5 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**

**Center-of-gravity:** The majority places the labor market at or slightly above full employment, with a subset (Brainard, Tarullo, Yellen, Kashkari) arguing some residual slack remains. Wage growth is still modest, preventing the score from moving higher.

Score: The center of gravity is concerned but not alarmed — most participants think expectations are "roughly anchored" but with downside risks. **EQUITY_STRESS:**

No participant describes current equity markets as severely undervalued or stressed at the time of the meeting. Score: markets noted as having recovered; concern about potential recurrence. **Score: 45** ±10 | Moderate confidence. (Minor stress concern from potential recurrence, not acute current distress.)

**BANKING_STRESS:**

Tarullo responds that U.S. bank equity declines reflected business model skepticism, not wholesale funding stress. Dudley notes negative rate risks to bank NIM. However, no one characterizes U.S. banks as in crisis or near failure; the concerns are moderate. **Score: 40** ±12 | Moderate confidence. (Some fragility concern, but well-capitalized; sound at the systemic level)

**TREASURY_STRESS:**
No specific discussion of Treasury market dysfunction. Flight-to-quality flows into Treasuries are mentioned (Lacker, Brainard), but this is normal behavior, not dysfunction. **Score: N/A.**

**DOLLAR_CONCERN:**

Yellen: "combination of dollar appreciation and slow foreign growth continues to hold down exports." Multiple participants note the dollar has depreciated ~2% since early winter, providing some relief. Score: concern about dollar strength is prominent but has partially eased; the residual concern is about potential re-appreciation if the FOMC signals too-hawkish a path. **Score: 65** ±8 | High confidence. (Moderate-to-high concern about dollar strength and its disinflationary/export-drag effects)

**PRODUCTIVITY:**
Williams devotes extended remarks to productivity — the measured slowdown is "genuine, not mismeasurement," sustainable growth rate is lower (below 2%), r* is lower. The slow productivity backdrop is discussed as a structural constraint. No participant argues for above-trend productivity. **Score: 32** ±8 | Moderate confidence. (Below historical trend; Williams and several others accept the secular slowdown as real)
