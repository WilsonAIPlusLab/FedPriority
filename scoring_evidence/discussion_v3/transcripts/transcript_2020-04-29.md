# FOMC Participant Discussion Scoring — April 28–29, 2020

---

## Part 1: Thinking Process

### 1. Document Type
This is a **transcript** of the April 28–29, 2020 FOMC meeting. Individual participants are identified by name (e.g., MR. CLARIDA., MS. BRAINARD., MR. KASHKARI.). The transcript includes two sessions: a financial-stability go-around (Day 1 afternoon), an economic go-around (Day 1 afternoon–evening), and a policy go-around (Day 2 morning). Staff content (Desk briefing, Tealbook) is present in framing references but participants' own assessments are clearly attributable by speaker labels.

### 2. Era Context
April 2020 is the acute phase of the COVID-19 shock. The economy is in a self-imposed shutdown; unemployment is expected to peak near 15–20%. The Fed cut 150bp to the ZLB in March 2020, launched QE, and introduced nine new 13(3) facilities. This is the meeting immediately after the emergency March 15 action. The macro backdrop is catastrophic near-term contraction with deep uncertainty about the recovery path.

### 3. Directional Assessment — Each Core Dimension

**INFLATION_PRESSURE:**
Participants see inflation as clearly BELOW target and trending lower, driven by weak demand dominating supply disruptions. Clarida: "I also fully agree with the assessment that, on net, the COVID-19 contagion shock is disinflationary, not inflationary… if anything, I think the projected core PCE inflation path in the Tealbook may be too optimistic." Mester: "I expect inflation to remain low for some time to come." George: "the combination of global and domestic disinflationary factors is likely to keep headline and core inflation persistently low." Brainard: "the disinflationary effects of lower energy prices, increased slack, and a stronger dollar likely dominating." Kashkari: "A lot of factors are pushing inflation further below target… It's really difficult to identify factors that are likely to lift inflation." Kaplan: "we face disinflation and even deflationary risks in the short run." This is multiple, reinforcing voices describing below-target, potentially deflationary conditions. Score must be well below 50. The anchor for 2009 deflation fears is 15-20; COVID April 2020 is analogous. Direction: below target / disinflation risk. Score: ~18.

**OUTPUT_GAP:**
Participants see the economy as CATASTROPHICALLY BELOW potential — GDP declining 30–40% annualized in Q2, unemployment at 15–20% and rising, output "far below potential." Clarida: "the plausible best-case scenario we confront features an output gap of at least 4 percent of GDP persisting into 2022." Brainard: "unemployment is expected to reach double-digit levels not seen since the Great Depression." Bullard: "unemployment already rising close to or beyond Great Depression levels." The anchor for COVID Q2 2020 is explicitly stated in the rubric as 15-22. This is one of the worst observations in the dataset. Score: ~12.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS:**
Participants see labor market as having CATASTROPHICALLY HIGH unemployment, analogous to Great Depression levels. Rosengren: "the unemployment rate is going to peak above 20 percent." Bowman: "The Board staff now anticipates that the unemployment rate will rise to around 20 percent." Brainard: "close to 25 million filed an initial claim for unemployment between mid-March and mid-April." Williams: "the full recovery will be measured in years." Rubric anchor for 2009/2020 Q2 is 15-22. Direction: far from full employment / depression-level unemployment. Score: ~12.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT:**
Participants describe consumption and investment as COLLAPSING. Brainard: "Total retail, trade, and food services sales plunged 8.7 percent in March, by far the largest monthly decline on record. Spending is down nearly 100 percent over the previous year at restaurants and movie theaters." Harker: "credit card charges fall more than 30 percent this month, with declines of nearly 90 percent in travel." Mester: "Consumer spending is down across almost every sector." George: "a broad-based and sharp contraction." Investment also collapsing: Mester: "More than half of District contacts reported they have delayed or canceled planned capital projects." Anchor 2008 Q4–2009 Q1 / 2020 Q2 is 15-20. Direction: collapsing. Score: ~12.

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS:**
Conditions are described as having IMPROVED from the peak stress of March but remaining elevated — still tight in some markets but significantly improved from crisis levels. Clarida: "financial conditions have eased, and considerably so in many markets." Mester: "Although volatility and risk spreads have not returned to prepandemic levels, there has been a significant lessening of stress in most markets." Williams: "equity markets have rallied, and conditions in funding and credit markets have improved markedly." But Brainard: "high-yield corporate bond market, spreads remain elevated" and corporate default risk elevated. Daly: "corporate default risk remains elevated." The conditions moved from the 85-95 range in March to a more moderate tightness. Spreads are still elevated above normal; credit is not frozen but is restrictive for lower-quality borrowers. Score: ~65 (tight but not crisis-level; financial conditions partially restored by Fed actions, but still clearly above normal).

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN:**
Near-universal support for holding at ZLB and maintaining/expanding QE. Every single participant supports "no change" to policy (holding at ZLB). Multiple participants explicitly advocate for MORE easing via: stronger forward guidance, yield curve control (Brainard, Mester, Daly), expanded asset purchases (Rosengren, Brainard), average inflation targeting. There is zero hawkish sentiment. This is QE-era dovishness with additional calls for further accommodation. Anchor: 2-5 for March 2020 emergency. This meeting is just past the emergency — participants are supportive of current stance and actively discussing further dovish tools. Score: ~8 (strong dovish; unanimous hold + active discussion of more accommodation tools).

**DISSENT_INTENSITY:**
There are zero formal dissents. Every participant explicitly supports the statement as written. There are some minor differences in views (Bullard is more optimistic about reopening; Kashkari is more pessimistic; George is skeptical of QE vs. forward guidance; Rosengren wants more). But these are differences in emphasis, not fundamental framework disagreements or dissents. The mood is broadly consensus. Anchor: 10-15 for Jan 2009 emergency; March 2020 emergency likely similar. This meeting shows a few reservations but near-unanimous agreement on the decision. Score: ~22 (broad agreement with some differences in forward-looking emphasis).

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS:**
Financial stability is a DOMINANT topic. The entire first session is a formal financial-stability go-around: Rosengren, Mester, Barkin, Brainard, George, Quarles, Kashkari, Williams all give lengthy financial-stability statements. Topics include: bank capital and dividends, nonbank vulnerabilities (money market funds, leveraged loans, prime MMFs, hedge funds), insolvency risk, CRE stress, high-yield spreads, banking system resilience, stress tests. The financial-stability session dominates Day 1 and bleeds into the economic go-around. Anchor: 80-95 for Sept 2008, March 2020. This meeting (April 2020) still has a very high emphasis. Score: ~82 (dominant topic; rivals economic assessment in time and depth; formal go-around plus continued references throughout).

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE:**
Forward guidance is strongly dovish. The current guidance pledges rates on hold "until the economy has weathered recent events" and is on track to achieve dual-mandate goals. Multiple participants advocate for even STRONGER and more specific forward guidance — outcome-based, tied to full employment AND 2% average inflation (Brainard, Kashkari, Daly, Brainard). Chair Powell: "markets and surveys show an appropriate expectation that we will patiently await recovery before considering lifting off again." This is a very strong easing commitment, with active discussion of making it even stronger via average inflation targeting and YCC. Anchor: 2-10 for 2020 emergency. Score: ~7 (strong easing commitment; no tightening signal; active discussion of strengthening the dovish commitment further).

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED:**
Uncertainty is expressed as EXTREMELY HIGH throughout the transcript. Clarida: "even more uncertain today than it was six weeks ago." Brainard: "the biggest factor in my forecast is uncertainty—uncertainty about the course of the pandemic, about foreign spillovers, about the depth and duration of the downturn…an unusually wide range seems plausible." Williams: "the paths of both the virus and the economy are too unclear to make confident predictions." Multiple participants note standard models are not equipped for this shock. Participants present multiple scenarios (V, U, W, L, Nike swoosh) and cannot identify which is most likely. Anchor: 85-95 for March 2009 trough / March 2020 emergency. This April 2020 meeting is also in the extreme range. Score: ~90 (most participants emphasize deep uncertainty; distribution of outcomes unknown; "uncharted territory" and "unprecedented" are used repeatedly).

## Part 2: Evidence and Scores

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE

**Score: 18 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Clarida: "I also fully agree with the assessment that, on net, the COVID-19 contagion shock is disinflationary, not inflationary… if anything, I think the projected core PCE inflation path in the Tealbook may be too optimistic."
- Kashkari: "A lot of factors are pushing inflation further below target, whether it's declining oil prices, the strong dollar, wage cuts, or slumping demand in many sectors. It's really difficult to identify factors that are likely to lift inflation."
- George: "the combination of global and domestic disinflationary factors is likely to keep headline and core inflation persistently low over the medium term."
- Brainard: "the disinflationary effects of lower energy prices, increased slack, and a stronger dollar likely dominating any inflationary pressures arising from disruptions to production."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Participants universally assess inflation as below the 2% target and trending further below due to demand collapse, oil prices, stronger dollar, and slack — no one perceives upward pressure.

**Minority views:** Kaplan raises a longer-run question about inflation risks once capacity is reached in "a few years," but this is speculative and explicitly framed as a medium-term concern, not a current assessment. Mester notes household surveys show households associate bad economic news with higher inflation, but dismisses this as inconsistent with Phillips-curve models. Neither shifts the near-term center of gravity.

---

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP

**Score: 12 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Clarida: "the plausible best-case scenario we confront features an output gap of at least 4 percent of GDP persisting into 2022… This downturn will almost certainly feature the steepest decline in U.S. real economic activity since World War II."
- Bullard: "We have unemployment already rising close to or beyond Great Depression levels. Most estimates are in the range of 15 to 20 percent right now."
- Brainard: "Factory output fell more than 6 percent in March, the largest one-month decline since the end of World War II."
- Williams: WEI "is now down minus 12 percent, meaning that GDP is at a level 12 percent below that of a year ago."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Participants describe a catastrophic contraction — GDP projected down 30-40% annualized in Q2, with the output gap persisting for years — placing this squarely in the 1-15 range of the rubric.

**Minority views:** Bullard is marginally more optimistic about recovery timing and argues the shutdown is artificial, but even he describes current output as collapsing. No participant argues output is near potential.

---

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS

**Score: 12 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Rosengren: "the unemployment rate is going to peak above 20 percent."
- Bowman: "The Board staff now anticipates that the unemployment rate will rise to around 20 percent over the next few months."
- Brainard: "close to 25 million filed an initial claim for unemployment between mid-March and mid-April, and unemployment is expected to reach double-digit levels not seen since the Great Depression."
- Clarida: "The unemployment rate will soon soar to double-digit levels last seen during the Great Depression… We project the unemployment rate to peak at 18 percent in May."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Participants uniformly expect unemployment to reach 15–20%+, explicitly compared to Great Depression levels — firmly in the 1-15 rubric range ("Depression-level unemployment; massive layoffs; hemorrhaging jobs").

**Minority views:** Bullard argues that the unemployment rate does not mean what it normally means (it reflects pandemic relief, not structural disemployment), but he does not dispute the raw numbers or argue the labor market is tight. This is a philosophical point, not a disagreement about current tightness.

---

### 4. CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT

**Score: 11 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Brainard: "Total retail, trade, and food services sales plunged 8.7 percent in March, by far the largest monthly decline on record. Spending is down nearly 100 percent over the previous year at restaurants and movie theaters."
- Harker: "credit card charges fall more than 30 percent this month, with declines of nearly 90 percent in travel and 60 percent in both fuel and entertainment."
- Mester: "Consumer spending is down across almost every sector… More than half of District contacts reported they have delayed or canceled planned capital projects."
- Brainard: "oil futures prices saw a record-breaking decline into negative territory."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Consumer spending has effectively halted in many categories; business investment is being canceled across the board — this is the "collapsing; freefall; spending/investment halted" description in the 1-15 range.

**Minority views:** Harker notes some pockets of resilience (debit card spending up 6% in the latest week, home improvement surging), but this is a narrow bright spot in an otherwise devastated picture. The net assessment remains near-zero spending across the economy.

---

### 5. FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS

**Score: 65 ±8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Clarida: "financial conditions have eased, and considerably so in many markets… this easing of financial conditions is the direct consequence of the actions that the Federal Reserve has taken."
- Mester: "Although volatility and risk spreads have not returned to prepandemic levels, there has been a significant lessening of stress in most markets."
- Brainard: "In the high-yield corporate bond market, spreads remain elevated, and secondary-market liquidity conditions remain in the upper end of their typical ranges. The leveraged loan market also shows signs of fragility."
- Daly: "corporate default risk remains elevated. The collapse in oil prices will pressure the highly leveraged oil sector, and the commercial real estate sector remains at risk."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Conditions have improved substantially from the March panic (bid-ask spreads normalized in Treasuries, equity markets have rallied) but remain tight — elevated credit spreads, still-restricted access for lower-quality borrowers, and emerging solvency concerns suggest conditions are in the 60-70 range, not back to normal.

**Minority views:** Williams notes some complacency risk — "today's relative calm in financial markets may prove short lived." This is a forward risk, not a current tightness reading.

---

### 6. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN

**Score: 8 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Powell: "I believe that our current policy stance is appropriate. Markets and surveys show an appropriate expectation that we will patiently await recovery before considering lifting off again."
- All participants: universal support for the statement maintaining ZLB and open-ended QE.
- Rosengren: explicitly advocates for "broader authority to directly purchase a wider set of assets" and argues "asset purchases could be quite significant."
- Brainard: advocates for "strong outcome-based forward guidance that conditions liftoff on sustained achievement of full employment and 2 percent average inflation" plus yield curve control.
- Daly: "we will need to rely on aggressive forward guidance as well as QE and other tools, like yield curve control."
- Kashkari: argues for "firmer commitment not to raise rates and preemptively cut off the recovery."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Unanimously dovish — ZLB held, QE ongoing, and the dominant discussion is about how to expand and deepen accommodation, not about any exit. This is squarely in the 1-10 range ("emergency dovish: immediate large-scale easing, QE launch").

**Minority views:** Barkin suggests beginning to plan for facility suspension when purposes are addressed; Quarles and Bullard raise concerns about moral hazard and path dependence. Neither advocates tightening.

### 7. DISSENT_INTENSITY

**Score: 22 ±8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Zero formal dissents. All participants state support for the statement as written.
- Minor differences include: Bullard (more optimistic on reopening, wants shutdown to become more granular); Kashkari (most pessimistic on recovery timeline, "12-to-18-month time frame"); George (skeptical of QE vs. forward guidance).
- Powell's summary: "a strong consensus supporting the statement as written."
- No sharp exchanges or fundamental framework disagreements on the decision itself.

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Near-consensus meeting with zero dissents. Some range in views on the forward path (Bullard vs. Kashkari on reopening optimism; George vs. Rosengren on tools), but these do not rise to the level of formal disagreement or distinct camps on today's decision.

**Minority views:** The widest range is between Bullard (argues pandemic risk is overstated vs. shutdown policy; questions the unemployment rate's meaning) and Kashkari (deepest pessimism, 12-18 month recovery). But both support the statement.

### 8. FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS

**Score: 82 ±8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Formal financial-stability go-around involves seven participants giving extended remarks: Rosengren, Mester, Barkin, Brainard, George, Quarles, Kashkari, Williams.
- Topics covered at length: bank capital adequacy and dividend policy, nonbank vulnerabilities (MMFs, leveraged loans, hedge funds, nonbank mortgage servicers), CECL provisioning, emerging insolvency wave, CRE stress, high-yield spreads, stress testing methodology.
- Brainard gives a multi-page, highly structured financial stability assessment; Quarles gives a detailed supervisory update; Mester discusses Financial Stability Subcommittee research agenda.
- Financial stability themes continue throughout the economic go-around (Daly, Williams, Evans all reference it).

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Financial stability is the first formal agenda item (go-around), receives the most structured attention of any topic, and multiple participants devote most of their Day 1 remarks to it — this is dominant-topic territory (80-90 range).

**Minority views:** N/A — all participants engage with financial stability to some degree.

### 9. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE

**Score: 7 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Current guidance: rates on hold "until we are confident the economy has weathered recent events" and is on track to achieve dual-mandate goals. This is already a strong dovish commitment.
- Brainard: advocates for "strong form of forward guidance whereby the Committee would condition liftoff on the achievement of full employment and 2 percent average inflation over a sustained period."
- Kashkari: "firmer commitment not to raise rates and preemptively cut off the recovery."
- Daly: wants to "return to the framework review discussion sooner rather than later" to put in place "aggressive forward guidance."
- Powell: "The time is coming when it will be appropriate for us to adjust and clarify our policy stance, when the path ahead is clearer." Market expectations: funds rate near zero through 2022.

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Forward guidance is already at the strong-easing end (explicit pledge not to raise rates), with the dominant discussion being about how to make it even more committedly dovish through outcome-based thresholds, average inflation targeting, and potential yield curve control — firmly in the 5-10 range.

**Minority views:** Clarida discusses conditions under which the forward guidance language might need to be refined, but frames this as a future-meeting question, not a tightening signal.

### 10. UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED

**Score: 90 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Clarida: "is even more uncertain today than it was six weeks ago."
- Brainard: "the biggest factor in my forecast is uncertainty… an unusually wide range seems plausible… a wave of insolvencies is possible."
- Williams: "the paths of both the virus and the economy are too unclear to make confident predictions."
- Mester: "the standard models are not equipped to deal with a self-imposed deep recession and unprecedented policy actions." Staff provided multiple scenarios because modal forecast is unreliable.
- Bostic: "this public health shock is catalyzing structural sectoral adjustments… a degree of uncertainty that will likely contribute to significant volatility."
- Rosengren: chose to forecast as "an average of the baseline and Second Waves scenario" because modal forecast is too uncertain to use alone.

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Virtually every participant stresses the unprecedented, extreme nature of uncertainty. Standard models are explicitly questioned. Multiple scenarios are treated as roughly equally likely. This matches the rubric's 76-90 ("most emphasize deep uncertainty; uncharted territory") and approaches 91-100 ("distribution of outcomes itself unknown").

**Minority views:** Bullard expresses more confidence about reopening dynamics (analogizes to historical pandemics), but even he acknowledges substantial downside risks and the unprecedented nature of the shutdown.

### Optional Dimensions

**INFLATION_EXPECTATIONS: 30 ±8 [Moderate confidence]**
Kashkari: "The financial market indicates that inflation expectations have fallen. The five-year, five-year-forward rate is down about 50 basis points since earlier in the year." Brainard advocates for average inflation framework specifically because inflation "will continue to fall well short of 2 percent." Multiple participants advocate for AIT to help re-anchor expectations upward. Expectations are below target but not severely unanchored — in the 25-35 range (anchored below target, not anchored well above or at target).

**CREDIT_STRESS: 75 ±10 [Moderate confidence]**
Brainard: "high-yield corporate bond market, spreads remain elevated… leveraged loan market also shows signs of fragility." Kaplan: "average credit spread for high-yield energy companies now is about double the overall high-yield index, about 1,600 basis points." Williams: "sky-high business debt, and now the hardest-hit sectors, such as energy and retail, are in dire straits, and numerous large-scale defaults and bankruptcies are sure to ensue." Credit stress is high in specific markets even as overall liquidity has been restored. Score: 75 (wide spreads, credit rationing in lower-quality segments, not yet a full freeze).

**BANKING_STRESS: 60 ±10 [Moderate confidence]**
Multiple participants discuss: CECL provisioning eating into capital, CET1 ratios dipping, stress tests showing capital near limits, banks providing forbearance under strain. Mester and Quarles both discuss at length. But banks are described as "still financially healthy" and "well capitalized" entering the crisis. Rosengren, Brainard, George, and Kashkari all advocate dividend suspension as a precautionary measure, suggesting banks are under pressure but not yet in distress. Score: 60 (emerging stress, capital buffers under strain, but not yet crisis-level impairment).

**EQUITY_STRESS: N/A**
Williams notes "equity markets have rallied" — participants don't discuss equity market stress meaningfully in their own assessments (they note improvement, not ongoing crash concern).

**TREASURY_STRESS: 45 ±10 [Low confidence]**
Barkin briefly: "the world's premier market for safe assets shouldn't be misfiring." Kashkari asks about bid-ask spreads. But the general message is that Treasury market dysfunction has PASSED — the Desk briefing confirmed normalization. Score: 45 (some residual concern but largely resolved; not enough participant commentary for high confidence).

**DOLLAR_CONCERN: N/A**
Brainard mentions "stronger dollar" as a disinflationary factor in passing, but no 2+ participants discuss dollar dynamics as a standalone concern.

**PRODUCTIVITY: N/A**
Barkin and Kaplan mention productivity concerns (new health protocols reducing throughput, redesigned supply chains less efficient), but this is mentioned briefly and as a medium-term scar rather than a focused discussion by 2+ participants.
