# FOMC Meeting Scoring — 1993-09-21

## Part 1: Thinking Process

**Document type**: Transcript. Individual speakers identified in a go-around format followed by a policy vote. Speakers include: Forrestal, Keehn, Parry, Broaddus, Hoenig, Boehne, Jordan, McTeer, Syron, Stern, Melzer, Lindsey, McDonough (Vice Chairman), Angell, Kelley, LaWare, Phillips, Mullins, and Chairman Greenspan. Approximately 19 participants.

**Era context**: September 1993. The economy is in a sluggish recovery from the 1990-91 recession. The fed funds rate is at 3 percent, which was historically low at the time. Unemployment remains elevated (~6.7%). Inflation is moderate (~3% CPI). Long-term rates have been declining. The recovery is characterized by "jobless recovery" frustrations and balance sheet repair ("headwinds").

### Core Dimension Reasoning

**INFLATION_PRESSURE**: Participants see inflation as below concerning levels and showing some disinflationary progress. Forrestal: "no upward pressure on wages"; Keehn: "very, very intense pricing environment" with "outright reductions" in purchase costs; Parry: more optimistic on prices, "greater improvement in CPI"; Boehne: "subdued inflationary pressures"; Phillips: "commodity prices have retreated; the PPI has been showing a disinflationary trend"; Mullins: PPI up only 0.7% over 12 months, core CPI last 6 months at 2.5%. Jordan is the outlier noting the Greenbook shows inflation "higher than we previously thought." Overall center of gravity: inflation is low and well-behaved, with some concern it hasn't come down further from ~3%. This places it in the "below target but not alarming" range. Higher = more pressure. Evidence says modest/low pressure. Score in 30-38 range.

**OUTPUT_GAP**: The economy is growing but below potential. Multiple participants describe "moderate growth" at roughly 2-2.5% pace. Greenspan: "we may be not all that far from potential here" at "maybe 2-1/2 percent." Boehne: "moderate growth... employment growth insufficient to cause a significant drop in unemployment." Melzer: "real growth around potential." Mullins: underlying final sales around 3%. The bright-line rule: some say "near potential" (Melzer, Greenspan hints), but unemployment at 6.7% and Mullins noting "ample projected slack" suggest still modestly below potential. Score 42-46.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS**: The labor market is clearly slack. Keehn: "no one I have talked to plans to add permanent employees"; Boehne: "employment growth sluggish"; Parry: California lost 588,000 jobs; Broaddus: "frustrated by the sluggishness in job growth"; Phillips: "continued concern about the employment situation"; Hoenig: "durable goods employment actually has declined." However, Stern reports "pretty tight labor market conditions in some of the major markets" and Mullins notes payrolls grew 1.7 million over 12 months with "pent-up labor demand" from strong hours. Greenspan notes "tight labor market with average hours of work moving up." Center of gravity: below full employment but improving; some pockets of tightness emerging. Score 35-40.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT**: Mixed picture. Forrestal: "retail sales quite disappointing" but auto sales good; Keehn: automotive "surprisingly good pace," steel strong; Broaddus: "consumer spending is looking increasingly solid," "private domestic final demand is firming up"; Mullins: "Consumption is projected to grow more than 3 percent; business fixed investment is continuing to grow." McDonough: business investment spending "likely to be right" driven by technology. Housing strengthening broadly. But Boehne: "weak commercial loan demand"; Jordan: retailing "disappointing." Center of gravity: moderate pace, at or slightly below trend, with pockets of strength. Score 44-48.

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS**: Financial conditions are clearly loose/accommodative. Mullins: "rates are still quite low now, both short and long rates... the financial system has eased... monetary policy is doing what it can do to support growth. We're still pumping out the liquidity. M1 growth is 12 percent." Broaddus: "much greater than expected declines in long rates"; Mullins: long bond yield down 50bp in last month; real long-term rates "quite low levels." Phillips: "banking system is stronger; the financial markets are stronger." However, small business credit complaints persist (Forrestal's advisory council: "lack of credit availability"). Overall, conditions are clearly accommodative. Higher = tighter. Evidence says loose. Score 22-28.

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN**: Unanimous "B symmetric" (no change, symmetric directive) vote. Greenspan: "I don't see any inclination... to move off where we are." Every single participant votes "B symmetric" without qualification except Angell who notes the need to "remain alert" to recognition lags. Jordan raises concerns about potential need for tightening if inflation forecast is correct but does not advocate action. Melzer worries about "very stimulative posture" but doesn't push for change. This is a hold-steady meeting with a mild undercurrent of future tightening awareness. Score 48-52 (balanced to very slight hawkish lean from the warnings).

**DISSENT_INTENSITY**: Unanimous vote. Very broad agreement on "B symmetric." The economic assessments were also quite convergent around "moderate growth, subdued inflation." Minor philosophical differences (Jordan on objectives, Melzer on stimulative stance) but no real policy disagreement. Score 12-18.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS**: McDonough raises significant concerns about equity market levels: "growing concern in the financial markets about the level of the DOW"; worry about mutual fund holders' reaction to a correction; concern about international financial linkages. Syron mentions "increasingly nervous fund managers." Phillips: "I do see it [stock market] as more problematic than the bond market." Angell mentions equity prices. This is more discussion than typical for 1993 but not dominant. Score 25-32.

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE**: Greenspan: "I personally don't see any particular reason why we should veer from that prior to the next meeting." Symmetric directive = no bias in either direction. Melzer warns "we may well be in a very stimulative posture and those chickens will come home to roost" but doesn't advocate action. Jordan raises inflation objective concerns. Mullins: "we still will likely face some challenges on monetary policy." The forward signal is essentially neutral/data-dependent with some vague future tightening consciousness. Score 48-52.

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED**: Kelley: "the thing that concerns me the most right now is that I don't know what I should be most concerned about"; "the world may have more than the usual potential" for unexpected events. McDonough worries about equity correction and international spillovers. Phillips: "less room for error in projections." Angell: the data "seem almost unbelievable... all of this gets to be pretty confusing." Greenspan questions the data: "something wrong with the numbers." Several participants express genuine uncertainty about data quality and the outlook. Score 42-50.

### Cross-dimension coherence check
- OUTPUT_GAP (44) and LABOR_TIGHTNESS (37): Both below 50, labor more slack than output gap — consistent with "jobless recovery" narrative where output is closer to potential than employment levels.
- HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN (50) and FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE (50): Both balanced — consistent with unanimous hold and symmetric directive.

### Polarity checks
- INFLATION_PRESSURE: Higher = more pressure. Evidence = subdued/low. Score ~35. Correct.
- OUTPUT_GAP: Higher = more above potential. Evidence = modestly below potential. Score ~44. Correct.
- FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS: Higher = tighter. Evidence = loose/accommodative. Score ~25. Correct.

## Part 2: Evidence and Scores

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE
**Score: 35 +/-7 [High confidence]**

Evidence:
- MR. KEEHN: "I continue to be impressed, really even surprised, by the intensive competitive conditions of the marketplace and the very restraining effect that is having on pricing. Many companies are achieving outright reductions in the cost of their outside purchases."
- MR. MULLINS: "the PPI has performed remarkably well; it's up only .7 percent over the entire 12 months... Core CPI for the year to date is up at a rate of 3.3 percent... the most recent 6 months with core CPI at 2.5 percent."
- MR. BOEHNE: "subdued inflationary pressures"

Center of gravity: Participants see inflation as well-behaved and trending lower, with CPI around 3% and recent core at 2.5%. Intense competitive pressures restraining prices. Below 2% target (in 1993 terms, ~3% was considered moderate-low). Score 35 reflects "below target; slack keeping prices in check; disinflation."

Minority views: Jordan notes the Greenbook shows inflation "higher than we previously thought" and questions whether the objective has changed. Mullins notes core CPI is still 3.3% and commodity prices ex-oil show "a bit of an upward pattern." These widen the band.

---

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP
**Score: 44 +/-6 [High confidence]**

Evidence:
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "we may be not all that far from potential here. I'd say the economy is moving [up at a rate] that has to be over 2 percent at this point, maybe 2-1/2 percent."
- MR. MELZER: "real growth around potential and reasonably well behaved prices is something I guess we ought to feel good about."
- MR. MULLINS: "the forces of moderate expansion seem well entrenched... the last four quarters have averaged 3 percent [real growth]."

Center of gravity: Economy growing moderately (~2-2.5%), near but still slightly below potential given 6.7% unemployment and slack referenced by several members. Greenspan says "not all that far from potential" which suggests approaching but not yet there.

Minority views: Some like Broaddus see stronger growth ahead; others like Phillips worry about fiscal drag keeping growth closer to 1%.

---

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 37 +/-8 [High confidence]**

Evidence:
- MR. KEEHN: "no one I have talked to plans to add permanent employees to their payroll"
- MR. BOEHNE: "employment growth sluggish... employment growth insufficient to cause a significant drop in unemployment"
- MR. STERN: "renewed reports of pretty tight labor market conditions in some of the major markets in the District... difficulty in hiring and retaining workers"
- MR. MULLINS: "payroll jobs... have grown by 1.7 million in the last 12 months... strong hours suggest pent-up labor demand"

Center of gravity: Labor market is still slack overall with 6.7% unemployment and reluctance to hire permanent workers, but there are scattered reports of tightness and improving claims data. Below full employment but moving in the right direction.

Minority views: Stern and Greenspan note pockets of tightness (hours, some regional markets). Parry highlights severe California weakness. Wide spread across regions.

---

### 4. CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT
**Score: 47 +/-7 [High confidence]**

Evidence:
- MR. BROADDUS: "consumer spending is looking increasingly solid... private domestic final demand is firming up noticeably"
- MR. KEEHN: "automotive business continues at a surprisingly good pace. The fourth-quarter production schedules have been set some 11 percent over last year"
- MR. MULLINS: "Consumption is projected to grow more than 3 percent; business fixed investment is continuing to grow... Housing growth is expected to pick up"
- MR. FORRESTAL: "Retail sales have also been quite disappointing... retailers now are reporting poor sales"

Center of gravity: Net spending picture is moderate — autos and housing strong, retail mixed to weak, business investment growing driven by technology. Roughly at trend. Score 47 = essentially at trend pace with slight softness in parts.

Minority views: Forrestal and Jordan see disappointing retail; Broaddus and Mullins see solid underlying demand.

---

### 5. FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 24 +/-6 [High confidence]**

Evidence:
- MR. MULLINS: "rates are still quite low now, both short and long rates. I think the financial system has eased and that monetary policy is doing what it can do to support growth. We're still pumping out the liquidity. M1 growth is 12 percent"
- MR. BROADDUS: "the most significant development by far since the last meeting is the further decline in long-term rates"
- MR. MULLINS: "the long bond yield has come down 50 basis points in the last month. Real long-term rates... are starting to get to quite low levels"

Center of gravity: Financial conditions are clearly loose/accommodative. Short rates at 3%, long rates falling substantially, M1 growing at 12%, banking system recovering. This is squarely in the "loose/accommodative" range.

Minority views: Small business still reports credit difficulties (Forrestal), and Boehne notes "weak commercial loan demand." But the dominant narrative is easy conditions.

---

### 6. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN
**Score: 50 +/-5 [High confidence]**

Evidence:
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "I don't see any inclination, at least on the part of anyone who has spoken up, to move off where we are, which at the moment is a symmetric and no change directive"
- Every participant votes "B symmetric" without exception
- MR. MELZER: "I just don't think we can gauge the current thrust of monetary policy very well, and I'm very uneasy about the fact that we may well be in a very stimulative posture" (mild hawkish concern but no action)
- MR. BOEHNE: "I don't believe there's very much that monetary policy can do to alter that outlook even if we wanted to"

Center of gravity: Pure hold with symmetric bias. No one advocates change in either direction. Some mild future tightening awareness from Melzer and Jordan but not enough to shift the current lean. Score 50 = balanced.

Minority views: Jordan and Melzer have mild hawkish leanings in philosophy but explicitly support the current stance.

---

### 7. DISSENT_INTENSITY
**Score: 15 +/-5 [High confidence]**

Evidence:
- Unanimous "B symmetric" vote from all participants
- MR. STERN: "Far be it for me to break the pattern: 'B' symmetric"
- MR. KELLEY: "a rather tight consensus with a couple of important exceptions"

Center of gravity: Near-consensus. The economic assessments converge around moderate growth and subdued inflation. Policy vote is unanimous. Minor philosophical differences between Jordan/Melzer (concerned about stimulative stance) and the mainstream but no actionable disagreement.

---

### 8. FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS
**Score: 28 +/-7 [Moderate confidence]**

Evidence:
- VICE CHAIRMAN MCDONOUGH: "growing concern in the financial markets about the level of the DOW... what the effect of anything other than a very modest correction could have on the holders of mutual funds"
- MR. SYRON: "increasingly nervous fund managers"
- MS. PHILLIPS: "when I look at the stock market... I do see it as more problematic than the bond market"

Center of gravity: McDonough devotes significant time to equity market concerns and mutual fund vulnerability. A few others mention it briefly. This is somewhat above the 1993 typical baseline (anchor: 1-10 for 1993-96 typical), reflecting the DOW/mutual fund concerns, but it is not a dominant topic — most participants focus entirely on the real economy.

Minority views: Most participants do not discuss financial stability at all.

---

### 9. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE
**Score: 50 +/-6 [Moderate confidence]**

Evidence:
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "I personally don't see any particular reason why we should veer from that prior to the next meeting, which is in mid-November"
- Symmetric directive = no directional bias
- MR. MULLINS: "we still will likely face some challenges on monetary policy, and we should try to stay focused and conscious during this period"
- MR. ANGELL: "the recognition lag may be longer than we would wish it to be. So I agree with you that we need to remain alert"

Center of gravity: Neutral forward signal. The symmetric directive by definition signals no lean. Greenspan explicitly sees no reason to move before the next meeting. Some general warnings about future challenges but no directional signal.

---

### 10. UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED
**Score: 45 +/-8 [High confidence]**

Evidence:
- MR. KELLEY: "the thing that concerns me the most right now is that I don't know what I should be most concerned about... the world may have more than the usual potential for [unanticipated events] right now"
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "there is something wrong with the numbers we are looking at. It's just not credible to me that we can have a significant rise in employment... and say that the GDP indicates productivity declined"
- MR. ANGELL: "the data seem almost unbelievable... all of this gets to be pretty confusing"
- MS. PHILLIPS: "less room for error in projections"

Center of gravity: Participants express genuinely elevated uncertainty, though not extreme. Data quality concerns (Greenspan, Angell), international risks (McDonough, Phillips, Kelley), and potential for unexpected shocks (Kelley) all contribute. This is above normal hedging but not "considerable uncertainty" territory.

## Optional Dimensions

### INFLATION_GOODS
**Score: 32 +/-8 [Moderate confidence]**

Evidence:
- MR. KEEHN: "Many companies are achieving outright reductions in the cost of their outside purchases... steel... aren't making any money"
- MR. MULLINS: "PPI... up only .7 percent over the entire 12 months"
- MR. FORRESTAL: "Prices of raw materials and finished products are steady"

Goods prices are subdued to declining, especially in manufactured goods with intense competitive pressures.

### INFLATION_SERVICES
N/A — Not specifically discussed as a sub-component by 2+ participants.

### INFLATION_HOUSING
N/A — Home prices discussed in some regions (Parry: rising in intermountain west; McTeer: downward pressure from new construction) but not as an inflation sub-component per se.

### INFLATION_EXPECTATIONS
**Score: 44 +/-8 [Low confidence]**

Evidence:
- MR. JORDAN: "today we still have the opposite [regime from 1953-63]... people believed that increased inflation and interest rates were temporary... whereas today we still have the opposite"
- MR. MULLINS: references Michigan long-term inflation expectations in context of real rates
- MR. BROADDUS: "raising the credibility of our efforts to achieve price stability over time"

Some discussion of expectations still being above what would be consistent with price stability, but progress being made. Slightly below the anchored-at-target midpoint.

### CREDIT_STRESS
N/A — Credit conditions discussed but not in terms of market stress/spreads.

### EQUITY_STRESS
**Score: 40 +/-10 [Moderate confidence]**

Evidence:
- VICE CHAIRMAN MCDONOUGH: "growing concern in the financial markets about the level of the DOW and of the equity markets in general... the sustainability of these levels of equity prices"
- MS. PHILLIPS: "I do see it [stock market] as more problematic than the bond market, although I'm not one who has visions of tulips"

This is a mild overvaluation/bubble concern — closer to the low end of the scale (1 = extreme overvaluation concern). McDonough expresses it most strongly. Score 40 reflects moderate concern about elevated valuations, tilted toward the overvaluation side.

### BANKING_STRESS
N/A — Banking discussed as recovering/improving, not in stress terms.

### TREASURY_STRESS
N/A — Treasury market not discussed in dysfunction terms.

### DOLLAR_CONCERN
N/A — Dollar briefly mentioned by Mullins ("the dollar has eased some") but not a point of concern by 2+ participants.

### PRODUCTIVITY
**Score: 55 +/-10 [Moderate confidence]**

Evidence:
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "the thought that we are having declining productivity just doesn't square with my understanding of the real world" — argues productivity is actually better than reported data suggest
- MR. KEEHN: companies "able to overcome [cost increases] through productivity improvements"
- MS. PHILLIPS: "we've seen a marked improvement [in productivity] so far in the '90s, but the question remains as to whether that's going to continue"

Mixed signals: official data show recent productivity decline, but Greenspan strongly argues the data are wrong and true productivity is positive. Keehn sees firm-level productivity gains. Score 55 reflects the center of gravity that productivity is modestly above historical trend when accounting for data skepticism, though with significant uncertainty.
