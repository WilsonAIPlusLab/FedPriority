# FOMC Transcript Scoring — April 27–28, 2010

---

## Part 1: Thinking Process

### 1. Document Type
This is a **transcript** of a two-day FOMC meeting (April 27–28, 2010). Individual speakers are clearly identified (e.g., "MR. ROSENGREN.", "MS. YELLEN.", "MR. HOENIG.", "MR. KOCHERLAKOTA.", etc.). The document is segmented into three sections: (1) a Day-1 go-around on balance-sheet exit-strategy options, (2) a Day-2 economic go-around, and (3) a Day-2 policy discussion. All three sections contain participant views and are scored here. Staff content (Greenbook/Bluebook framing) is noted but not scored.

### 2. Era Context
April 2010 is approximately ten months into the post-GFC recovery. The economy exited recession in mid-2009 but unemployment remains near 9.5–10 percent (still high historically). The Fed funds rate is at 0–0.25 percent with large-scale asset purchases (LSAP) completed. The "extended period" language is in place. A key new development on Day 2 is the Greek sovereign-debt crisis actively escalating (S&P downgrades Greece to junk, two-year Greek yields hit 25 percent, contagion feared). This informs the financial stability and uncertainty dimensions meaningfully.

### 3. Directional Assessment — Core Dimensions

**INFLATION_PRESSURE:**
Participants overwhelmingly describe inflation as **below target** and decelerating further. Multiple speakers (Yellen, Rosengren, Evans, Kohn, Pianalto, Dudley, Lockhart) report continued disinflation. Pianalto's median CPI is at a record low of 0.6 percent. Yellen expects core PCE "a little less than 1 percent this year." Kohn: "inflation well below my target." Rosengren: "continued disinflation." Bernanke's summary: "Inflation has generally been slowing and is expected to stay low." The minority upside voice (Plosser, Lacker, Kocherlakota citing 10-year-10-year forward breakevens near 3 percent) acknowledges near-term readings are low but flags medium-term upside risks. Direction: well below target. Anchor context: 2009–2010 is the 15–25 zone per rubric anchors. Near-term readings are especially soft. Score should be in the low 20s range.

**OUTPUT_GAP:**
Participants describe the economy as **in early-stage recovery, still well below potential**. Nearly all participants use language of slack, below potential, far from mandate. Yellen: "economy is operating far below maximum sustainable employment." Rosengren: "very far from attaining either element of our mandate." Kohn: "significant slack still remaining at the end of 2012." Bernanke's summary: "Considerable slack remains in the labor market." Plosser and Lacker are more optimistic (sustainable recovery under way, GDP growth 3.5 percent) but do not argue the gap is closed. Rubric bright-line: "slack remains" → score must be < 50. Recovery has begun from GFC trough; position is 2010 early-recovery = above 2009 trough, but still firmly below potential. Anchor: 35–42 for 2010–14 recovery period. Score around 36–38.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS:**
Unemployment is approximately 9.7 percent at this time (participants reference 9.25 percent end-year Yellen forecast, Tarullo discusses 15.6 percent dropout unemployment). Labor markets are described as "still very weak" (Rosengren), "very depressed" (Yellen), containing "a lot of slack in virtually every segment" (Tarullo), with firms "very cautious" about hiring. Bernanke: "Wage pressures remain low." Improvement signals are noted (temp hiring up, Caterpillar callbacks) but the level is clearly far from full employment. Rubric anchor: 2009/2020 trough = 15–22; 2011–14 = 35–40. April 2010 is early recovery but unemployment still near 9.5–10 percent. Score around 27–30.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT:**
Consumer spending surprised to the upside (Lacker: "favorable surprise on retail sales"; Evans: "recent strength in consumer spending"; Yellen: "consumer spending and business investment in equipment and software have rebounded more quickly than anticipated"). Business investment in equipment/software is solid. Chicago PMI 63.8, manufacturing improving. However, the sustainability is questioned by most: Kohn sees limited upside, Lockhart doubts consumer durability, Yellen warns of household debt overhang. Housing is weak (nonresidential construction "moribund"). Net position: spending is recovering, modestly above trend for the near term but below a robust/strong reading. Score in the low-to-mid 40s, leaning toward 43–45 (sluggish to at-trend rather than clearly above trend).

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS:**
Yellen explicitly states: "although overall financial conditions have improved and are no longer a sizable drag on the economy, they are also not an impetus for robust growth... overall, recent conditions have been only slightly positive." She notes loans "remain hard to get and expensive for businesses" — loan spreads ~50 bp above normal. Plosser: "Conditions in financial markets continue to support recovery... credit conditions are easing." Kohn: "improvement in financial conditions—higher stock prices, lower corporate bond yields." Bernanke: "Financial markets generally continue to improve... Financial conditions overall are close to neutral." The Greek situation adds a new tightening risk (Dudley: "some banks are having difficulty getting dollars"). Large firms have easy access; small/medium borrowers face tight bank lending. Net: conditions are roughly neutral to slightly tight, with bank credit remaining restrictive for less-creditworthy borrowers. Score: 52–55 (neutral to slightly tight).

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN:**
The clear majority favor **alternative B** (hold at 0–0.25 percent, maintain "extended period" language). Explicit B supporters: Yellen, Rosengren, Kohn, Evans, Lockhart, Pianalto, Tarullo, Warsh, Duke, Vice Chairman Dudley, Bernanke. The lone explicit C-preference is Hoenig (calling for rate hike to 1 percent "as early as this summer/September"). Plosser favors elements of C and wants rate hikes by second half 2010. Lacker also advocates rate hikes later this year. Bullard supports B with changes (including asset-sale language). The center of gravity is firmly on maintaining maximum accommodation with zero rates for an extended period, but with a notable hawkish minority. Rubric anchor: 15–25 for QE era 2010–12. The presence of the "extended period" debate, near-zero rates, and majority preference for no change puts this in the 20–30 range. The hawkish minority is real but clearly outnumbered. Score: 25.

**DISSENT_INTENSITY:**
There is one formal dissent (Hoenig). But beyond the formal dissent, there are notable strong reservations from Plosser, Lacker (both clearly advocating earlier tightening), and some complexity from Bullard and Kocherlakota. The majority-to-minority split is substantial and reflects genuine divergence about whether to hold vs. begin exit. The balance-sheet debate (day 1) shows wide dispersion (options 2 through 5 debated). However, there are no sharp personal exchanges or 3+ dissents. Rubric: 1 formal dissent plus 2–3 strong reservations = moderate-to-notable disagreement. Anchors: 25–35 = typical one-dissent meeting; 55–65 = 2010–11 QE2 debates. This meeting sits between the two: a clear majority vs. a vocal minority. Score: 45.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS:**
Financial stability receives **prominent and extended discussion**. The Greek crisis dominates Day 2 morning: Dudley gives a detailed 3-page assessment of Greek sovereign debt, contagion risks to Portugal/Spain/Italy, European banking stress, and potential dollar funding problems. Warsh gives a long assessment of nonlinear European risks and U.S. financial stability vulnerabilities. Rosengren calls for more structured financial stability discussions. Bernanke: "The Greek situation has revived concerns about sovereign debt." Additionally, Dudley flags potential bank rating downgrades from regulatory reform. Multiple other participants comment on financial conditions, bank lending tightness. Rubric: "Prominent topic; most participants discuss at length" = 65–75; anchors 60–75 for 2008 early crisis/2020 recovery. The Greek crisis discussion is extensive and a genuine focus of the meeting alongside the economic go-around. Score: 65.

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE:**
The overwhelming consensus is to retain the "extended period" language and signal no near-term rate increases. Yellen: "there is no case for tightening monetary policy soon." Kohn: "I don't see any risk to waiting." Evans: "quite some time" for accommodation. Bernanke endorses holding and emphasizes the economy is "still very early in the recovery process." The forward signal is clearly dovish — extended accommodation expected, no tightening expected in the near term by the majority. Hoenig/Plosser/Lacker represent minority forward-tightening signals. Center of gravity: "low for long" / mild easing bias with extended accommodation. Rubric: 16–25 = mild easing bias or "low for long" emphasis. Score: 18.

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED:**
Multiple participants note elevated uncertainty. The Greek crisis injects genuine uncertainty about tail outcomes. Evans: "tail uncertainty refuses to go away." Bullard: "unhappy ending there, which is looking increasingly likely." Warsh: "quite important for us to take quite seriously the prospects of nonlinear outcomes." Bernanke: "considerable uncertainties about the sustainability of final demand." Kohn: "The risks around this outlook are considerably smaller than they were several months ago." The uncertainty is real but has receded from its 2008–2009 extreme. The Greek situation raises it again as a new tail risk. Rubric: 46–60 = elevated: multiple participants emphasize unusual uncertainty. Score: 52.

### 5. Cross-Dimension Coherence

- **OUTPUT_GAP (37) and LABOR_TIGHTNESS (28)**: Both well below 50, consistent with early recovery from GFC. Labor somewhat lower than output gap because unemployment is still ~9.5–10 percent even as GDP is growing — consistent with a "jobless early recovery" narrative. No inconsistency; in fact this is the Okun's law puzzle several participants discuss.
- **HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN (25) and FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE (18)**: Both dovish, consistent. The forward guidance is slightly more dovish than the current lean, reflecting that the statement retains "extended period" which is a strong commitment. No divergence.
- **OUTPUT_GAP (37) and INFLATION_PRESSURE (22)**: Both below 50, consistent: high slack suppresses inflation. Coherent.

## Part 2: Evidence and Scores

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE
**Score: 22 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Pianalto: "The 12-month increase in the median CPI has fallen precipitously, to a record low of 0.6 percent in March—evidence of disinflationary forces at work."
- Yellen: "I expect core PCE prices to rise a little less than 1 percent this year... the very low recent readings on core measures of inflation are disconcerting."
- Rosengren: "My forecast also has continued disinflation, and many of the models used in my research department imply more disinflation than in the Greenbook. Wages and salaries continue to decline, and employers I have talked to report little pricing power."
- Bernanke summary: "Inflation has generally been slowing and is expected to stay low."

**Center of gravity:** Participants broadly describe inflation as well below target with ongoing disinflation, driven by substantial slack in labor and product markets; only a small minority flags medium-term upside risks via long-run inflation expectations.

**Minority views:** Plosser and Lacker express concern about medium-term upside inflation risk; Kocherlakota cites the 10-year-10-year forward breakeven rising to near 3 percent. These push the uncertainty band to ±5.

---

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP
**Score: 37 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Rosengren: "we are very far from attaining either element of our mandate... we will not achieve either element of our mandate over the period we usually forecast."
- Yellen: "The economy is operating far below maximum sustainable employment... the economy's substantial slack will continue to weigh on inflation."
- Kohn: "significant slack still remaining at the end of 2012."
- Bernanke summary: "Considerable slack remains in the labor market... a moderate and sustainable recovery is under way, albeit from a low base."

**Center of gravity:** Participants agree the economy is recovering but remains firmly below potential; recovery is described as moderate, from a low base, with a long climb back to full utilization expected to extend well beyond 2010.

**Minority views:** Plosser and Lacker are more upbeat (Plosser: "growing confidence that the recovery is on a sustainable path"; risks "tilted to the upside"), but they do not argue the output gap is closed. This adds ±5.

---

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 28 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Tarullo: "there is a lot of slack in virtually every segment of our labor market... We have barely begun to have net job growth."
- Yellen: "Labor markets also remain depressed, and the pace of recovery will not return the economy to full employment for several years. I expect the unemployment rate will end this year about 9¼ percent."
- Evans: "there's still a huge amount of slack in labor markets. Many firms advertising for workers have been inundated with responses."
- Bernanke summary: "Wage pressures remain low... employment plans are somewhat muted."

**Center of gravity:** Unemployment near 9.5–10 percent, wage pressures absent, hiring remains cautious — labor market is far from full employment but has begun showing early signs of stabilization (temp hiring, some callbacks).

**Minority views:** Hoenig and Lacker note improving labor indicators (temp hires up 40 percent, solid gain in private payrolls in March) but do not dispute the high level of unemployment. Narrow band ±5.

---

### 4. CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT
**Score: 43 ±7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Yellen: "Consumer spending and business investment in equipment and software have rebounded more quickly than I had anticipated."
- Kohn: "Stronger economic growth in 2010 reflected better-than-expected consumption and investment."
- Lockhart: "we take the view that the recent strength in consumer spending won't be sustained. We see a slower pace of reduction of unemployment weighing on household spending."
- Bernanke summary: "Retail sales, and household spending more generally, have recently picked up, despite sluggish income and wage growth... The sustainability of consumption spending is a key issue."

**Center of gravity:** Consumer spending and business equipment/software investment have surprised to the upside and are recovering, but most participants question sustainability due to high unemployment, debt overhang, and fading stimulus — placing conditions below trend in a structural sense even if recent readings are somewhat above.

**Minority views:** Warsh sees stronger near-term GDP momentum; Plosser and Lacker see risks tilted upside. Uncertainty band ±7 reflects genuine dispersion.

---

### 5. FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 53 ±7 [Moderate confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Yellen: "although overall financial conditions have improved and are no longer a sizable drag on the economy, they are also not an impetus for robust growth... Favorable conditions in money and bond markets are being offset by tight terms and conditions on bank loans."
- Bernanke summary: "Financial conditions overall are close to neutral... credit remains quite tight for bank-dependent borrowers such as small firms and consumers."
- Plosser: "Conditions in financial markets continue to support recovery at this point, as credit conditions are easing in the banking industry."
- Dudley (morning update): "some of the banks are having difficulty getting dollars at this point" (Greek contagion).

**Center of gravity:** Large firms have easy access to credit, bond spreads are normalized, equity markets are recovering — but bank credit remains tight for smaller/less-creditworthy borrowers, and the Greek crisis is adding new financial stress at the margin. Net: roughly neutral to very slightly tight.

**Minority views:** Plosser and Lacker are more positive about financial conditions improving. Greek-crisis risk pushes uncertainty band upward. ±7.

---

### 6. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN
**Score: 25 ±7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Yellen: "I favor alternative B. While it's good news that economic growth is picking up, we're climbing out of a very deep hole. The economy is operating far below maximum sustainable employment, and inflation is undesirably low... It does not make sense to tighten just because we are beginning to move in the right direction."
- Kohn: "I favor alternative B with the 'extended period' language... there are real threats of weakness and failure of the recovery and disinflation."
- Dudley: "it all points to alternative B and keeping the 'extended period' language in."
- Hoenig (minority): "I prefer alternative C, and I think we need to get rid of the 'extended period' language... I believe we should begin to consider raising the fed funds rate towards 1 percent as early as this summer."

**Center of gravity:** Approximately 11 of ~16 participants explicitly support alternative B (hold, maintain extended period). 3–4 participants advocate for hawkish alternatives or earlier tightening. The dominant lean is strongly dovish — maintain maximum accommodation at the ZLB.

**Minority views:** Hoenig (explicit C), Plosser and Lacker (rate hike second half 2010 view), Bullard (wants asset-sale language and state-contingency changes). This minority is vocal but outnumbered. ±7.

---

### 7. DISSENT_INTENSITY
**Score: 45 ±7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Hoenig (formal dissent signaled): "I prefer alternative C... I believe we should begin to consider raising the fed funds rate towards 1 percent as early as this summer."
- Plosser: "I believe economic growth risks, as I stated earlier, are slightly to the upside, and inflation risks are tilted to the upside over the medium to longer term... I think it is time for taking initial steps to normalize interest rates."
- Lacker: "the time to raise rates is likely to come far sooner than the Greenbook assumes. A time later this year seems quite likely, at least to me."
- Balance-sheet debate: day-1 discussion shows wide dispersion from option 1 (hold everything indefinitely) to option 5 (rapid aggressive sales), with no clear consensus on sequencing.

**Center of gravity:** One formal dissent (Hoenig) plus at least two participants (Plosser, Lacker) expressing strong reservations about the pace of accommodation. The day-1 balance-sheet discussion also reveals meaningful disagreement about the exit strategy timeline, though it is less acrimonious. The meeting stops short of the deep splits seen in later 2010–11 QE2 debates.

**Minority views:** The majority coalition is large and cohesive; dissent is from a clear minority. The disagreement concerns economics and timing, not tactics alone. ±7.

---

### 8. FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS
**Score: 65 ±8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Dudley (extensive report): "two-year Greek sovereign debt yields rose to 25 percent... S&P downgraded to junk status... contagion has already begun to Ireland and Portugal... U.S. banks could start to back off from European banks... LIBOR problem that we saw a year and a half ago could potentially resurface."
- Warsh: "I think the Greek situation is very serious, and it's quite important for us to take quite seriously the prospects of nonlinear outcomes both for Europe and for the U.S."
- Rosengren: called for "a more structured discussion of tails of the distribution" and systematic approach to contagion pathways and supervisory/monetary policy integration.
- Bernanke summary: "The Greek situation has revived concerns about sovereign debt and worsened prospects for Europe—and potentially, for the United States as well."

**Center of gravity:** Financial stability receives prominent attention in this meeting, primarily driven by the Greek/European sovereign-debt crisis escalating in real time on Day 2. Multiple participants devote substantial portions of their remarks to financial stability risks. It is a significant focus but does not fully dominate the meeting (economic go-around and policy discussion also receive substantial time).

**Minority views:** A few participants (e.g., Pianalto, Evans briefly) do not dwell on financial stability extensively. ±8.

---

### 9. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE
**Score: 18 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Yellen: "Because I see no case for tightening monetary policy soon, I support maintaining the 'extended period' language... The risks associated with tightening policy prematurely far exceed any risks from tightening policy too late."
- Rosengren: "This will require maintaining our accommodative policy for an extended period of time."
- Evans: "I'm comfortable with the 'extended period' language... 'quite some time'—or whatever language we use to express another expected six months."
- Bernanke (summary of Committee decision): "I would like to recommend alternative B... We would exclude entirely paragraph 4. There's no reason to make reference to it if we're not undertaking redemptions at this time."

**Center of gravity:** The Committee retains "extended period" language signaling no near-term rate increases. The forward signal is clearly a dovish "low for long" posture. Rubric anchor: 16–25 = mild easing bias or "low for long." Score 18 reflects that this is somewhat more than the mildest easing bias — the "extended period" is a meaningful commitment.

**Minority views:** Hoenig's preferred forward signal would be a rate hike to 1 percent by September 2010 (highly hawkish). Plosser and Lacker expect hikes later 2010. These minority views do not shift the center of gravity materially. ±5.

---

### 10. UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED
**Score: 52 ±8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Evans: "tail uncertainty refuses to go away... I am growing increasingly concerned about the international risks."
- Warsh: "I think we are prudent to be perhaps even a little more cautious... the prospects of nonlinear outcomes both for Europe and for the U.S."
- Bernanke: "there remain some uncertainties about the sustainability of final demand and improvements in the labor market."
- Yellen: "given all of the prevailing uncertainties, I am not that confident in this forecast and see both upside and downside risks."
- Kohn: "The risks around this outlook are considerably smaller than they were several months ago."

**Center of gravity:** The Greek crisis injects genuine tail-risk uncertainty that multiple participants acknowledge. However, compared to the extreme uncertainty of 2009 (85–95 range), this meeting is characterized by improved confidence in the recovery alongside a new but bounded source of tail risk. Several participants note risks are "broadly balanced" or "smaller than before." Uncertainty is elevated above normal but not "high" in the rubric sense.

**Minority views:** Kohn and Pianalto are more sanguine about uncertainty having declined. Warsh and Evans are more concerned. ±8.

---

### Optional Dimensions

**INFLATION_EXPECTATIONS: 48 ±6 [Moderate confidence]**

Evidence: Yellen: "inflation expectations remain well anchored, which should temper any further fall in inflation." Lockhart: "expectations seem to be well anchored for now." Kocherlakota: "The Board's measure of the 10-year, 10-year forward break-even inflation rate... is close to 3 percent. It has gone up by 65 basis points since September." Bullard: "I expect medium- and longer-run inflation expectations to continue to edge higher during the remainder of 2010."

Center of gravity: Near-term expectations are described as well anchored near target; the longer-run (10-year-10-year) measure has drifted up modestly. On balance, anchored at approximately target with a slight upward drift at the very long end. Score 48 (just below 50 anchored-at-target, reflecting the slight concern about long-run drift offsetting anchoring).

**CREDIT_STRESS: 60 ±8 [Moderate confidence]**

Evidence: Yellen: "loans remain hard to get and expensive for businesses... lending rate spreads over the fed funds rate were about 50 basis points above normal in the first quarter." Duke: extensive discussion of bank credit conditions across auto, card, C&I, and mortgage markets — all showing some stress/tightness. Evans: "bank lending to small- and medium-sized businesses continues to be weak."

Center of gravity: Spreads are above normal, bank credit is tight for smaller borrowers, but the stress is not crisis-level. Large firms have market access. Score 60 (somewhat elevated stress, above normal but not severe).

**BANKING_STRESS: 58 ±8 [Moderate confidence]**

Evidence: Duke: "smaller banks reported new unexpected problems popping up... Banks with large homebuilder portfolios will continue to fail." Lockhart: "The wave of small bank failures will persist through 2010." Dudley: potential bank rating downgrades from regulatory reform (Morgan Stanley, Goldman, BofA, Citigroup flagged). Rosengren: "bank lending continues to shrink significantly."

Center of gravity: Banking system is stabilizing for large institutions (reserve releases, profit return) but smaller banks remain under stress, particularly from commercial real estate. Not systemic risk but moderate ongoing stress. Score 58.

**PRODUCTIVITY: 55 ±8 [Low confidence]**

Evidence: Pianalto: "My outlook for productivity growth during the next few years is less sanguine than the Greenbook's... I expect a notable deceleration in productivity growth over the next few years." Lockhart: "We've assumed a little more productivity growth and a lot less job creation in comparison to the Greenbook." Tarullo: "unexpectedly high leap in productivity during the depths of the recession that probably will not be replicated."

Center of gravity: Productivity growth has been high recently (post-recession burst) but is expected to decelerate. Current level is above historical trend but declining. Score 55 (modestly above historical trend, with expected deceleration). Confidence is Low because only 2–3 participants discuss it, and it is secondary to the main discussion.
