# FOMC Transcript Scoring — April 30–May 1, 2019

---

## Part 1: Thinking Process

### 1. Document Type
This is a **transcript** from a two-day FOMC meeting (April 30–May 1, 2019). Speakers are individually identified. The document spans multiple sessions: (a) a balance-sheet maturity structure discussion (April 30 morning), (b) a Desk briefing with Q&A on reserve conditions, (c) a financial stability go-around, (d) an economics go-around, and (e) a policy go-around (May 1). The source file contains the full participant discussion extracted by the decomposition pipeline. I score only participant/member content.

### 2. Era Context
May 2019. The hiking cycle that ran from Dec 2015 through Dec 2018 (225 bps total) ended with the December 2018 hike. In January 2019 the Committee pivoted to a "patient" stance. By this meeting, that pause has been in place for two full meetings. GDP Q1 2019 came in at a stronger-than-expected 3.2% annualized, though underlying private demand slowed. Core PCE inflation printed at 1.6% year-over-year in March 2019, well below the 2% target. The labor market had unemployment at ~3.8%, well below estimates of NAIRU. Financial conditions had eased materially since the December 2018 tightening episode. The meeting is primarily about (1) whether to sustain patience on rates, and (2) a long-run balance-sheet maturity-structure policy discussion.

### 3. Core Dimension Directional Signals — Pre-Scoring

**INFLATION_PRESSURE:**
Nearly every participant discusses inflation as **below target**. Core PCE at 1.6% year-over-year is cited repeatedly. The Dallas Fed trimmed mean at ~2% is the outlier optimistic signal. Multiple participants (Evans, Brainard, Daly, Bullard, Williams, Clarida, Harker) express concern about a persistent undershoot. Michigan survey inflation expectations are described as "tied for the lowest ever recorded" (Clarida). The staff revised underlying inflation down to 1.8% permanently. Rosengren alone expresses more confidence the softness is temporary. Some participants invoke "transitory" factors for recent readings, but the rubric instructs to score the current perceived level, not the forecast. The center of gravity: participants perceive inflation as **meaningfully below target** — in the 26–40 range. The degree of concern is elevated but not deflationary-alarm territory. Score: low-to-mid 30s.

**OUTPUT_GAP:**
Q1 GDP printed 3.2%. Most participants characterize growth as **above trend or at trend**. Rosengren: "growth in the first half of the year, and 2019 as a whole, is likely to be above my estimate of potential growth." Brainard: "real GDP growth is comfortably above the potential growth rate." Williams: "above-trend growth." Clarida: trend ~2.2%, current 3.2% so slightly above. Barkin: "Real GDP seems likely to grow faster than trend." The labor market with unemployment at 3.8% is well below NAIRU estimates of 4.1–4.6%, implying a positive output gap. Bright-line rule: "above potential" language → score MUST be > 50. Score: mid-50s range, reflecting modest but clear above-potential assessment.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS:**
Extraordinarily consistent across all participants: the labor market is **very tight**. Unemployment at 3.8%, at 50-year lows for initial claims, widespread reports of firms unable to find qualified workers, firms lowering hiring standards, recruiting ex-offenders and from marginalized groups, wage growth at 3–3.2%. Mester: "unemployment rate… more than ½ percentage point below the Cleveland staff's estimate of its longer-run normal level." Multiple participants describe "tight labor markets" explicitly. Even the most dovish (Kashkari) acknowledges this but argues there's still slack by inference from subdued wage-price passthrough. Score: high 60s to low 70s — very tight, consistent with rubric anchor 65–75.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT:**
Consumer spending is characterized as having been weak in Q1 (1.2% PCE growth) but expected to rebound strongly in Q2. Business investment slowed considerably. The headline GDP strength was driven by inventories and net exports. Bowman: "step-down in growth in the categories of consumer spending and business investment." Brainard: "slowdown in gains in business fixed investment is likely to prove more persistent." Multiple participants see consumer spending as solid going forward but current Q1 reading as below-normal. Quarles: "worst showing in over two years" for equipment spending. Net assessment: spending at or slightly below normal trend currently, with expectation of rebound. Score: upper 40s, just below midpoint — slightly below-trend with recovery expected.

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS:**
Financial conditions are described as having **eased markedly** since December 2018. Brainard: "financial conditions have continued to ease since our March meeting and, depending on the measure, have now either largely or entirely reversed the deterioration in the fourth quarter." Bowman: "Financial markets have improved since the end of last year, overall borrowing conditions continue to be favorable." Quarles: "risk appetite still seems a touch below the levels that we hit last year." Evans: "investor demand is strong across asset classes, and banks in the District report ongoing competitive pressure on rates and terms." Williams: "stock market that's hitting record highs." The overall picture is accommodative/loose financial conditions. Score: upper 20s to low 30s — somewhat loose/accommodative.

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN:**
This is the most important dimension to calibrate carefully. The vote is unanimous for Alternative B (hold at 2.25–2.50%). The "patient" stance is explicitly the Committee's position. No participant argues for a hike; a few (Bullard, Evans) argue a rate cut should be "on the table" for future meetings. Bullard calls current policy "mildly restrictive" and says a cut "may be premature at this meeting, but perhaps not for long." Evans expresses concern about inflation complacency. The center of gravity is **hold with mild dovish lean** — nobody is hawkish, a few are leaning toward eventual easing. This places us in the 38–47 range: "lean dovish to balanced." The presence of explicit rate-cut discussion (even if premature) from 2-3 participants pulls it modestly dovish of balanced. Score: 42.

**DISSENT_INTENSITY:**
Unanimity on the vote (Alternative B). But there is notable variation in views: Bullard and Evans explicitly flag rate cuts as potentially appropriate soon, while Quarles says it's "premature to discuss cutting rates." Rosengren cautions against signaling easing. Most participants support hold; 2–3 express clearly dovish leanings. This is a meeting with broad consensus on the current action but measurable disagreement about the direction of future risks. No formal dissents. Score: 30–35 range — broad agreement with notable emphasis differences.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS:**
This dimension scores high. There is an entire dedicated go-around on financial stability. Multiple participants (Mester, Rosengren, Brainard, Barkin, Quarles, George, Williams) discuss financial stability at length. Topics include: leveraged loans/CLOs ($2T market), corporate debt at record highs, covenant-lite transactions, reach-for-yield behavior, CLO exposures of Japanese banks, countercyclical capital buffer considerations, commercial real estate valuations. Brainard delivers a lengthy remarks section. This is far above typical discussion — it rivals the economic discussion in emphasis. Score: high 60s to low 70s.

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE:**
The Committee retains the "patient" language in the statement. Powell and Williams flag that the forward guidance wording may evolve at the June meeting but commit to patience for now. No one signals hikes coming. Bullard explicitly says a rate cut "should be something we prepare for." The forward guidance is squarely in the **neutral to mildly dovish** camp — patience, no directional commitment. Rubric anchor for 2019 mid-cycle: 35–45. Score: 40 — neutral/easing lean data-dependent posture.

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED:**
Multiple dimensions of uncertainty are acknowledged: uncertainty about the inflation outlook (whether softness is transitory or persistent), uncertainty about r* and u* (star variables "hard to pin down"), uncertainty about trade policy, uncertainty about term premium effects on balance sheet. Williams: "uncharted waters" on some issues. Clarida acknowledges "I don't know the true model of the economy." Brainard: "it's hard to know with certainty how today's structures will fare in a period of prolonged stress." The level is elevated but not extreme — standard caveats plus genuine model uncertainty. Score: 48–52 — elevated but not dramatically so.

### 5. Cross-Dimension Coherence
- OUTPUT_GAP (55) and LABOR_TIGHTNESS (68): Both above 50, consistent with each other. The labor market is tighter than the output gap would suggest because the phillips curve is flat — this is noted extensively by participants. The divergence between tight labor and low inflation is the central puzzle of this meeting. Acceptable divergence explained by the flat Phillips curve discussion.
- HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN (42) and FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE (40): Both slightly below 50, consistent with each other. The current action is hold with a mild dovish lean, and forward guidance points the same direction. Coherent.
- INFLATION_PRESSURE (34) is low while OUTPUT_GAP (55) is above midpoint: This is the central puzzle discussed throughout the meeting — tight labor, above-trend growth, but inflation below target. Participants explicitly note this divergence and it explains the policy uncertainty. This divergence is correctly reflected in the scores.

Center of gravity: solidly "hold with patience," with a slight dovish tilt from Evans and Bullard. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN = 42.

## Part 2: Evidence and Scores

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE
**Score: 34 ±6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Clarida: "core PCE inflation has also softened to 1.6 percent in March... the Michigan survey has been fluctuating in a very low range, and, indeed, the most recent print is tied for the lowest ever recorded."
- Evans: "The news on inflation has not been good. The recent data on core inflation have softened considerably... I remain quite concerned that inflation expectations could stay stubbornly low relative to our objective."
- Brainard: "the staff threw in the towel on the prospects for further increases in underlying inflation and now assume that underlying inflation will remain flat at 1.8 percent instead of rising over the next few years."
- Bullard: "core PCE year-over-year—only 1.6 percent. Inflation expectations are still soft... This is mildly concerning to me. I think it could be used to justify a rate cut."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Participants collectively perceive inflation as meaningfully below the 2% target (1.6% core PCE, underlying at 1.8%), with the staff having marked down its inflation forecast and inflation expectations at or near historical lows; the dominant view is below-target and concerning, though most participants expect a gradual return to target and do not yet classify this as a deflationary threat.

**Minority views:** Kaplan and the Dallas Fed view (trimmed mean at ~2%) provide the optimistic anchor; some participants (Mester, Bostic) treat the softness as transitory. These views inform the ±6 uncertainty band.

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP
**Score: 54 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Rosengren: "my expectation is that growth in the first half of the year, and 2019 as a whole, is likely to be above my estimate of potential growth."
- Brainard: "real GDP growth is comfortably above the potential growth rate."
- Williams: "a very strong labor market, above-trend growth."
- Clarida: references unemployment at 3.8% "below my u* estimate of 4.1 percent" implying positive output gap, though modest.

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Participants see the economy as modestly above potential, with Q1 GDP at 3.2% above most trend estimates and the labor market well below NAIRU, though the gap is judged as small and possibly narrowing.

**Minority views:** Williams and Barkin note the headline Q1 strength was due to inventory/net export transients and that underlying private demand was softer; the ±5 band reflects disagreement about gap magnitude.

---

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 68 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Mester: "The unemployment rate has been 4 percent or less since March of last year... Labor force participation rates have held at an above-trend level... unemployment rate… more than ½ percentage point below the Cleveland staff's estimate of its longer-run normal level."
- Mester: "Firms are dealing in a number of ways with the continued difficulty in finding qualified workers. Some business contacts are limiting drug screenings... more are actively recruiting from the population of ex-offenders."
- Barkin: "new claims for unemployment insurance hit a 50-year low."
- Rosengren: "labor markets remain very tight, and I expect labor costs to continue to drift higher."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Near-unanimous participant assessment that the labor market is very tight — unemployment at 3.8%, well below most NAIRU estimates, with pervasive anecdotal reports of worker shortages and firms finding increasingly creative methods to recruit; this is consistent with rubric anchor range 65–75 for this era.

**Minority views:** Kashkari argues there is still meaningful slack inferred from the absence of wage-price passthrough — but even he does not dispute the low unemployment numbers, only their interpretation. This view informs the ±5 band.

---

### 4. CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT
**Score: 47 ±6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Bowman: "there was a step-down in growth in the categories of consumer spending and business investment. Consumption growth dropped to 1.2 percent in the first quarter after registering a 2½ percent pace in the fourth quarter of last year."
- Quarles: "spending on equipment about flat in the quarter—that's the worst showing in over two years... PCE was held down by the sharpest decline in spending on durables since immediately after the crisis."
- Brainard: "the slowdown in gains in business fixed investment is likely to prove more persistent."
- Most participants: expect consumer spending to rebound in Q2 to above-trend; business investment outlook mixed.

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Q1 private demand slowed below trend (weak PCE growth and equipment investment), but most participants expect a recovery — the score reflects a current below-normal reading with significant expected improvement, placing net assessment just below midpoint.

**Minority views:** Rosengren, Brainard, Daly view the Q1 weakness as temporary; Quarles and Bowman are more cautious about the persistence of investment slowdown. The ±6 band captures this uncertainty.

---

### 5. FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 30 ±6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Brainard: "financial conditions have continued to ease since our March meeting and, depending on the measure, have now either largely or entirely reversed the deterioration in the fourth quarter of this year."
- Quarles: "financial conditions have continued to improve since the turbulence we saw late last year. A number of asset prices have returned to their previous levels."
- Evans: "investor demand is strong across asset classes, and banks in the District report ongoing competitive pressure on rates and terms in CRE and C&I."
- Williams: "a stock market that's hitting record highs."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Financial conditions are described as loose/accommodative — having fully or nearly fully reversed the December 2018 tightening, with competitive credit markets, easing spreads, and rebounding equity markets — placing conditions in the rubric's "somewhat loose to neutral" range, toward the looser end.

**Minority views:** Some participants note residual global fragility and elevated corporate leverage as partial offsets to ease; the ±6 captures this asymmetry.

---

### 6. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN
**Score: 42 ±6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- All 15+ voting participants: support Alternative B (hold at 2.25–2.50%). Zero support for rate hike.
- Bullard: "current policy setting is mildly restrictive... a slightly lower setting for the policy rate... I support alt-B for today, but I may want to shift to something like alternative A, especially if inflation and inflation expectations news continues to drag."
- Evans: "I think there's more to look at, however. Namely, the news on inflation has broken the wrong way... every little bit that we help out with in light of these low-inflation data can help us in the future."
- Quarles: "I do think it's premature to discuss cutting rates, in view of the strong data on the labor market, GDP, and relatively easy financial conditions."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** The entire Committee votes to hold; the center of gravity is "patience/hold" — which by itself would be ~47–50, but the explicit dovish lean from Bullard and Evans (favoring eventual cuts) and the absence of any hawkish lean from any participant pulls the score to 42.

**Minority views:** Rosengren cautions against signaling easing; Quarles says "premature to discuss cutting." These voices prevent the score from dropping further toward 35.

---

### 7. DISSENT_INTENSITY
**Score: 28 ±6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Unanimous vote for Alternative B: zero formal dissents.
- Quarles: explicitly differentiates himself from rate-cut discussants: "I do think it's premature to discuss cutting rates."
- Rosengren: "I believe it is important that we not encourage expectations that we are likely to ease."
- Bullard and Evans: express distinct view that policy may be too tight and a cut should be prepared.

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Broad consensus on the current hold decision with zero formal dissents, but measurable disagreement about whether future rate cuts should be part of the Committee's toolkit — fitting the 16–30 range of "broad agreement with minor emphasis differences."

**Minority views:** The Evans/Bullard dovish lean represents a genuine minority view that is more than rhetorical. A handful of participants implicitly push back on this. The ±6 reflects that the disagreement is real but does not rise to formal dissent territory.

### 8. FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS
**Score: 68 ±6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Mester delivers extended remarks on financial stability tabletop exercise results, CCyB considerations, and stress-test design — spanning multiple paragraphs.
- Rosengren: "The memo shows that the quantity of leveraged loans and high-yield bonds is now more than $2 trillion. This is substantially larger than the subprime residential real estate market in 2007."
- Brainard: "Concerns about elevated valuations are amplified by vulnerabilities from business debt, which is close to the highest level experienced over the past 20 years and concentrated among the riskiest borrowers."
- Quarles: reviews FSB work on CLO exposures, bank stress test methodology for corporate debt shocks.
- Also: Williams, Barkin, George all contribute substantively to the financial stability go-around.

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Financial stability receives a dedicated and extensive go-around discussion with 6+ participants providing substantive, multi-paragraph contributions focused on leveraged lending, CLO markets, corporate debt vulnerabilities, and macroprudential tools — this places the topic at the "prominent" level of attention, consistent with rubric score 65–75.

### 9. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE
**Score: 40 ±6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Powell: "the incoming data strongly support our patient policy stance... I see no strong case for moving in either direction."
- Williams: "Communicating patience and data dependency has served us well... I see a flat path of the funds rate as appropriate through year-end."
- Clarida: "the language 'the Committee will be patient as it determines . . . future adjustments to the policy rate' should be retained, at least until such time as we are really ready to signal an imminent change in policy."
- Williams floats modified guidance language for June: "In light of expectations of moderate economic growth and muted inflation, the Committee will be patient..."

**Center-of-gravity summary:** The forward guidance is explicitly patient and neutral-to-dovish — "no strong case for moving in either direction" — with possible softening toward a more inflation-sensitive (hence more dovish) formulation at the June meeting; this places the tone in the rubric's "easing lean with data dependence" range (26–40), at the upper end.

### 10. UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED
**Score: 50 ±7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- Clarida: "I don't know the true model of the economy. In particular, I don't know either the slope or the intercept of the Phillips curve."
- Williams: "I would argue that the fact that arguments on both sides of the policy stance today have been brought up shows that we're probably roughly in the right place."
- Multiple participants: express uncertainty about whether inflation softness is transitory or persistent.
- Brainard: "it's hard to know with certainty how today's [CLO] structures will fare in a period of prolonged stress."
- Multiple: uncertainty about r* magnitude, u* level, trade policy resolution, European outlook.

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Participants express elevated uncertainty — particularly about inflation dynamics, the slope of the Phillips curve, r*, and whether current softness is transitory — but this is characterized as genuine analytical uncertainty rather than crisis-level "uncharted territory"; score 50 reflects genuinely elevated uncertainty about key parameters without extreme Knightian uncertainty.
