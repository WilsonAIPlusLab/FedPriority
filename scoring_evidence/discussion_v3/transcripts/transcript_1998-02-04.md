# FOMC Participant Discussion Scoring — 1998-02-04

## Part 1: Thinking Process

### Document Type
This is a **transcript** with individual speaker attributions. The meeting is a two-day Humphrey-Hawkins meeting (Feb 3-4, 1998). The first portion covers monetary aggregate ranges (largely procedural), and the second portion is the policy go-around. Speakers include: Chairman Greenspan, Vice Chairman McDonough, Governors Meyer, Rivlin, Kelley, Ferguson, Gramlich, Phillips (silent), and Presidents Broaddus, Parry, Boehne, Minehan, Hoenig, McTeer, Moskow, Stern, Guynn, Jordan.

### Era Context
February 1998: Late-1990s expansion. The Asian financial crisis is unfolding (began mid-1997). U.S. economy is growing strongly, labor markets are very tight, but inflation is low due to productivity gains and Asian disinflationary pressures. The fed funds rate is at 5.50%.

### Dimension-by-Dimension Reasoning

**INFLATION_PRESSURE**: Participants see inflation as currently very low/benign. Greenspan: "overall unit costs for all practical purposes are within shouting range of zero." CPI around 2% with ~1% upward bias, so true inflation near 1%. Broaddus says "we are very close to price stability right now." However, there is awareness of latent upside pressure from tight labor markets -- Greenspan notes "further pressures on wages" and tight labor markets "as tight as any time I can remember." The Asian crisis is providing additional disinflationary force (commodity prices declining across the board). The center of gravity: inflation is low, near price stability, but with recognized latent upside risk from labor tightness. Direction: below midpoint. Magnitude: "close to price stability" with unit costs near zero = clearly below target range. Score should be in the 30-38 range. Higher = more pressure; evidence says low actual pressure but latent risk. Score 35.

**OUTPUT_GAP**: The economy is clearly running above potential. Greenspan: "the economy gets down to the low levels of unemployment and extremely tight labor markets that we currently are seeing." Parry: "we do not see any signs whatsoever of a slowdown in the domestic expansion." Jordan: demand growth is strong, money growth rapid. Hoenig: "benefiting from a productivity supply shock." The Asian crisis is expected to slow things but hasn't yet. Bright-line rule: above-potential language throughout -> must be >50. The economy is running hot domestically, though the Asian tsunami is expected to moderate it. Score 58-62. I'll set 60.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS**: Extremely tight. Greenspan: "extremely tight labor markets," "as tight as any time I can remember, and that goes back a very long way." Continuing contraction in available workers. Parry: "such tight labor markets." Meyer: "tight labor markets and the continuing strength of demand." This is among the strongest language on tightness. Anchors suggest 65-75 for 1999-2000; Feb 1998 is very similar. Score 70.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT**: Strong. Parry: "we do not see any signs whatsoever of a slowdown in the domestic expansion." Greenspan: "a stark demand situation" if Asian effects are small. Jordan: "even stronger nominal demand growth coming into this year." No participant describes weakness in domestic spending. Above trend but not "unsustainably rapid." Score 62.

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS**: Financial conditions are loose/accommodative. Jordan: "the thrust of monetary policy in 1997 was more expansionary than I thought... ample availability of credit from the banking industry... relatively rapid growth of various measures of money, and the ongoing strength in various asset markets." Jordan's questions to Kohn note "a wide array of other financial indicators that do not suggest a restrictive monetary policy at all." Real short-term rates are high by one measure but every other indicator says loose. Direction: conditions are loose. Higher = tighter; loose = low score. Score 28.

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN**: The consensus is clearly "B" symmetric -- hold steady. The vote is unanimous for no change. However, the center of gravity leans mildly hawkish: Broaddus favors alternative "C" (tighten); Parry wants asymmetry toward tightening; Jordan says "odds are strongly" next move is up; Meyer says "the next move still is more likely to be up." Only Greenspan mentions the possibility of moving "in one direction or the other." No one argues for easing. The committee holds but with a hawkish lean. This is "hold steady with mild tightening bias" = 51-55. Score 53.

**DISSENT_INTENSITY**: Broaddus formally favors "C" (tightening). Parry wants asymmetric toward tightening. Jordan is hawkish. But the vote passes unanimously for "B" symmetric -- the hawks acquiesce. This is broad agreement with notable minority emphasis differences. 1-2 expressed preferences for tighter but no formal dissent. Score 28.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS**: The Asian crisis is discussed extensively, but primarily in terms of its trade/demand/inflation channel, not as a financial stability concern per se. There are references to asset markets (Jordan mentions financial industry, asset markets), capital flows (Greenspan), and exchange rates. Some attention to financial conditions but it's not the dominant frame -- it's discussed as an external demand shock. Moderate. Score 30.

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE**: Greenspan: "wait-and-see is clearly the appropriate policy for the short term" and "important to be sensitive to moving in one direction or the other if called for." The forward signal is data-dependent/neutral to mildly hawkish. Meyer: "the next move still is more likely to be up." Gramlich: "once these risks are clarified, we may have to act quite forcefully." No commitment to any direction. Neutral with mild tightening lean. Score 55.

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED**: Substantial uncertainty about the Asian crisis impact. Greenspan: "What we do not know is its eventual order of magnitude." "it will probably take a couple of months for us to get a much better fix." Minehan: "I wish I understood better how to do it" (re: forward-looking policy). Gramlich: "the risks are large on either side, but they are very different and they are very hard to figure out." Hoenig: wondering about both a supply shock wearing off and a demand shock arriving. This is elevated uncertainty. Score 55.

### Cross-Dimension Coherence Check
- OUTPUT_GAP (60) and LABOR_TIGHTNESS (70): Both above 50, coherent. Labor tightness is higher because that's where the most emphatic language is -- "tightest I can remember."
- HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN (53) and FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE (55): Both mildly hawkish-leaning, coherent. Forward guidance slightly higher because multiple participants signal next move is up.

### Polarity Verification
- INFLATION_PRESSURE: Higher = more pressure. Evidence: low actual inflation, latent risk. Score 35 = below midpoint. Correct.
- OUTPUT_GAP: Higher = more above potential. Evidence: above potential. Score 60. Correct.
- FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS: Higher = tighter. Evidence: loose conditions. Score 28. Correct.
- HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN: Higher = more hawkish. Evidence: hold with hawkish lean. Score 53. Correct.

## Part 2: Evidence and Scores

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE
**Score: 35 +/-8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "overall unit costs for all practical purposes are within shouting range of zero and have been that way for the last 12 to 18 months"
- MR. BROADDUS: "we are very close to price stability right now... the true underlying rate of inflation may now be at 1 percent or perhaps a little higher"
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "we are beginning to see" the deflationary push from Asia via commodity prices declining across the board; "a deflationary push from East Asia is going to affect the economy"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Participants perceive inflation as very low, near price stability, with unit costs near zero and additional disinflationary pressure arriving from Asia, though latent upside risk exists from tight labor markets.

**Minority views:** Jordan warns of "substantial acceleration of prices even if it is somewhat delayed," contributing to the upward uncertainty band.

---

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP
**Score: 60 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "the economy gets down to the low levels of unemployment and extremely tight labor markets that we currently are seeing" -- domestic demand running above trend
- MR. PARRY: "we do not see any signs whatsoever of a slowdown in the domestic expansion"
- MR. JORDAN: "we would have been looking at even stronger nominal demand growth coming into this year, fueled by rapid money growth"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** The domestic economy is clearly operating above potential with very strong demand, though the incoming Asian shock is expected to moderate this somewhat.

**Minority views:** Greenspan frames potential for the Asian tsunami to pull growth down, which could lower the gap, widening the uncertainty band downward.

---

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 70 +/-5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "extremely tight labor markets... pressures in these markets by any measure we look at anywhere in the country are as tight as any time I can remember, and that goes back a very long way"
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "further pressures on wages... increasingly tight labor markets with a continuing contraction in the number of individuals both in the labor force and out who would like to work but are not working"
- MR. PARRY: "such tight labor markets"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Labor markets are described in the strongest possible current terms -- historically tight by any measure, with ongoing contraction in available workers and upward wage pressure.

**Minority views:** Limited disagreement on the characterization; Greenspan notes the Asian shock may ease labor markets "fairly quickly" but this is prospective, not current.

---

### 4. CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT
**Score: 62 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- MR. PARRY: "we do not see any signs whatsoever of a slowdown in the domestic expansion"
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: if the Asian tsunami is small, "we are looking at a stark demand situation"
- MR. JORDAN: "even stronger nominal demand growth coming into this year, fueled by rapid money growth"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Domestic spending and investment are clearly above trend, with robust demand that shows no signs of slowing, though participants expect the Asian crisis to moderate this going forward.

**Minority views:** Some uncertainty about how much the Asian shock will reduce domestic demand.

---

### 5. FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 28 +/-8 [Moderate confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- MR. JORDAN: "a wide array of other financial indicators that do not suggest a restrictive monetary policy at all... the ample availability of credit from the banking industry... relatively rapid growth of various measures of money, and the ongoing strength in various asset markets"
- MR. JORDAN: "the thrust of monetary policy in 1997 was more expansionary than I thought at the time, and it became progressively more expansionary as we went through the year"
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN (via Kohn discussion): real short-term rates appear restrictive by one measure, but broader conditions are loose

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Financial conditions are perceived as accommodative -- ample credit availability, rapid money growth, and strong asset markets, despite nominally high real short-term rates.

**Minority views:** The real-rate measure suggests some tightness, creating a modest upward pull on the uncertainty band.

---

### 6. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN
**Score: 53 +/-8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "a position of wait-and-see is clearly the appropriate policy for the short term... I see very little choice but to stay where we are, namely 'B' symmetric"
- MR. BROADDUS: "I continue respectfully to think that the dominant risks are on the upside... I still favor alternative 'C'"
- MR. MEYER: "the next move still is more likely to be up... there is that upside risk"
- MR. PARRY: "asymmetry in the direction of higher rates is the proper position"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** The committee unanimously votes for no change with a symmetric directive, but the center of gravity tilts mildly hawkish -- multiple participants explicitly state the next move is more likely up, and two prefer tighter policy or asymmetry now.

**Minority views:** Broaddus (favors "C"), Parry (favors asymmetry toward tightening), and Jordan (expects rate hike needed) pull hawkish. No one argues for easing.

---

### 7. DISSENT_INTENSITY
**Score: 28 +/-6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- MR. BROADDUS: "I still favor alternative 'C'" -- but acquiesces to consensus
- MR. PARRY: prefers asymmetry toward tightening -- but votes for "B" symmetric
- Vote is unanimous for "B" symmetric

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Broad agreement on holding steady, with minor emphasis differences. Two participants prefer tighter policy or asymmetry but do not formally dissent. The vote is unanimous.

**Minority views:** The hawkish dissenters-in-spirit (Broaddus, Parry, Jordan) elevate this slightly above minimal disagreement.

---

### 8. FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS
**Score: 30 +/-8 [Moderate confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "a tsunami of the Asian crisis... rolling across the Pacific" -- discussed primarily as a trade/demand shock, with references to capital flows, exchange rates, and commodity prices
- MR. JORDAN: references to "various asset markets," "the financial industry, the banking industry, and various measures of money and credit growth"
- MR. FERGUSON: "We need time to observe the strength of the factors that will be exerting an impact on both sides of the economy"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Financial conditions and the Asian crisis receive moderate attention, but the discussion frames Asia primarily as a trade/demand channel rather than a financial stability threat to U.S. markets. Asset markets and credit conditions are mentioned by a few participants but not as a focal concern.

**Minority views:** None.

---

### 9. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE
**Score: 55 +/-8 [Moderate confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "important to be sensitive to moving in one direction or the other if called for"
- MR. MEYER: "the next move still is more likely to be up"
- MR. GRAMLICH: "once these risks are clarified, we may have to act quite forcefully"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** The forward signal is data-dependent with a mild tightening lean. No commitment to any direction, but multiple participants signal the next move is more likely to be a hike than a cut. The stance is patient but not easing-biased.

**Minority views:** The Asian crisis could shift the bias dovish; no participant explicitly signals easing may be needed.

---

### 10. UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED
**Score: 55 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence:**
- CHAIRMAN GREENSPAN: "What we do not know is its eventual order of magnitude... it will probably take a couple of months for us to get a much better fix"
- MR. GRAMLICH: "the risks are large on either side, but they are very different and they are very hard to figure out"
- MS. MINEHAN: "I wish I understood better how to do it" (regarding forward-looking policy in an uncertain environment)
- MR. HOENIG: "we are in a period when we are benefiting from a productivity supply shock and wondering if it is wearing off, and at the same time we are waiting for a demand shock from Asia and wondering how hard it is going to hit the economy"

**Center-of-gravity summary:** Participants express elevated uncertainty driven by the unknown magnitude of the Asian crisis impact, the sustainability of productivity gains, and the difficulty of being preemptive in this environment. Multiple participants emphasize they simply do not know.

**Minority views:** Some participants (Broaddus, Jordan) express more conviction about the upside risk direction, somewhat reducing the uncertainty from their perspective.
