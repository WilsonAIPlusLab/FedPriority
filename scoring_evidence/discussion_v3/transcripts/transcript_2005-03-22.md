### 2. Era Context
March 2005: Mid-cycle expansion. The Fed is in a measured-pace tightening cycle, having raised rates from 1% (June 2004) to an expected 2.75% at this meeting (+25bp). Growth is solid (~4% projected), labor market recovering, but not yet at full employment. Core PCE inflation has recently ticked up and is approaching or modestly above the Fed's informal target range. Oil prices are elevated. The "conundrum" (low long-term rates despite rate hikes) is actively discussed. The key debate is whether to drop/modify "measured pace" language and whether inflation risks are tilting upside.

### 3. Directional Assessment for Each Dimension

#### INFLATION_PRESSURE
**Direction**: Participants see inflation as **modestly above their comfort zone / at the upper edge of acceptable**, with risks clearly tilted upside. This is NOT extreme inflation; it is an elevated-but-manageable reading with meaningful upside concern.

Evidence across speakers:
- Moskow: "inflation risks may have increased," "recent price data… have a firmer tone," oil prices "continue to surprise us on the upside," TIPS inflation compensation moved up
- Hoenig (most hawkish): "core inflation, by whatever measure... drifted higher last year," "unexpectedly higher than earlier projections," sees inflation accelerating further; funds rate below neutral; explicitly says "we should aim to be comfortably within the range of neutral by the end of the year"
- Guynn: "both goods and services inflation are now showing signs of picking up," "surge in PCE and PPI data"
- Santomero: "risk to inflation has shifted to the upside," "relatively sharp acceleration in core consumer good prices," medium-term inflation expectations "shifting upward"
- Pianalto: pricing environment "very challenging," dam showing "cracks" with Wal-Mart now accepting supplier price increases
- Minehan: "core consumer prices are up a full percentage point on a year-over-year basis"
- Bies: "over the last few months, we have seen core inflation move modestly higher," unit labor costs now rising at 2.3%
- Poole: "I view the inflation forecast more as a median... the distribution is skewed to the right—substantially higher probability that we could have a ½-point upside surprise"
- Kohn: "price pressures have been a bit more intense than anticipated," risks of second-round effects elevated; but still sees restraining forces (productivity, high markups, flat wages)
- Lacker (more sanguine): expects "core PCE inflation to return to near 1½ percent after not too long," inflation expectations "creeping up" but "not yet a problem"
- Yellen: January core PCE "disappointing," raises core PCE forecast by "a tenth or two, to just over 1½ percent"
- Geithner: "somewhat greater price pressure," core PCE "a bit above 1½ percent but not above 2 percent"
- Ferguson: "long-term inflation expectations continue to be well anchored," incoming data "not flashing a signal that we are clearly behind the curve"

Center of gravity: Most participants see inflation above the low levels of 2003, with core PCE running around 1.5–1.7% and clearly trending upward. Language ranges from "firmer tone" to "upside risk" to "accelerating." The majority view is that inflation is somewhat elevated but not alarming, with significant upside risk. This maps to roughly 58–63.

**Score direction check**: Higher = more inflation pressure. Evidence says participants see MORE inflation pressure than before, modestly above comfort zone, upside skewed. Score > 50. Magnitude: "somewhat elevated," "firmer tone," "upside risk," not "alarming" or "well above target." Range: 55–65.

Mixed picture: skilled labor shortages in some sectors (District-level), but Kohn explicitly says "slack in labor markets persists." National unemployment below 5.5% but not at NAIRU. Some wage pressures emerging in specific categories. This maps to a tightening-but-not-tight market.

**Score direction check**: Higher = tighter labor market. Evidence: some tightness emerging (skilled workers), but aggregate slack persists (Kohn), unemployment still somewhat above NAIRU. Score: 48–55.

- Yellen: retain "measured pace" and "accommodative" language

The center of gravity: consensus for +25bp, some hawkish lean beyond that, nearly unanimous support for tightening bias going forward. The debate is about pace (stay measured vs. signal more flexibility/faster path). No one argues for a pause or cuts.

**Score direction check**: Higher = more hawkish. Evidence: unanimous tightening, some calling for even more aggressive path/flexibility. Strong consensus for continued hikes. Score: 68–75 (strong hawkish: consensus for continued tightening, discussions of possibly moving 50bp, preserving flexibility for faster path).
