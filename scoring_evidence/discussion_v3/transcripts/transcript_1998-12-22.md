# FOMC Meeting Scoring: 1998-12-22

## Part 1: Thinking Process

### Document Type
This is a **transcript** with individual speaker attributions. It covers the go-around (district reports and national outlook) and the policy discussion. Speakers include: Broaddus, Parry, Moskow, Poole, Minehan, Boehne, Guynn, Stern, McTeer, Jordan, Hoenig, McDonough, Kelley, Gramlich, Ferguson, Rivlin, Meyer, and Chairman Greenspan. This is a well-populated late-1990s meeting with roughly 18 participants.

### Era Context
December 1998: The U.S. is in the late stages of the 1990s expansion. The Asian financial crisis hit in 1997-98, Russia defaulted in August 1998, LTCM collapsed in September 1998, and the Fed cut rates three times (75bp total) in September-November 1998. By December, financial markets had stabilized and the stock market had rebounded sharply. GDP growth was surprisingly strong, labor markets were very tight (unemployment around 4%), and inflation was very low despite tight labor markets -- the "new economy" narrative. Brazil remained a risk.

### Dimension-by-Dimension Reasoning

**INFLATION_PRESSURE**: Participants see inflation as remarkably low and well-behaved despite extremely tight labor markets. Greenspan: "virtually nothing that suggests upward pressure on prices." Moskow: "price pressures remain benign." Parry: "expect inflation to hold steady...core CPI around 2-1/4 percent." Multiple participants note favorable factors (low commodity prices, strong productivity, subdued expectations). However, several participants flag upside inflation risks looking forward: Broaddus warns goods price deflation could end as Asia heals; Poole worries about rapid money growth; Jordan sees "brown spots" in health care and leisure prices; Meyer flags upside risks from wage-price Phillips curve divergence and dissipating favorable factors. The CENTER OF GRAVITY is: inflation is currently very low and benign, but a handful of participants see upside risks ahead. Direction: below the midpoint -- participants perceive muted inflation pressure currently. Score should be in the 35-42 range given "well-behaved," "benign," "subdued" language, but with some noting it could edge up. The fact that several see upside risks ahead keeps it from going lower. Score: 38.

**OUTPUT_GAP**: The economy is clearly growing above trend. Broaddus: "we may still be above the maximum path on which the economy can grow without an increase in inflation." Greenspan: "economy that is expanding at a fairly rapid pace." Parry: "surprisingly strong output growth." Stern: "more growth in aggregate demand than is indicated in the Greenbook." The Greenbook forecasts slowdown to trend but many participants note the economy has surprised to the upside repeatedly. Multiple participants describe the economy as having strong momentum. The bright-line rule applies: "above trend" language means score > 50. The economy is growing above potential but participants expect a slowdown to trend. Score: 57 (above potential, running somewhat hot, consistent with late 90s boom anchor of 57-63).

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS**: This is one of the most discussed topics. Nearly every district reporter describes very tight or extremely tight labor markets. Broaddus: "extraordinary tightness in labor markets" and "getting even tighter in the service sector." Guynn: "labor markets remain extremely tight." Jordan: "everybody talks about importing labor from someplace else." Stern: "very tight...clear signs of wage pressures, especially for entry-level jobs." Hoenig: "tight labor markets." Boehne: "labor markets tight." Minehan: "unemployment...about a percentage point lower than in the rest of the country." Greenspan: "labor markets that, if anything, are growing marginally tighter." Unemployment around 3.7-4%. This is very tight by historical standards. Direction: well above the midpoint. Anchor for 1999-2000 is 65-75. Score: 70.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT**: Consumer spending is described as very strong. Broaddus: "brisk holiday sales...broadly based." Jordan: "malls are packed...holiday retail sales will be excellent." Moskow: "consumer spending continues to be relatively healthy." Kelley: "retail sales are strong, particularly in the cyclically sensitive areas like housing and autos." Investment is strong but some note expected deceleration. Broaddus: "business investment in particular to decelerate." But Jordan: "motor vehicle industry is exceptionally strong." Construction is robust across many districts. On net, spending is above trend with strong momentum. Direction: above the midpoint. Score: 62 (above trend, "robust," "strong momentum" but with some expected moderation).

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS**: The financial crisis of fall 1998 (LTCM, Russia) had tightened conditions significantly, but by December conditions had eased considerably. Moskow: "heightened concerns about credit availability...have eased somewhat as a result of our actions." McDonough: "fixed income markets have not completely returned to normal but...will probably return to somewhat greater normalcy." Minehan: "Wall Street financing dried up during the late summer and fall market turmoil but other investors filled the gap, albeit at higher financing rates." Meyer: "the deterioration of financial conditions...now appears less so. The equity market correction has simply disappeared. While private risk spreads have widened...the absolute level of private borrowing costs...is about what it was at the end of last year." Greenspan: "we broke what was a dangerously eroding financial situation." Banks are lending aggressively (Hoenig: banks "competing very vigorously for loans"). Stern notes "generally accommodative credit conditions." Overall: conditions were tight but have normalized substantially; some residual tightness in spreads but equity markets recovered and credit is flowing. Direction: roughly neutral to slightly loose. Score: 42 (somewhat loose to neutral; conditions are generally supportive but some residual spread widening).

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN**: Unanimous support for no change in the funds rate ("B" symmetric). However, the tenor of the discussion has shifted notably toward concerns about upside risks. Greenspan: "our policy is back to balance and we should be looking to both the upside and the downside." Poole: "If it were not for the fact that a tightening today would be a very large shock to the market...I think there would be a case for tightening now." Jordan: "domestic considerations are going to require at some point that we start to raise the level of the overnight interbank nominal rate." Broaddus: "had you recommended an asymmetric directive toward tightening, I would have been perfectly happy." Hoenig: "I'd suggest that we consider not holding off too long on the unwinding of our recent easing actions." Meyer: "we are going to have to be very vigilant against potential upside risks to inflation." Guynn: "be as ready to move on the upside to tighten policy as we were very recently to ease." On the other side, no one calls for easing. Minehan's academic advisors: "almost no support for further near-term easing." The center of gravity is: hold steady, symmetric directive, but a meaningful contingent leans hawkish looking forward. Score: 52 (lean very slightly hawkish from balanced; hold steady but with a nascent tightening bias emerging among several participants).

**DISSENT_INTENSITY**: The vote is unanimous for no change with symmetric directive. However, there are notable differences in emphasis. Several participants (Poole, Jordan, Broaddus, Hoenig) clearly lean hawkish and flag upside risks; others (Rivlin, Gramlich) are more balanced or see downside risks as more prominent. Jordan explicitly "disagreed with the funds rate reduction in November." Broaddus says "with at least some sense of unease." But all agree on the current action. The directive language debate (options 2 vs 3, "in coming months," money sentence) shows substantial procedural disagreement but this is about communication, not substance. Score: 28 (broad agreement on policy action with minor emphasis differences; no formal dissents; some spread of views on forward outlook).

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS**: Financial conditions and markets are discussed extensively. The fall 1998 crisis (LTCM, credit spreads, equity volatility) is a prominent backdrop. Multiple participants discuss: equity market levels and sustainability (Jordan at length, Greenspan at length on wealth effects, Moskow on skepticism of earnings forecasts), stock market bubble concerns, financial market fragility, credit conditions, bank lending. Greenspan devotes a very long section to stock market valuation, wealth effects, and housing capital gains. Jordan: "perpetual great expectations" in equities, concern about pension allocations to equities, moral hazard belief that "Fed would ease policy to prop it back up." Poole discusses money growth flowing to asset prices. This is a meeting where financial stability is a prominent topic. Score: 55 (moderate to prominent discussion; several participants address it meaningfully, Greenspan devotes extended remarks).

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE**: The decision is symmetric ("B" symmetric), meaning no directional lean. However, several participants signal that the next move is more likely to be a tightening: Poole, Jordan, Broaddus, Hoenig, Meyer all suggest future tightening may be needed. No one suggests further easing is likely absent a shock. Greenspan: "we should be looking to both the upside and the downside." McDonough: "further easing actions should take place only if there is clear and new evidence of weakness." The forward signal is neutral to slightly hawkish. Score: 55 (neutral with mild tightening lean; "watchful waiting" but multiple participants flag need to unwind easings).

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED**: Many participants emphasize wide uncertainty. Boehne: "unusually wide spectrum of plausible outcomes." Minehan: "the room to maneuver in the case of error is very small." Greenspan: "the risks on both sides have widened" and "probabilities have risen quite substantially on both sides." Ferguson: "the outlook for the economy is 'murky.'" Rivlin: "the world is still a scary place." Meyer: "considerable uncertainty about inflation going forward." Kelley: "I am not at all clear in which direction the next policy move should be." Multiple participants cite Brazil, equity market fragility, and both upside and downside scenarios. This is elevated uncertainty. Score: 55 (elevated; multiple participants emphasize unusual uncertainty about both directions).

### Cross-Dimension Coherence Check
- OUTPUT_GAP (57) and LABOR_TIGHTNESS (70): Both above 50, consistent with late-cycle boom. Labor tightness is higher because it is discussed more emphatically and is the most salient feature. The gap between them reflects that while labor markets are extremely tight, overall output growth is expected to moderate. Reasonable divergence.
- HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN (52) and FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE (55): Both slightly above 50, consistent. Participants hold steady but lean mildly hawkish looking ahead. Aligned.

### Polarity Verification
- INFLATION_PRESSURE (38): Higher = more pressure. Evidence says "well-behaved," "benign," "subdued." Low score matches.
- OUTPUT_GAP (57): Higher = more above potential. Evidence says "above trend," "above the maximum path." Above-50 score matches.
- LABOR_TIGHTNESS (70): Higher = tighter. Evidence says "extraordinary tightness," "extremely tight." High score matches.
- FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS (42): Higher = tighter conditions. Evidence says conditions eased from crisis, "accommodative credit conditions," banks competing for loans. Below-50 score matches.
- HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN (52): Higher = more hawkish. Evidence says hold steady with emerging tightening lean. Slightly above 50 matches.

## Part 2: Evidence and Scores

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE
**Score: 38 +/-8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- GREENSPAN: "What has not changed in any material way is the seeming lack of pricing power in the economy. I see virtually nothing that suggests upward pressure on prices."
- MOSKOW: "price pressures remain benign"
- PARRY: "We expect inflation to hold steady next year...robust productivity growth, ample industrial capacity, low commodity prices, and subdued inflation expectations...core CPI to come in at around 2-1/4 percent."
- BROADDUS: "inflation currently remains exceptionally well behaved...some time next year the decline in commodity prices and in import prices...should end...goods price inflation could well bottom out and begin to rise."
- JORDAN: "we are seeing the brown spots" in health care and leisure services prices.

**Center-of-gravity summary**: The vast majority of participants describe inflation as benign and well-behaved, with favorable supply-side factors keeping it low, but several flag that these favorable factors may dissipate and upside risks exist.

**Minority views**: Jordan, Poole, and Broaddus see greater upside inflation risk; this informs the +/-8 band.

---

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP
**Score: 57 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- BROADDUS: "even with the slowing, we may still be above the maximum path on which the economy can grow without an increase in inflation"
- GREENSPAN: "an almost surely unforecastable economy that is expanding at a fairly rapid pace"
- STERN: "more real growth or at least more growth in aggregate demand than is indicated in the Greenbook"
- MEYER: "stronger-than-anticipated growth in the second half, maintaining the continuous pattern of positive demand surprises"

**Center-of-gravity summary**: The economy is clearly operating above potential with strong momentum, though participants expect growth to slow toward trend in 1999.

**Minority views**: Some participants (Ferguson, Rivlin) see more prominent downside risks that could bring growth below trend; this widens the band slightly.

---

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 70 +/-5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- BROADDUS: "the current extraordinary tightness in labor markets...getting even tighter in the service sector...more reports of labor shortages now than we did a while back"
- GUYNN: "labor markets remain extremely tight...the bigger concern...relates to the availability and quality of the remaining workers"
- JORDAN: "everybody talks about importing labor from someplace else"; company "plans to raise wages 10 to 12 percent across the board"
- STERN: "Labor markets remain very tight and there are clear signs of wage pressures, especially for entry-level jobs"
- GREENSPAN: "labor markets that, if anything, are growing marginally tighter, little evidence of wage acceleration"

**Center-of-gravity summary**: Nearly every participant reports very tight to extremely tight labor markets with acute worker shortages, though broad wage acceleration has not materialized.

**Minority views**: Very little disagreement on this dimension; narrow band.

---

### 4. CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT
**Score: 62 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- BROADDUS: "Retailers are reporting very busy stores, brisk holiday sales, and the strength seems to be broadly based"
- JORDAN: "malls are packed...holiday retail sales will be excellent...C&I loan demand is reported...to be the strongest that he can recall"
- KELLEY: "Retail sales are strong, particularly in the cyclically sensitive areas like housing and autos"
- MOSKOW: "consumer spending continues to be relatively healthy"; "Light vehicle sales are expected to be strong again"
- BROADDUS: "business investment in particular to decelerate, given the prospective slowing in corporate earnings"

**Center-of-gravity summary**: Consumer spending is robust and broadly based, housing and auto sales are strong, and business investment remains solid though some deceleration is expected due to slowing profits.

**Minority views**: Several note expected deceleration in investment and possible consumer retrenchment if stock market falls.

---

### 5. FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 42 +/-8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- MOSKOW: "heightened concerns about credit availability that were so apparent in September and October have eased somewhat as a result of our actions to lower interest rates"
- MEYER: "the deterioration of financial conditions...now appears less so. The equity market correction has simply disappeared. While private risk spreads have widened in the capital markets, the absolute level of private borrowing costs...is about what it was at the end of last year"
- STERN: "generally accommodative credit conditions do give me some pause"
- HOENIG: "Our banks are competing very vigorously for loans and they are competing on price"
- MCDONOUGH: "fixed income markets have not completely returned to normal but...will probably return to somewhat greater normalcy"

**Center-of-gravity summary**: Financial conditions have eased substantially from the fall 1998 crisis; credit is flowing freely, equity markets have recovered, and banks are competing aggressively for loans, though some residual spread widening persists.

**Minority views**: McDonough and Minehan note conditions have not fully normalized; Meyer notes spreads are still wider.

---

### 6. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN
**Score: 52 +/-6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- GREENSPAN: "we should stay where we are...our policy is back to balance and we should be looking to both the upside and the downside"
- POOLE: "If it were not for the fact that a tightening today would be a very large shock to the market...I think there would be a case for tightening now"
- JORDAN: "domestic considerations are going to require at some point that we start to raise the level of the overnight interbank nominal rate"
- BROADDUS: "had you recommended an asymmetric directive toward tightening, I would have been perfectly happy with that"
- HOENIG: "I'd suggest that we consider not holding off too long on the unwinding of our recent easing actions"
- GRAMLICH: "conditions look about right...I am relatively comfortable with the present stance of policy"

**Center-of-gravity summary**: Unanimous support for no change with symmetric directive, but a significant group of participants (Poole, Jordan, Broaddus, Hoenig, Meyer) lean hawkish looking forward, while no one advocates further easing.

**Minority views**: The hawkish contingent (Poole, Jordan, Broaddus, Hoenig) would have accepted asymmetric toward tightening; this pushes the score slightly above 50.

---

### 7. DISSENT_INTENSITY
**Score: 28 +/-5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- Unanimous vote for no change, symmetric directive
- JORDAN: "I disagreed with the funds rate reduction in November" (past dissent, not current)
- BROADDUS: "not with agony...but with at least some sense of unease"
- Significant procedural debate on directive language options but substantive policy agreement

**Center-of-gravity summary**: Broad agreement on current action with no formal dissents; differences in emphasis about forward risks (upside vs. balanced) are notable but do not rise to the level of significant disagreement.

**Minority views**: None formally dissenting; spread of views is about emphasis and forward outlook rather than current action.

---

### 8. FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS
**Score: 55 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- GREENSPAN: Extended discussion of stock market valuation, wealth effects, housing capital gains, saving rate driven by asset prices; "the presumption we have discussed...that we can effectively manage a bubble is probably based on a lack of humility"
- JORDAN: "perpetual great expectations" in equity market; "more and more people coming to that belief" that Fed will prop up markets; concern about pension allocations to equities
- POOLE: money growth "flowing through to some combination of asset prices and output"
- STERN: "an excessively elevated equity price" as downside risk
- MINEHAN: academic advisors expressed "concern that financial and especially stock market conditions might have been accorded too much weight in monetary policymaking"

**Center-of-gravity summary**: Financial stability is a prominent topic, with Greenspan and Jordan devoting extended remarks to equity valuation concerns, wealth effects, and asset price dynamics; several others reference financial market conditions as a key risk factor.

**Minority views**: Some participants focus more on real economy than financial conditions.

---

### 9. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE
**Score: 55 +/-7 [Moderate confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- GREENSPAN: "we should be looking to both the upside and the downside in judging the potential direction of our next move"
- MCDONOUGH: "further easing actions should take place only if there is clear and new evidence of weakness"
- MEYER: "we are going to have to be very vigilant against potential upside risks to inflation going forward"
- HOENIG: "I'd suggest that we consider not holding off too long on the unwinding of our recent easing actions"
- GRAMLICH: "We can wait until we see some brown spots before we have to do anything"

**Center-of-gravity summary**: The forward signal is nominally neutral (symmetric directive) but with a mild hawkish tilt; multiple participants signal the next move is more likely to be a tightening, and no one advocates further easing absent a shock.

**Minority views**: Gramlich and Rivlin emphasize patience and data-dependence, keeping the score from going higher.

---

### 10. UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED
**Score: 55 +/-7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- BOEHNE: "we have an unusually wide spectrum of plausible outcomes"
- GREENSPAN: "the risks on both sides have widened"; "the probabilities have risen quite substantially on both sides"
- FERGUSON: "the outlook for the economy is 'murky'...perhaps even murkier than the staff presentation might suggest"
- MINEHAN: "the room to maneuver in the case of error is very small"
- KELLEY: "I am not at all clear in which direction the next policy move should be, let alone when it should occur"
- MEYER: "considerable uncertainty about inflation going forward"

**Center-of-gravity summary**: Participants express elevated uncertainty about the outlook, with risks prominently on both sides -- upside from domestic momentum and downside from international fragility (Brazil, Japan) and potential equity correction.

**Minority views**: Gramlich is somewhat more sanguine ("conditions look about right"), but even he acknowledges risks.

### Optional Dimensions

**INFLATION_GOODS**: Multiple participants discuss falling commodity prices, low import prices, declining oil prices, steel price declines. Broaddus: "decline in commodity prices and in import prices of manufactured goods." Moskow: "steel...contracts...averaging a decline in prices of about 5 percent." Goods prices are clearly under deflationary pressure from Asia and commodities. **Score: 25 +/-7 [Moderate confidence]**

**INFLATION_SERVICES**: Broaddus notes "service price increases" are being offset by goods deflation. Guynn: "health care where costs are expected to increase at a double-digit rate." Jordan: "prices being raised...in a broad range of leisure goods industries including recreation, travel, tourism, entertainment, and restaurants." Service prices showing more pressure than goods. **Score: 58 +/-8 [Moderate confidence]**

**INFLATION_HOUSING**: Greenspan discusses housing prices rising substantially, but this is more in context of wealth effects than inflation per se. Some discussion of strong residential real estate markets. Not enough specific discussion of shelter/rent inflation by 2+ participants as an inflation component. **N/A**

**INFLATION_EXPECTATIONS**: Parry: "subdued inflation expectations." Poole: "very, very deeply embedded views about price stability." No one expresses concern about expectations becoming unanchored. **Score: 42 +/-5 [Moderate confidence]** (anchored, slightly below target)

**CREDIT_STRESS**: Conditions have eased from fall crisis. Meyer: "private risk spreads have widened...but the absolute level of private borrowing costs...is about what it was at the end of last year." Minehan: "Wall Street financing dried up during the late summer and fall market turmoil but other investors filled the gap, albeit at higher financing rates." Banks competing vigorously for loans. Residual widening but normalizing. **Score: 48 +/-7 [Moderate confidence]**

**EQUITY_STRESS**: Several participants express concern about equity market overvaluation. Jordan: "perpetual great expectations, which he considers to be unrealistic"; concern about pension fund allocation increases. Greenspan: "presumption that stock market prices can continue to grow 20 percent a year seems absurd"; "a nonnegligible probability that the market could go down very substantially." Stern: "excessively elevated equity price." This is clearly an overvaluation/bubble concern, not crash/undervaluation. Per the rubric, 1 = extreme overvaluation concern (bubble). **Score: 20 +/-10 [Moderate confidence]**

**BANKING_STRESS**: Banks are described as healthy and competing aggressively. Hoenig: "banks are competing very vigorously for loans." Jordan: "C&I loan demand...the strongest that he can recall." No stress discussed. Some implicit concern about excessive risk-taking. **Score: 30 +/-10 [Low confidence]** (low stress, possible excessive risk-taking)

**TREASURY_STRESS**: N/A -- not discussed as a specific dysfunction topic.

**DOLLAR_CONCERN**: Poole notes "the dollar has tended to be strong rather than weak." Meyer: "a particular upside risk from a sharper-than-projected depreciation of the dollar." Ferguson: "waning impact of the dollar's appreciation." Some discussion but mostly as context rather than concern. **N/A** (not enough focused concern by 2+ participants)

**PRODUCTIVITY**: Multiple participants reference strong productivity. Parry: "robust productivity growth." Rivlin: "good productivity growth continued even in manufacturing." Greenspan: "rather strong productivity gains." This is discussed as a notably positive force. **Score: 68 +/-7 [Moderate confidence]**
