## Part 1: Thinking Process

### 1. Document Type
This is a two-day FOMC **transcript** (March 14–15, 2017). Speakers are individually identified (MR. WILLIAMS, MS. MESTER, MR. KASHKARI, VICE CHAIRMAN DUDLEY, MS. BRAINARD, MS. GEORGE, MR. EVANS, MR. KAPLAN, MR. HARKER, MR. POWELL, MR. ROSENGREN, MR. BULLARD, MR. FISCHER, MR. KASHKARI, CHAIR YELLEN, etc.). The transcript is fully segmented — Day 1 covers a balance-sheet/reinvestment policy discussion, followed by an economic outlook go-around; Day 2 covers the policy decision round. This is a normal, full-length meeting.

### 2. Era Context
March 2017: mid-hiking cycle. The Fed had begun liftoff in December 2015, hiked once in December 2016, and this meeting is the first 2017 hike. The broad backdrop is a labor market near or at full employment, core PCE inflation running ~1.7%, headline PCE approaching 2% on energy base effects, equity markets elevated post-election, and the balance sheet normalization discussion actively underway. I note this context only to orient the anchor comparisons; scoring is based entirely on what participants say in this transcript.

### 3. Directional Signals — Core Dimensions

**INFLATION_PRESSURE**
Participants broadly agree inflation is *below* but *approaching* target. Core PCE is running ~1.7% (Mester, Brainard, Evans all cite this explicitly). Headline PCE has risen to ~1.9% due to energy base effects, but participants caution this is temporary. Evans projects sustained 2% not until end of 2019. Kashkari explicitly argues that "Core PCE inflation basically hasn't moved" and says it is "too soon to declare victory." Williams notes inflation needs to "reach 2 percent, not just get close to it." Fischer says "we are very close to attaining our targets." The center of gravity is *slightly below target but approaching it*, not worrisomely low. Comparable to the 40–45 range for a "approaching but not yet at target" reading, noting that unlike 2013–14 there is genuine momentum. I assign **44**: below target, some progress, but committee retains watchfulness language.

**Direction Rule**: Participants see inflation as slightly below target, with headline boosted by energy and core still at ~1.7% — consistent with a score below 50.
**Magnitude**: "Somewhat below" / "approaching" = ~5–10 points below 50. Score: 44.
**Polarity check**: Higher = more pressure. Evidence = below target → score < 50. ✓

**OUTPUT_GAP**
Multiple participants describe economy growing "at or above trend." Williams: "GDP growth of 1¾ percent this year," near potential; Rosengren forecasts growth ~2.3% vs. potential of ~2%, expecting unemployment to "go significantly below most estimates of full employment." Mester says "Real GDP growth continues at or above its trend pace." Dudley describes growth as "slightly above-trend pace." Powell: "economy close to full employment and still tightening." Chair Yellen: "recent spending and production indicators do not suggest that overall activity is accelerating." Bullard is the outlier — he sees GDP barely at trend (~2%) and argues for a near-steady-state view. The majority see the economy at-to-slightly-above potential. Bright-line rule: at-potential/slightly-above language → score 50–55.

**Direction Rule**: Majority see economy at or modestly above potential.
**Score**: 52 — at potential edge, mild above-trend language from most, offset by Bullard's dissent and Yellen's caution on spending softness.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS**
This is the most heavily discussed dimension. Multiple participants describe labor market as at or near full employment. Mester: "We have met the full employment part of our dual mandate." Brainard: "closing in on full employment." Harker: unemployment falling to 4.4% by year-end. Rosengren: unemployment at 4.5% end-of-year "beyond full employment." Kaplan: "labor shortages" in the Permian, Texas job growth at 2.5%. George: difficulty rehiring rig workers. Kashkari is the clearest dissenter — he argues significant uncertainty about remaining slack remains, labor force participation still rising, and "the job market may still have some slack left in it." Powell: "close to full employment and still tightening." Yellen: "more or less back to normal but not overheating." Wages described as "subdued" or "modest" (Yellen), which limits the tightness reading somewhat.

**Direction Rule**: At or very near full employment, with a meaningful minority noting potential hidden slack.
**Score**: 53 — at the NAIRU edge, majority at "full employment" but not showing strong wage pressure or shortages across the board; consistent with the 50–53 "near NAIRU/full employment" anchor.

**Cross-check OUTPUT_GAP vs. LABOR_TIGHTNESS**: Both near 50–55, consistent. ✓

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT**
Several participants note softness in consumer spending (Q1 PCE fell 0.3% in January), with consensus that it is temporary. Mester: "believe the soft consumer spending data in January will prove to be temporary." Brainard: "continued job gains, rising wealth, and elevated confidence should lead to stronger gains in coming quarters." Powell: "personal consumption…supported by above-trend increases in employment, moderate wage gains, and rising wealth." Business investment described as recovering from oil-price drag — Brainard notes "signs of renewed growth in business investment." Optimism is elevated but "has not yet translated into substantially increased consumer activity" (Mester, Kaplan, Evans contacts). Evans' contact: "Caterpillar…not as much as one might have expected." The picture is moderate — at-trend spending with soft Q1 and expectation of bounce.

**Direction Rule**: Consumption and investment at or just below trend — soft Q1 seen as temporary, fundamentals supportive.
**Score**: 49 — essentially at trend, soft Q1 drags just under midpoint but forward-looking sentiment is supportive.

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS**
Several participants note that financial conditions have actually *eased* since December despite the hiking cycle. Dudley explicitly: "rise in equity prices and the narrowing of credit spreads has outweighed the rise in long-term Treasury yields…financial conditions have eased despite a shift in expectations about us moving a bit faster" — equivalent to a 40–70bp reduction in short-term rates per one investment bank's index. Powell: "Equity markets are booming, which is adding accommodation." Brainard: "easing in financial conditions" since start of year — equities up ~5%, dollar slightly down, high-yield spreads narrower. Rosengren raises concern about narrow credit spreads and high P/E ratios as potential "froth." No participant characterizes conditions as tight.

**Direction Rule**: Financial conditions described as loose/accommodative, not tight. Score must be well below 50.
**Score**: 32 — conditions clearly accommodative/loose; equity-driven easing offsetting rate hikes; consistent with the 45–55 "normal" anchor for 2017–19 but here clearly tilted to the looser side.

**Polarity check**: Higher = tighter. Evidence says loose → score < 50. ✓

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN**
The meeting voted to raise rates 25bp (Alternative B). The vote count: One formal dissent — Kashkari voted for Alternative A (no hike). Nearly all other participants supported the hike. Mester argues the "base case" should shift to removing accommodation. Rosengren wants more aggressive normalization path and quarterly hikes. Harker supports three hikes in 2017 + four in 2018. Mester: "upward path of policy rate as appropriate." Fischer: "time to raise the interest rate now, and likely we will make further rate increases this year." Williams: four funds rate increases for 2017 in his SEP. Evans supports the hike but projects only two for the year — least hawkish of the hikers. Bullard: leans toward Alternative A, worries about trajectory; accepts B reluctantly. Brainard: supports B as written, emphasizes gradualism, concerned about overshooting. Kaplan: gradual removal, three hikes in 2017. One formal dissent (Kashkari).

**Direction Rule**: Strong majority favors tightening now and continued hiking path. Score well above 50.
**Anchor check**: 2017–18 hiking cycle anchor 70–75. A meeting with near-consensus hike (only 1 dissent) + active discussion of balance sheet normalization this year + several voices calling for quarterly pace → toward 70–73 range. Slightly tempered by Evans/Bullard/Brainard arguing for gradualism and caution.
**Score**: 70 — "strong hawkish: consensus for continued tightening; further increases needed." Matches anchor.

**DISSENT_INTENSITY**
One formal dissent (Kashkari, Alternative A). Several participants express reservations — Bullard most explicitly wavers between A and B, ultimately accepting B but voicing significant concerns. Evans accepts B but flags two as his base case. Brainard supports B but emphasizes gradualism. The statement language debate (keep "only," monitor inflation, etc.) shows notable differences in emphasis but not fundamental framework disagreement. Overall: one formal dissent, 2–3 meaningful reservations, moderate differences about pace.

**Direction Rule**: Moderate-to-notable disagreement — one formal dissent plus several reluctant supporters.
**Score**: 38 — "moderate differences: notable differences about pace/risk; 1 dissent or 2–3 reservations." Matches rubric anchor for a typical one-dissent meeting (25–35) but the depth of debate around statement language and Bullard's near-dissent pushes it slightly higher.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS**
Multiple participants mention financial stability concerns, but not as a dominant theme. Rosengren is the most prominent: discusses "froth" in asset markets, high P/E ratios, narrow credit spreads, commercial real estate capitalization rates at historical lows, reaching-for-yield. Brainard warns about "valuations in some asset markets…somewhat stretched." Powell: "risk of a correction in equity markets." Fischer: concerns about loosening financial-sector discipline, risk of another crisis if capital requirements reduced. Dudley mentions Deutsche Bank raising equity capital as a positive sign. Kashkari: "Stock markets continue to rise, and prices do seem high relative to standard valuation measures." This represents more than passing mention — several participants address it meaningfully — but it is not the dominant theme of the meeting.

**Score**: 38 — "limited-to-moderate discussion; several participants address it meaningfully as a side topic."

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE**
The forward signal: Committee removes "only" from "only gradual increases" — deliberately shifting to "gradual increases" to signal expectation of continued hiking. Yellen's intended press conference language: "consistent with our long-standing expectation that gradual increases in the federal funds rate would be warranted." SEP median shows three hikes in 2017. Williams projects four hikes in 2017. Rosengren argues for "quarterly" pace. Balance sheet normalization discussed as beginning later in 2017. The forward signal is clearly toward continued tightening, but "gradual" and "data-dependent" language remains prominent. Evans signals only two hikes. The guidance is unambiguously hawkish but explicitly not aggressive.

**Anchor**: 2015–18 hiking cycle = 60–75. A meeting that hikes 25bp, signals continued gradual hikes, removes "only" from guidance, and discusses early balance sheet normalization sits at ~72.
**Score**: 72 — "clear tightening signal: further rate increases or balance sheet reduction ahead."

**Alignment check HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN (70) vs. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE (72)**: Consistent. ✓

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED**
The principal source of uncertainty is U.S. fiscal policy — virtually all participants note they cannot forecast its size/timing. Mester: "considerable uncertainty about U.S. fiscal and other government policies." Evans: "uncertainty remains high" on fiscal/trade/regulatory. Williams: "uncertain about fiscal policy." Bullard warns about soft vs. hard data divergence. Harker: "too much uncertainty about the scope and timing." Brainard notes European election risks, China leverage buildup. Kashkari expresses uncertainty about remaining labor slack. Governor Fischer speaks more broadly about political and structural uncertainties. However, the baseline macro forecast is described by many as "little changed" from December, risks seen as "roughly balanced," and the decision to hike was made with confidence. The uncertainty is real but bounded — it does not reach "particularly uncertain" levels.

**Direction Rule**: Elevated but not extreme — fiscal policy uncertainty is a clear recurring theme; global political risks flagged; but baseline outlook stable.
**Score**: 42 — "somewhat elevated: certain factors harder to assess than usual"; consistent with fiscal policy uncertainty in 2017 as a notable but not paralyzing factor.
