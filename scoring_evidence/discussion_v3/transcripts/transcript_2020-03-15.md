## Part 1: Thinking Process

### 1. Document Type
This is a **transcript** of an emergency FOMC meeting held Sunday, March 15, 2020. It contains individually attributed participant statements organized in two rounds: (1) an economic outlook go-around and (2) a policy go-around. Speakers are identified by name and title (e.g., "MR. CLARIDA.", "CHAIR POWELL.", "MS. DALY."). This is a segmented transcript with clear participant and Chair attributions throughout.

### 2. Era Context
March 15, 2020 is the **COVID-19 emergency meeting** — one of the clearest emergency-meeting edge cases specified in the rubric. The meeting occurs at the onset of the pandemic shock to the U.S. economy. The prior expansion had run to record length with strong labor markets, but COVID-19 was triggering a rapid, unprecedented shutdown of economic activity. The Fed had already cut 50bp on March 3 and was now deliberating a further 100bp cut to the ZLB. This is precisely the scenario flagged in the rubric's EDGE CASES: "Emergency meetings (Sept 2001, Sept 2008, March 2020): Discussion may be short/narrow. Use N/A liberally. Note in anomaly flag." However, this transcript is actually quite long (~112 pages of source material, ~2,500 lines extracted), with extensive participant remarks. Confidence should still be rated appropriately by evidence density.

### 3. Core Dimension Direction Statements

**INFLATION_PRESSURE**: Multiple participants explicitly describe the net effect of COVID-19 as **disinflationary**. Clarida: "the net effect of the virus is likely to be disinflationary, not inflationary...we will end the year further away from our price-stability objective." Daly: "we face considerable disinflationary pressures coming from weaker demand, higher uncertainty, a stronger dollar, and lower oil prices...inflation is going to be an ongoing concern." Bowman notes "inflation has been tracking relatively close to our 2 percent target" as a prior-to-shock observation — but all forward-looking remarks stress disinflation. Participants see inflation as **below target and moving further below** due to the shock. This places the score well below 50, in the 18–25 range (meaningful deflation/disinflation risk, below target). The rubric anchor of 15–20 applies to 2009 deflation fears; here there's explicit disinflation language but not yet a deflationary spiral. Score: ~22.

**OUTPUT_GAP**: Near-universal view that the economy is about to contract sharply. Clarida expects "real GDP will contract in the second quarter, perhaps sharply." Rosengren expects "a recession." Kaplan: "it is our base case...that we will have a recession this year. We'll have a substantial contraction." Bullard: "a bad Q2 could be as much as 10 percent, at an annual rate, decline." Williams describes "essentially, a global recession." Powell: "It seems likely that the outbreak will cause the economy to shrink in the second quarter, maybe by a substantial number." Participants see output as about to fall **far below potential** — well below the rubric's range for "catastrophic contraction" territory. This is the moment just before the COVID collapse. The rubric anchor places 2020 Q2 at 15–22. At this meeting, the sharp contraction is anticipated but not yet realized — a few participants hold a non-recession baseline (Clarida: GDP around 1.25%, not a recession baseline; Bostic: "My baseline forecast does not include a recession"). However, the center of gravity has shifted firmly toward a severe near-term contraction. Score: ~18.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS**: Pre-meeting, labor was at a 50-year low unemployment rate. Most participants note this as the "strong" pre-crisis baseline. However, near-term projections involve rapid labor market deterioration. Barkin: "hiring dropping immediately." Daly: "starting to see it in hours and even in layoffs here." George: "job cuts right away" for oil and gas/tourism. Rosengren: "pushing the unemployment rate noticeably above the natural rate." The center of gravity at this snapshot is ambiguous — as of the meeting, layoffs have begun but the labor market has not yet collapsed. Participants describe an economy transitioning from very tight (50-year low) to deteriorating rapidly. A forward-looking assessment gives this meeting a score in the 20–28 range — the rapid deterioration is imminent and already beginning. Score: ~22.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT**: Severe near-term pullback is almost universally expected. Mester: "large national department store retailer...seen a broad-based nationwide pullback in physical store sales...an average sales decline of 60 to 70 percent." Evans: "Investment was already disappointing before the outbreak, and now businesses could pull back even more on cap-ex...consumers could pull back substantially." Brainard: "consumers cut back on certain types of spending and businesses reduce investment in the face of heightened uncertainty." Bostic: "marked deterioration in year-ahead sales growth expectations." The rubric anchor for 2020 Q2 is 15–20 (collapsing). While the collapse is just beginning, participants describe it as severe and deepening. Score: ~18.

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS**: This is perhaps the most prominently discussed topic. Williams: Treasury securities market "has shown a rapid deterioration in liquidity...similar to what we saw in the financial crisis." Rosengren: "Financial firms complain they cannot even trade in off-the-run Treasury securities...Mortgage markets are badly disrupted...the corporate bond market has shut down." Kaplan: "investment-grade companies are seeing their credit spreads gap out...access to CP has been said to be going away." Brainard: "massive repricing and repositioning in financial markets...stress on market making in Treasury securities markets." Powell: "the famously liquid market for U.S. Treasury securities — is experiencing high abnormal conditions of severe illiquidity." The rubric anchor of 85–95 applies to September 2008 credit freeze and March 2020 "dash for cash." This meeting is that scenario — extreme credit tightness, market dysfunction across Treasuries, MBS, CP, and corporate credit. Score: ~90.

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN**: The overwhelmingly dominant stance is emergency dovish — 100bp rate cut to ZLB, QE launch ($500B Treasuries + $200B MBS "at least"), discount window reforms, swap line expansions, reserve requirement elimination. Powell explicitly proposes 100bp; virtually all participants support this (Clarida, Williams, Rosengren, Harker, Evans, Bullard, Kashkari, George, Daly, Evans, Brainard all explicitly support 100bp or fully support the package). Mester favors 50bp instead and Bostic raises concerns about 100bp; Quarles supports 100bp with "reservations"; Kaplan also reluctantly supports 100bp. Harker and Barkin express communication concerns but ultimately support the package. This is clearly in the 1–5 range — "Emergency dovish: immediate large-scale easing, QE launch, 'do whatever it takes'." The rubric anchor is 2–5 for March 2020. Score: ~3.

**DISSENT_INTENSITY**: Despite the emergency nature, there is notable disagreement, primarily about the **magnitude** of the rate cut (100bp vs. 50bp). Mester explicitly prefers 50bp and argues against going to zero. Bostic raises substantive concerns about 100bp and the communication risks. Quarles supports 100bp "with reservations." Kaplan went into the meeting preferring 50bp but relented. Harker supports 100bp but raises communication concerns. This is not a unanimous slam-dunk — multiple participants have substantive disagreements about the pace/magnitude, though all support easing. The rubric notes that "zero dissents but 4-5 'reluctant' supporters can score 40-50." Here, at least 2–3 participants (Mester, Bostic, and arguably Quarles/Kaplan) have genuine procedural/magnitude disagreements, and Mester's preference for 50bp is a clear directional divergence. The formal vote appears unanimous (no formal dissent is recorded in the text). Score: ~42.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS**: Financial stability and financial conditions are the **dominant topic** of this meeting. Nearly every participant addresses market dysfunction, credit stress, Treasury market illiquidity, or systemic financial risk at length. Williams devotes his entire economic go-round to Treasury/MBS market dysfunction. Rosengren discusses corporate bond market shutdown, leveraged firm vulnerabilities, shadow banking contraction. Kaplan focuses exclusively on credit market deleveraging. Brainard provides detailed analysis of Treasury market fragilities. Powell opens the policy discussion with financial market dysfunction as "job one." The rubric anchor of 80–95 applies to September 2008 and March 2020 — this meeting is that scenario. Score: ~92.

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE**: The forward guidance committed to holding at ZLB "until we're confident that the economy has weathered recent events and is on track to achieve our dual-mandate goals." This is a strong dovish commitment — threshold-based language keeping rates at zero until recovery. Several participants (Evans, Kashkari, Daly) argue the guidance could be even stronger/more dovish. Brainard describes it as a commitment to "remain at the lower bound until we are confidently on track to reach full employment and 2 percent inflation." This is squarely in the rubric's 2–10 range ("strongest easing commitment: threshold-based or date-based guidance promising near-zero rates"). Score: ~6.

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED**: Extreme uncertainty is the defining epistemological theme. Evans: "the nature of uncertainty is impossible to quantify. This is truly the Knightian uncertainty situation." Williams: "the Knightian uncertainty around this...the uncertainty is so hard to measure or anticipate." Clarida: "The U.S. economic outlook is profoundly uncertain." Bostic: "There is much uncertainty in this response on all three fronts." Daly: "we are in this Knightian uncertainty in which we can't quantify the risks ahead of us." Harker: "fraught with uncertainty." George: "significant unknowns that we face." Multiple participants explicitly invoke "Knightian uncertainty." The rubric anchor of 85–95 covers March 2020; "cannot bound the range" language is present. Score: ~93.

## Part 2: Evidence and Scores

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE
**Score: 22 ±6 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- Clarida: "the net effect of the virus is likely to be disinflationary, not inflationary...my baseline view is that we will end the year further away from our price-stability objective than we began."
- Daly: "we face considerable disinflationary pressures coming from weaker demand, higher uncertainty, a stronger dollar, and lower oil prices. So this means that even when the pandemic abates, inflation is going to be an ongoing concern."
- Bowman (baseline pre-shock): "inflation has been tracking relatively close to our 2 percent target" — but framed as a prior condition now under threat.

**Center-of-gravity summary**: Participants broadly see inflation as already below target and moving substantially further below target due to the demand collapse, with explicit disinflation language from multiple speakers — placing conditions well into the subdued/deflation-risk range.

**Minority views**: Bullard briefly argues oil price decline "acts like a tax cut for consumers" and has a positive aspect, but this is a minor note, not a counterargument to the disinflation consensus.

---

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP
**Score: 18 ±8 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- Kaplan: "it is our base case...that we will have a recession this year. We'll have a substantial contraction."
- Bullard: "a bad Q2 could be as much as 10 percent, at an annual rate, decline."
- Powell: "It seems likely that the outbreak will cause the economy to shrink in the second quarter, maybe by a substantial number."

**Center-of-gravity summary**: The center of gravity has shifted decisively toward a severe near-term contraction with GDP falling sharply below potential — though a minority (Clarida, Bostic) hold a non-recession baseline with positive-but-low GDP growth for 2020. Expectations of catastrophic Q2 contraction dominate.

**Minority views**: Clarida forecasts GDP around +1.25% for 2020 as a whole, avoiding a technical recession. Bostic also has a non-recession baseline. These are clear minority views.

---

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 22 ±8 [Moderate confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- Barkin: "hiring dropping immediately, as businesses exercise caution on open positions. And if a shutdown lingers, employers will take action soon enough."
- Rosengren: "I fear that we will observe these leveraged firms shedding employees more quickly, pushing the unemployment rate noticeably above the natural rate."
- Daly: "unlike many of the other reports, we're starting to see it in hours and even in layoffs here."

**Center-of-gravity summary**: The labor market is transitioning very rapidly from a 50-year low in unemployment to imminent severe deterioration; participants universally expect massive layoffs to follow from the shutdown, placing the forward-looking assessment firmly in the "approaching high unemployment" range. Current tightness is acknowledged as a legacy condition already eroding.

**Minority views**: Several participants (Mester, Bostic, Barkin) note that as of the meeting date, large-scale layoffs had not yet materialized, with firms trying to retain workers. This informs the uncertainty band.

---

### 4. CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT
**Score: 18 ±7 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- Mester: "large national department store retailer...seen a broad-based nationwide pullback in physical store sales since the middle of last week, with an average sales decline of 60 to 70 percent across the nation."
- Evans: "consumers could pull back substantially...Investment was already disappointing before the outbreak, and now businesses could pull back even more on cap-ex."
- Brainard: "as consumers cut back on certain types of spending and businesses reduce investment in the face of heightened uncertainty."

**Center-of-gravity summary**: Both consumer spending and business investment are seen as collapsing rapidly across all districts, with anecdotal evidence of 60–70% sales declines in retail and widespread hiring freezes and investment pullbacks, placing this squarely in the "collapsing/freefall" range.

**Minority views**: Mester notes that one manufacturer of personal care/household products saw very high demand due to "pantry-loading" and maintained investment plans, but this is acknowledged as exceptional rather than representative.

### 5. FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 90 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- Williams: "the U.S. Treasury securities market...has shown a rapid deterioration in liquidity over the past week...extraordinarily low, low levels of liquidity, similar to what we saw in the financial crisis."
- Rosengren: "Financial firms complain they cannot even trade in off-the-run Treasury securities...Mortgage markets are badly disrupted...the corporate bond market has shut down."
- Powell: "the famously liquid market for U.S. Treasury securities — is experiencing high abnormal conditions of severe illiquidity."

**Center-of-gravity summary**: Virtually every participant who addresses financial conditions describes crisis-level dysfunction across Treasury markets, MBS markets, CP markets, and corporate credit — with explicit comparisons to the 2008 GFC — placing conditions at the extreme end of the financial tightness range.

**Minority views**: Bowman notes that "outside of moderate concerns about elevated corporate debt levels, our periodic assessments of financial stability have not revealed any major systemic risks" and banks are well-capitalized — but this is framed as a pre-shock assessment, not a characterization of current conditions.

---

### 6. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN
**Score: 3 ±3 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- Powell: "My own view is that we should go ahead with 100 basis points...I feel that there is no useful purpose to be served in holding back today."
- Clarida: "I support the decision to reduce the target range for the federal funds rate by 100 basis points."
- Kashkari: "I support the 100 basis point reduction. I think cutting 50 now and 50 a few weeks from now doesn't make any sense. If we're going to get back to the lower bound, let's just get there."

**Center-of-gravity summary**: The overwhelming consensus is for the most aggressive dovish action available — immediate 100bp cut to ZLB combined with QE launch of $700B+ in asset purchases and full liquidity package — with only a minority (Mester, Bostic as loudest voices) preferring 50bp instead of 100bp. The rubric anchor of 2–5 for March 2020 is confirmed.

**Minority views**: Mester explicitly prefers 50bp, Bostic raises concerns and tilts toward 50bp, Quarles supports 100bp "with reservations," Kaplan entered preferring 50bp but accepts 100bp. These views do not shift the center from emergency-dovish territory.

### 7. DISSENT_INTENSITY
**Score: 42 ±8 [Moderate confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- Mester: "I favor a 50 basis point reduction at this time...I'm reluctant to move to the lower bound and use up all of our interest rate policy space at this time."
- Bostic: "I appreciate the arguments supporting a 100 basis point reduction...I do think it is important to raise the issue of risks that make this approach something less than an obvious slam dunk."
- Quarles: "I can support that if that is the conclusion of this Committee...I do so with some reservation."

**Center-of-gravity summary**: No formal recorded voting dissents, but at least 2–3 participants (Mester, Bostic, Quarles) have substantive disagreements about the rate cut magnitude, and 2 additional (Kaplan, Harker) entered with reservations before accepting 100bp. This qualifies as the "zero dissents but 4–5 reluctant supporters" scenario the rubric identifies as 40–50.

**Minority views**: Multiple participants also share communication concerns (Harker, Barkin, Bullard, Bowman) about the risk of panicking markets — a separate dimension of disagreement about tactics even among those who support 100bp.

### 8. FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS
**Score: 92 ±5 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- Williams (entire go-round devoted to): "the primary focus for us is the fact that the U.S. Treasury securities market...has shown a rapid deterioration in liquidity...it's spilling over into the broader financial system."
- Powell: "at this particular moment, liquidity and market functioning are job one."
- Brainard: "The most immediate imperative is to prevent severe stress in financial markets from spiraling into much more severe financial and economic crises."

**Center-of-gravity summary**: Financial stability and market functioning are the dominant, primary topic of this meeting — discussed at length by virtually every participant in both go-rounds, rivaling and arguably exceeding the economic assessment discussion. The rubric anchor of 80–95 for March 2020 is confirmed.

**Minority views**: None — this is the near-universal focus.

### 9. FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE
**Score: 6 ±4 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- Powell: "Our proposed guidance says we'll keep the funds rate there until we're confident that the economy has weathered recent events and is on track to achieve our dual-mandate goals."
- Brainard: "the forward guidance that policy will remain at the lower bound until we are confidently on track to reach full employment and 2 percent inflation."
- Daly: "we have to say...that we have confidence that both the virus is behind us and the economy has recovered" — arguing the guidance could be even more dovish.

**Center-of-gravity summary**: Forward guidance is a strong, open-ended commitment to hold at ZLB until dual-mandate recovery is achieved — a threshold-based promise of extended accommodation that scores at the extreme dovish end of the forward guidance scale. Several participants argue it should be even stronger/more explicit.

**Minority views**: Barkin questions whether the specific "on track to achieve" language is necessary and might create confusion, suggesting a simpler formulation — but this is a communication/wording concern, not a hawkish lean.

### 10. UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED
**Score: 93 ±4 [High confidence]**

**Evidence**:
- Evans: "the nature of uncertainty is impossible to quantify. This is truly the Knightian uncertainty situation. Financial markets are struggling to even bound the risk posed by the coronavirus."
- Williams: "when the uncertainty is so hard to measure or anticipate...Knightian uncertainty around this."
- Daly: "we are in this Knightian uncertainty in which we can't quantify the risks ahead of us."

**Center-of-gravity summary**: "Knightian uncertainty" is explicitly invoked by multiple speakers — a term reserved for the most extreme cases where the distribution of outcomes is itself unknown. Every participant expresses extraordinary uncertainty about the economic and health outlook, with models and historical precedents acknowledged as inapplicable.

**Minority views**: Quarles suggests the crisis may be "more rapid" in recovery "once we get past the lockdown, more like...a natural disaster or 9/11 than a typical business recession" — implying somewhat more confidence in the shape of recovery, but this does not materially reduce the aggregate uncertainty expressed.
