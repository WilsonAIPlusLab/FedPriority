# FOMC Participant Discussion Scoring — April 28–29, 2009 (Transcript)

---

## Part 1: Thinking Process

### 1. Document Type
This is a **transcript** of individual participant statements from a two-day FOMC meeting (April 28–29, 2009). Speakers are identified by name (e.g., MR. ROSENGREN., MS. YELLEN., CHAIRMAN BERNANKE.). The document contains: (a) a Q&A session on the staff's balance-sheet analysis; (b) a full economic go-around covering all regional presidents and governors; (c) Chairman Bernanke's summary; (d) a policy go-around discussing alternatives A vs. B; and (e) a transparency discussion. All of (a)–(d) are participant-attributable and scorable.

### 2. Era Context
April 2009 is the trough of the Great Financial Crisis recession. Real GDP was contracting sharply (Q1 2009 came in at approximately −6.1% annualized). Unemployment was rising rapidly toward 9%+ and would peak above 10% later in 2009. The FOMC had already cut the federal funds rate to 0–0.25% in December 2008 and launched large-scale asset purchases (MBS, agency debt, $300 billion Treasuries) at the March 2009 meeting. The Greenbook baseline called for a prolonged zero-rate path. Deflation was a recognized risk; upside inflation risk from balance-sheet expansion was also debated. Era anchors say OUTPUT_GAP should be 15–22, LABOR_TIGHTNESS 15–22, INFLATION_PRESSURE 15–20, HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN 2–5.

### 3. Directional Signals for Each Core Dimension

**INFLATION_PRESSURE:**
The center of gravity among participants is that inflation is below target with downside (deflation) risk dominant, though a vocal minority (Plosser, Bullard, Warsh, Pianalto) worry about medium-term upside from balance-sheet expansion. Yellen: "core inflation to dip down to 1½ percent this year and 1 percent next year"; Kohn: "inflation should continue to drift down"; Evans: "core PCE inflation coming in at about 1¼ percent in 2010 and 2011"; Rosengren: "significant slack in the labor markets will continue to place a lot of downward pressure on inflation, and there remains a significant risk that deflation will not be avoided"; Bernanke's summary: "Most see inflation as remaining low for the foreseeable future... deflation risks have moderated but remain a worry for some." Plosser is an outlier forecasting PCE above 3% in 2010–2012, but that is explicitly a minority view. Score: below-target, meaningful deflation risk acknowledged but not spiraling — squarely in the 15–25 range. The central forecast (1–1.5% core PCE, below 2%) and active deflation concern puts this at **18**.

**OUTPUT_GAP:**
Near-unanimous agreement that the economy is in deep recession, far below potential. Yellen: "optimal policy simulation indicates we should lower the funds rate to negative 7½ percent"; Kohn: "a large output gap persisting for some time"; Bernanke summary: "slack looks to be high and increasing." The contraction in Q1 was "much steeper than anticipated" (Plosser). Unemployment heading toward 9.6–10%. Production "well below even the weak level of demand" (Kohn). Plosser argues the measured gap is overstated because equilibrium output has fallen, but even he concedes GDP will be negative 2009 and positive but below-trend in 2010. Bright-line rule: "below potential" language throughout. Score: deep recession territory, GFC trough — **15**.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS:**
Labor market described as "appalling" (Yellen), unemployment rising 1.7 pp in four months, permanent layoffs dominating, involuntary part-time work surging toward 16% underemployment rate (Yellen), "hemorrhaging jobs" character, firms cutting staffing with no net hiring. Pianalto expects unemployment to peak above 10%. Bernanke: "labor markets remained weak." "Job cuts exceed hires by a 10 to 1 margin" (Fisher/Texas survey). Era anchor is 15–22 for 2009. Score: **16** — near the bottom of the labor market, roughly consistent with high unemployment and severe slack.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT:**
Some very tentative signs of stabilization at low levels — "pace of contraction in aggregate spending appears to have slowed" (Yellen), "consumer spending shows some signs of bottoming out" (Bernanke), Plosser cites +0.4% month-over-month consumer spending in April. But retail contacts describe March as "more characteristic of what to expect." Inventory decumulation brisk. Capital expenditures remain negative (Lacker survey). Commercial real estate "almost no financing available" (Lockhart). Business investment severely constrained. Fisher: "there are no sparks." The FOMC characterizes conditions as contracting but with tentative stabilization signals. Era anchor is 15–20 for 2008 Q4–2009 Q1. By April 2009 there are slightly more green shoots vs. Q1 — call this **22**: still deep contraction territory, marginally better than absolute trough but clearly contracting.

**FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS:**
Mixed picture: financial conditions "generally improved" since March meeting (stock market up sharply, convertible markets booming, corporate bond issuance robust, spreads narrowing, SLOOS improving somewhat). But credit still "contracting," banking system "not on a sound footing" (Dudley), commercial real estate financing "almost none available," banks capital-constrained, stress-test risk seen as large. Kohn: "borrowing rates, terms, and conditions for many private borrowers are still very tight." Warsh: "The financial markets... still show signs of improvement." Financial conditions improved materially from the crisis peak but remain substantially tight. Era: Sept 2008 was 85–95; by April 2009 conditions have improved considerably but credit still constrained. Score: **68** — clearly tight, somewhat reduced from crisis peak, credit still restricted, banks still impaired.

**HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN:**
The policy vote is near-unanimous for alternative B (hold, no new purchases). Yellen is the sole advocate for alternative A (increase purchases). Rosengren and Evans express sympathy for A but ultimately support B. The discussion centers on maintaining the already-large QE program, not adding to it. The floor is zero. The majority opposes expansion. However, the underlying stance IS maximum accommodation (zero rates + large-scale QE ongoing). Plosser is the most hawkish, arguing for rate hikes by year-end. Warsh "extremely uncomfortable" with Treasury purchases. Hoenig also cautious about balance-sheet size. Era anchor: 2–5 for March 2009 emergency. April 2009 is similar to March in terms of overall policy stance — still at ZLB + QE ongoing, but now "watchful waiting" rather than "do whatever it takes" emergency mode. The group's center of gravity is dovish (continuing QE, zero rates, further action if needed) but not launching new emergency measures. Score: **12** — strong dovish, majority maintaining max accommodation, minority arguing for more (Yellen/Rosengren) vs. minority arguing for less aggression (Plosser/Warsh/Hoenig). Closer to 11–18 range than the extreme 2–5 emergency launch.

**DISSENT_INTENSITY:**
The formal vote goes to alternative B with at most one potential dissent (Yellen supported A). In the straw vote on statement language, ~9–10 out of all participants favored retaining the contested sentence. Rosengren and Evans expressed sympathy for A. Plosser/Bullard/Warsh all have policy preferences that diverge from the center. Plosser's inflation forecast diverges dramatically from the majority. Warsh is uncomfortable with Treasury purchases entirely. Hoenig cautious. Lacker also has distinctive views on balance-sheet mechanics. But there are no formal multiple dissents in the final vote, and the discussion, while intellectually rich and lively, does not reflect "heated" or "sharp personal exchanges." There is substantive economic disagreement (output gap concept, inflation risks, effectiveness of QE) but the majority coalesces around B. Score: **38** — moderate differences, notable emphasis divergence on inflation risks and QE effectiveness, one or two potential reservations, but ultimate convergence on the action.

**FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS:**
Financial stability and conditions are extensively discussed. Warsh devotes his entire go-around to financial markets (equity markets, convertibles, corporate bonds, stress tests). Duke's entire statement is about banking conditions and stress tests. Rosengren leads with stress-test financial stability risk. Yellen devotes significant discussion to banking system capital adequacy. Lockhart discusses financial market fragility and insurance sector. Bernanke's summary explicitly covers financial conditions at length. Multiple participants cite the upcoming stress-test disclosure as the single biggest near-term financial stability risk. Score: **72** — prominent topic, most participants address it substantively and at length.

**FORWARD_GUIDANCE_TONE:**
The statement maintains the existing QE programs (no change) and reaffirms zero rate "for an extended period." The forward signal is for continued accommodation with possible further action if conditions deteriorate further. Yellen argues for more purchases now. The Greenbook assumes zero rates for the entire forecast period. Bernanke explicitly says "I don't see much scope for a stronger recovery" and that further stimulus would be good but "better to stand pat on purchases this time." The guidance is "low for long, watchful waiting, prepared to do more." Score: **12** — strong easing bias, "low for long" emphasis, extended accommodation committed, potential further action signaled if needed. This is in the 8–18 range.

**UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED:**
Uncertainty is pervasive and explicitly named throughout. Bullard: "We're going to have to see more data... turning points are notoriously hard to predict." Tarullo devotes significant time to historical false dawns. Bernanke: "very tentative... subject to revision." Multiple participants reference the stress-test outcome as a key near-term unknown. Plosser acknowledges "sizable error bands." Pianalto: "the level of uncertainty around my projection is unusually great." Bullard: "actual events have far outstripped these measures of uncertainty, so we are way outside the confidence bands." Bernanke's summary: "participants were very cautious... overall, participants were very cautious." Era anchor: 85–95 for March 2009 trough. April 2009 is one meeting later with some tentative stabilization — still very high uncertainty but perhaps marginally below the absolute peak. Score: **80** — very high uncertainty, most participants emphasize unusual difficulty, "uncharted territory" in balance-sheet operations, wide range of scenarios including "false dawn" discussed.

## Part 2: Evidence and Scores

### 1. INFLATION_PRESSURE
**Score: 18 ±6 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Rosengren: "The significant slack in the labor markets will continue to place a lot of downward pressure on inflation, and there remains a significant risk that deflation will not be avoided."
- Yellen: "I expect core inflation to dip down to 1½ percent this year and 1 percent next year... there is considerable chance of a more sizable fall in inflation."
- Bernanke summary: "Most see inflation as remaining low for the foreseeable future. Slack looks to be high and increasing... deflation risks have moderated but remain a worry for some participants."

**Center of gravity:** The overwhelming majority of participants project core PCE below 2% (most around 1–1.5%) with meaningful deflation risk acknowledged; the minority concern about future upside (Plosser, Warsh, Bullard) is framed as a medium-term tail risk, not current pressure.

**Minority views:** Plosser forecasts PCE above 3% in 2010 and 4% by 2012 using a DSGE model; Warsh and Pianalto also note concerns about upside inflation risks from balance-sheet expansion. These widen the uncertainty band upward by ~6 points.

---

### 2. OUTPUT_GAP
**Score: 15 ±4 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Yellen: "the optimal policy simulation indicates that we should lower the funds rate to negative 7½ percent, if that were feasible... even if we more than double the large-scale asset purchases we have put into motion, we are nowhere near the desirable outcome."
- Kohn: "I also agree with the staff expectation that the subsequent recovery will be gradual, more gradual than in past recessions, with a large output gap persisting for some time."
- Bernanke summary: "Slack looks to be high and increasing, as reflected in high unemployment and low capacity utilization."

**Center of gravity:** Unanimous agreement that the economy is deeply below potential. Q1 contraction was "much steeper than anticipated." Even Plosser, the most optimistic, revised down his 2009 forecast and acknowledges recession conditions, merely arguing the measured gap is smaller than the Greenbook suggests.

**Minority views:** Plosser argues the effective output gap is smaller because equilibrium output has fallen (financial shock acts like negative productivity shock), which provides some upside uncertainty. Bullard echoes this via New Keynesian output gap concept.

### 3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 16 ±4 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Yellen: "the data are appalling. Not only has the unemployment rate risen 1.7 percentage points over the past four months... the unemployment rate appears to understate the true magnitude of the labor market deterioration... the underemployment rate... will top the level reached in 1982 by year-end. We expect it to climb from its current rate of about 14½ percent to over 16 percent."
- Fisher/Texas survey: "our firms are reporting that job cuts exceed hires by a 10 to 1 margin."
- Bernanke summary: "labor markets remained weak... firms are undertaking permanent reductions in labor force; and involuntary part-time work is unusually elevated."

**Center of gravity:** Labor market in near-depression condition. Unemployment heading toward 9.6–10%+, underemployment approaching 16%, permanent layoffs dominating, no net hiring anywhere. Multiple participants call conditions "appalling," "dismal."

**Minority views:** Plosser notes labor market reallocations suggest NAIRU may have risen, implying less cyclical slack than the Greenbook — but still concedes conditions are extremely weak.

### 4. CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT
**Score: 22 ±6 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Yellen: "The pace of contraction in aggregate spending appears to have slowed. Consumer spending and housing show welcome signs of stabilization." But also: "I remain concerned that the banking system is not really strong enough to expand credit in the manner required for a rapid economic rebound."
- Lockhart: "My retail contacts advise that the weak retail spending numbers of March are more characteristic of what to expect in the second quarter... there is considerable downward pressure on prices [in housing]."
- Bernanke summary: "Production and investment remain weak... consumer spending shows some signs of bottoming out... Housing activity is giving some indications of stabilizing at a very low level."

**Center of gravity:** Spending and investment remain in contraction but the rate of decline has slowed materially from Q4 2008's freefall. Capital expenditures index still negative (Lacker), commercial real estate financing near zero (Lockhart), but consumer spending showing tentative stabilization. Score reflects "still contracting but stabilizing" rather than "in freefall."

**Minority views:** Plosser is somewhat more optimistic on consumer spending (April tracking +0.4% m/m); Warsh and Stern note improving tone. This provides modest upside uncertainty.

### 5. FINANCIAL_TIGHTNESS
**Score: 68 ±8 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Kohn: "borrowing rates, terms, and conditions for many private borrowers are still very tight, and we would have considered them very tight at any other time even if they have improved some."
- Lockhart: "one contact said that there is almost no financing available [for commercial real estate]. Even after accounting for weakening fundamentals, this source indicated that there is a serious lack of liquidity."
- Warsh: "Northern Trust, Credit Suisse, and BB&T are in the market today raising debt at yield spreads that are remarkable — 275 to 325 over 5-year and 10-year money." [Described as "very encouraging" vs. prior worse levels, but still elevated spreads.]

**Center of gravity:** Financial conditions have improved notably since the March 2009 nadir and the stock market's March 9 low. Corporate bond issuance is "healthy," equity markets up $3 trillion, convertible market "booming." But credit to households and small businesses remains very tight, banking system capital-constrained, commercial real estate financing near-frozen, and markets described as "fragile." Score reflects clearly tight but improving from crisis peak.

**Minority views:** Warsh is more encouraged by financial market improvements; Evans notes "credit conditions are somewhat better." This pulls the lower bound of uncertainty toward 60.

### 6. HAWKISH_DOVISH_LEAN
**Score: 12 ±5 | Confidence: High**

**Evidence:**
- Yellen: "I favor alternative A. The Greenbook projection... foresees unemployment remaining above 9 percent through the end of 2010 and inflation well below our preferred rate for even longer. I consider this unacceptable... it's time to wade in by substantially increasing our purchases of Treasury securities."
- Plosser: "I am against announcing any further increases today... Appropriate monetary policy in this forecast requires that we begin raising the funds rate by the end of this year."
- Bernanke's summary and consensus: near-unanimous support for alternative B (hold existing programs, no new purchases), with "watchful waiting" stance, "standing ready to evaluate" further action.

**Center of gravity:** Maximum accommodation is in place (ZLB + large-scale QE). The overwhelming majority explicitly supports maintaining this stance and not adding to it at this meeting. No one advocates tightening policy now. The forward lean is "more if needed." Policy is deeply dovish by any historical comparison — era anchor is 2–5 for this period.

**Minority views:** Plosser argues for rate hikes within the year (hawkish outlier, would pull score upward to ~15). Yellen argues for immediately more purchases (dovish outlier, pulls score toward 8–10). Center remains around 12.
