### 2. Era Context

August 2000 is the **late-cycle phase of the 1990s expansion**, coming after six Federal Reserve rate hikes totaling 175bp from June 1999 through May 2000. The U.S. economy was at peak output in what became the NBER peak (March 2001). The technology/dot-com boom was still largely intact, unemployment was near 4 percent (historically very tight), productivity growth had been revised sharply higher, and the Fed was on hold after its last hike in May 2000. Inflation was modestly above 2 percent but contained by strong productivity. The dollar was strong; the euro was near historic lows. I will let the text drive the scores, not the era context.

### 3. Directional Signals for Each Core Dimension

**INFLATION_PRESSURE:**
Staff sees inflation pressure as modestly above neutral but partially offset by strong productivity. Stockton notes: "we read the incoming wage data as suggesting that labor markets remain extremely tight. Virtually all of the data on hourly compensation have come in to the high side of our June forecast." However, the upward pressure on wages is "more than offset by our faster estimated structural productivity growth, which has acted to damp the rise in unit labor costs." The forecast projects core PCE moving "above 2 percent by the end of next year." The uptrend in inflation is "more gradual than we were previously projecting." Net assessment: inflation is slightly above target, building gradually, but productivity is dampening it — score in the 55-62 range.

**OUTPUT_GAP:**
Staff sees the economy as still above potential but the pace of above-potential growth is decelerating. Stockton explicitly states: "We still think the 6 percent pace of the past four quarters is not sustainable." The economy needs to slow to prevent inflation pressures from building further. The staff projects "the unemployment rate to rise to about 4-1/4 percent by the end of next year" and describes this as "still too low to prevent a further gradual deterioration in inflation." Meanwhile, labor productivity growth was revised to 3.5 percent this year — meaning potential output was also revised up. The net effect: "our revisions to the demand and supply sides of the forecast were nearly a wash for resource utilization." The economy is still running above potential (growth above sustainable, unemployment below NAIRU), but the gap is narrowing. Score: above 50, in the 58-63 range.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS:**
Staff sees labor market as very tight. Stockton: "labor markets remain extremely tight." The unemployment rate is around 4 percent (well below NAIRU at the time); the forecast projects it rising only to 4.25 percent by end of next year — still very low. The staff notes "there have been more than a few instances in which hiring has been held back by a lack of qualified workers." ECI came in "nearly a percentage point at an annual rate" above expectations. Score: 67-72 range (very tight, consistent with the 1999-2000 anchor of 65-75).

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT:**
Mixed but weighted toward solid/strong. Consumer spending "slowed sharply in the second quarter" but is expected to recover — though "the more subdued pace of spending...is far from baked in the cake." Business investment is a standout: "outlays for capital equipment have continued to surge ahead" and "the investment boom of recent years is now estimated to have been even stronger than previously thought." Housing and auto sectors are weakening (mortgage rates elevated, housing starts trending down, auto inventory overhangs). On net: strong business investment offsets softening consumer and housing spending. Score: 55-60 range (above trend, led by equipment investment, with consumer spending moderating).

**PRODUCTIVITY:**
Staff explicitly discussed and upgraded productivity. Stockton: "the annual revisions portray a pattern of greater acceleration of actual labor productivity, with the annual increase through the second quarter of this year in excess of 5 percent." Staff raised structural productivity growth to "3-1/2 percent this year and 3-3/4 percent in 2001, up about 1/2 percentage point from our last forecast." This is a "productivity boom" — clearly above historical trend. Score: 72-78 range.

Fisher covers financial markets. The yield curve is discussed — forward rates for the euro area have risen, the ECB has hiked; the dollar/euro interest rate differential has narrowed. For the U.S., Fisher notes mortgage rates are "up considerably from their lows." Stockton mentions "tighter monetary policy appears to be leaving its clearest mark on housing activity." Ford Motor Company yield curve is "almost identical to where it was in February" while most corporate yields are "noticeably lower." The overall tone is that financial conditions are somewhat tight — tighter than at the start of the cycle but not severely so. Fisher notes the funds rate has "behaved quite well." The conditions are somewhat restrictive, consistent with being post a 175bp tightening cycle. Score: 57-63 (somewhat tight).

**TREASURY_STRESS:**
Fisher discusses Treasury operations at length, but the discussion is entirely about reserve management, buyback programs, off-the-run vs on-the-run spreads, and foreign central bank bidding procedures — not Treasury market dysfunction. He mentions "a narrowing of these spreads" (on-the-run/off-the-run) after the Fed's operational announcement on July 5. The yield curve shows normal patterns. No language about liquidity problems, auction failures, or settlement issues. Score: 35-40 (normal functioning; yield changes from policy expectations are normal).

Spreads appear generally below normal, with abundant credit availability. Score: 32-38 (somewhat below normal; favorable credit conditions, consistent with late cycle pre-stress environment).
