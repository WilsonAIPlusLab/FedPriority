### 2. Era Context

May 1999: The U.S. economy is in the midst of a sustained late-1990s expansion. The unemployment rate is in the low 4 percent range — historically very tight. The FOMC had eased three times in late 1998 in response to global financial turbulence (Russia/LTCM/Asia). By early 1999, the economy had rebounded strongly. The April 1999 CPI report (released just before this meeting) showed a large 0.4 percentage point jump in the core CPI, prompting significant attention. Bond yields had risen sharply. The staff was wrestling with whether the economy was overheating and whether policy tightening was needed.

### 3. Directional Signals Per Dimension

**INFLATION_PRESSURE:** Staff sees inflation pressure as **modestly above neutral** but contested. The April core CPI printed +0.4 pp, but the staff was "inclined to be fairly sanguine." They note apparel reversal and tobacco rebound as one-off factors. However, they also acknowledge "an unemployment rate in the low 4s will in time prove incompatible with stable or declining inflation" and flag that "prices will most likely tend to accelerate over time unless domestic demand softens considerably." They also note the need for policy tightening. Oil prices are rising and feeding into expectations. The staff's central view is that underlying inflation is not clearly accelerating yet, but the risks are to the upside. Core CPI twelve-month change is described as roughly flat vs. a year earlier (abstracting from tobacco). This puts inflation in the 40-50 range for current level, but the staff's assessment of near-term risk tilts it somewhat above 50. Score: **50-55 range** — at or slightly above target with upside risk acknowledged.

**OUTPUT_GAP:** Staff sees the economy operating **above potential**. Prell says "the picture is still one of strength overall." Real domestic final purchases grew 7.5% in Q1 (even if overstated, still above trend). Staff forecasts real PCE in current quarter "somewhere in the neighborhood of 4 percent." Staff raised its GDP growth projection and sees growth staying well above 2-3% through 1999. The staff also explicitly points to the "likely need for some monetary policy tightening aimed at reining in aggregate demand" — clear language indicating above-potential operation. The late-1990s boom anchor (57-63) is appropriate. Score: **60 range** — clearly above potential / running hot.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS:** Staff sees labor market as **very tight**. Prell says "an unemployment rate in the low 4s" — the actual unemployment rate was approximately 4.2% at this meeting. He notes firms are "working beyond sustainable levels of labor utilization" and that even as business slows, firms will follow through on hiring from a "tight labor market." Companies "would like to add workers but are finding it difficult to do so in a very tight labor market." This is firmly in the 65-75 anchor range for the late-1990s tight labor market. Score: **70 range**.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT:** Staff sees spending as **above trend but with some deceleration expected**. Q1 real domestic final purchases grew 7.5% (though described as overstated due to transitory factors). Real PCE current quarter forecast ~4%. Staff notes "noticeable slackening of expenditure growth this quarter" but that 4% PCE growth is "scarcely a weak number." Residential investment is decelerating. Business investment remains strong (driven by technology adoption and labor market pressure). Net picture: above trend but moderating. Score: **60-62** — above trend.

**PRODUCTIVITY:** Staff has strong and specific views on productivity. Prell says "we've added several tenths to the rate of increase in output per hour through the year 2000" due to structural gains. He describes "substantial structural gains in efficiency through technological innovations, investments in more and better equipment, improved information management, and reorganization of production and distribution processes." He says there is no reason to anticipate gains will fall off markedly. About half the productivity increase is structural (vs. cyclical). Score: **65** — significantly above trend / productivity boom narrative.

**FINANCIAL_CONDITIONS:** Staff sees financial conditions as **broadly accommodative to mildly tightening**. The stock market has "weathered the negative news of recent days in remarkably good fashion." Lower-quality corporate bond issuers have seen access improve. Treasury yields have risen ("have been firming on their own"). Bond markets have adjusted to CPI news. Mr. Fisher notes bid/ask spreads on Treasuries are "a good bit wider" than at the last meeting but this is framed as market adjustment, not crisis. The equity market is essentially still at high levels. Stock and bond prices could turn back up. Overall: conditions remain broadly supportive of growth — accommodative to neutral. Score: **35-40** — broadly accommodative / some tightening of yields but credit accessible, equities high.

**TREASURY_STRESS:** Fisher explicitly notes bid/ask spreads on on-the-run and off-the-run securities are "a good bit wider today than they were at the time of your last meeting." He discusses elevated yield volatility following the CPI release and the PPI head-fake. However, this is framed as normal price adjustment to new information, not market dysfunction. There are no reports of auction failures, settlement failures, or liquidity crises. The yield movements (30-year to 5.87-5.91%) are large but orderly. Score: **45** — slightly elevated bid/ask spreads but orderly market functioning. Not a stress event.

**CREDIT_SPREADS:** Fisher notes "many lower quality corporate bond issuers have seen rates fall and their market access improve of late." This is clearly below-normal spread territory — compressed, accommodative credit. Score: **30** — tight spreads; ample credit availability for most issuers.

**EQUITY_VALUATION:** Staff notes the stock market "weathered the negative news of recent days in remarkably good fashion" and mentions "we can easily see stock and bond prices turning back up in the coming weeks." The wealth effect from high equity prices remains "central to our projection of domestic demand." Prell's key concern is that if stock market stays high, it will sustain above-trend demand. No valuation crash or distress. Elevated valuations are implicit — Greenspan-era "new era" valuations referenced. Score: **25** — elevated/stretched valuations with wealth effect concerns; dot-com era context.

**BANKING_STRESS:** The transcript has limited direct coverage of banking system health. Fisher/Kohn discuss discount window collateral and Y2K preparations for small banks, noting supervisory work is ongoing. The discussion is forward-looking (Y2K risk), not indicating current banking stress. No evidence of NPL problems or capital concerns. The conversation about discount window collateral implies banks are generally in adequate shape, with preparations being made. Score: **30** — healthy with some forward-looking Y2K monitoring; no current stress.

**DOLLAR_STRENGTH:** Fisher's financial report shows a mixed dollar picture. For most currencies globally: "much of the world has declining 3-month rates and declining currencies relative to the dollar" — dollar is broadly strong vs. rest-of-world. However, "two interesting exceptions are Mexico and Canada, whose currencies have been strengthening against the dollar" (dollar weakening vs. NAFTA partners). The net picture is dollar broadly strong globally but with notable North American exceptions. Fisher frames the North American weakness as potentially reflecting "the strength of the U.S. economy and its spillover to the north and to the south" — a nuanced framing. The broad global picture is dollar strength, but the North American exception is notable. Score: **58** — modestly above neutral; broadly strong vs. most currencies, but notable exceptions; net modest strength.
