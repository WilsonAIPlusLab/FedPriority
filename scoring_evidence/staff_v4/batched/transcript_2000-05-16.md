## Part 1: Thinking Process

### 1. Document Type
This is a **transcript** from an FOMC meeting on May 16, 2000. It is a pre-2005, partially unsegmented transcript, but the staff section is clearly identifiable. The document is partially corrupted with right-side text truncation on many lines, but enough content survives to score all major dimensions.

**Staff speakers identified:**
- **MR. REINHART** (Vince Reinhart, Associate Economist): International/foreign economic outlook, dollar/exchange rate analysis
- **MR. PRELL** (Mike Prell, Economist — his final meeting): Domestic economic conditions, inflation, labor markets, outlook discussion
- **MR. FISHER** (Peter Fisher, Manager System Open Market Account): Financial markets, Treasury market operations, yield curves, forward rates
- **MR. STOCKTON** (Dave Stockton, Associate Economist): Wealth effect discussion, consumption

The go-around discussion (Presidents Broaddus, Minehan, Moskow, etc.) that follows is **participant content** and is NOT used for scoring, except where staff (Prell, Reinhart, Stockton) respond to questions — those responses are staff content.

### 2. Era Context
**May 2000** — Late stage of the 1990s expansion. The NASDAQ had peaked in March 2000 and had a notable correction (the "dot-com bust" was just beginning). The economy was running at full/above-full capacity. The Fed had been tightening since June 1999 (this is Prell's last meeting; he notes five prior policy actions). Core CPI was around 2.2-2.4%. Unemployment was well below 4%. This is a classically "hot" economy with rising inflation concerns.

I must score what the **staff says**, not what I recall about the era.

### 3. Directional Signals — Each Dimension

**INFLATION_PRESSURE:**
Staff sees inflation ABOVE the neutral midpoint/target. Prell notes: "12-month change in the core backed off to 2.2 percent, after spiking to 2.4 percent last month. That number is the same as a year ago, but still about a quarter point above [the cyclical low]." Staff forecast shows "overall PCE inflation will run only in the low 2 percent area in 2001" but only with tightening. More critically, Prell states "the signs are clearer now that labor markets have become unsustainably tight and that an appreciable rise in the unemployment rate probably will be required to hang onto the bulk of the disinflation progress." He warns of "an uptrend in core inflation" and explicitly says "inflation will be tending to [rise]" if policy is insufficient. The baseline with additional tightening still shows inflation running modestly above target. Scenarios without tightening put core PCE at 3 percent by 2002. Prell also says "the news of the intermeeting period has highlighted the basic imbalances in the economy" and that inflation is rising. This is clearly above target and trending higher — moderately elevated, not severe.
**Direction: ABOVE midpoint. Score: 62-65.**

**OUTPUT_GAP:**
Staff sees economy ABOVE potential. Prell explicitly states "the signs are clearer now that labor markets have become unsustainably tight" — implying above potential. The Greenbook paragraph (referenced by McTeer) says "The expansion of aggregate supply has been substantial, but nowhere near enough to match the advance of demand." Prell confirms: "whether we look at the unemployment rate or industrial capacity utilization, in both cases the level of utilization has been rising. So, by definition in essence, the growth of aggregate supply has been less than the growth of aggregate demand." This is unambiguous above-potential language. The output gap is clearly positive and meaningful, not just marginally above. The economy has been running hot for some time. Prell warns of needing a recession ("not just a growth recession, but a meaningful absolute decline in activity") to correct imbalances — implying a large gap to close.
**Bright-line rule: "above potential" → MUST be > 50.** Score: 63-67.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS:**
Staff sees labor market as VERY TIGHT. Prell: "labor markets have become unsustainably tight." Unemployment is implied to be well below NAIRU (consistent with May 2000 actuals of ~3.9%). Staff warns of needing an "appreciable rise in the unemployment rate." The Greenbook anchor year for LABOR_TIGHTNESS 65-75 covers 1999-2000. ECI rose "very sharply in the first quarter" and staff raised their projection for compensation. Wage pressures building. Score should be high 60s.
**Direction: Well above NAIRU. Score: 68-72.**

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT:**
Staff sees spending/investment as STRONG but with signs of moderation beginning. Prell forecasts "aggregate demand growth will slacken appreciably" with tightening, but currently demand is still elevated. Wealth effects from stock market gains are still working through ("we got over a percentage point [wealth effect] in the first quarter"), but fading. Housing starts up 3% in April. The stock market has "on balance, flattened out now" (NASDAQ correction). Staff projects deceleration but current conditions are still above trend. The picture is strong but with emerging headwinds.
**Direction: Above trend currently, decelerating. Score: 60-63.**

**PRODUCTIVITY:**
Staff explicitly discusses productivity. Prell mentions "longer-range escalation of productivity" as a possible benign scenario. Reinhart notes productivity gains likely in Canada and some European countries. The key Prell statement: the economy's strong growth reflects "partly a reflection of an adjustment to a higher potential growth rate that has come with an acceleration of productivity." Stockton and Prell discuss the supply-side expansion. The staff believes productivity has risen substantially but that demand has outpaced even elevated supply. The "new economy" productivity boom is acknowledged. Score above trend.
**Direction: Above historical trend. Score: 63-67.**

**FINANCIAL_CONDITIONS:**
Staff (Fisher) describes financial conditions as having **tightened** over the intermeeting period. Forward rates have "risen sharply since mid-April." Euro forward rates have "backed up." The U.S. Treasury yield curve is "a little higher but also a bit more inverted than it was at the time of your last meeting." Credit spreads have "backed up by around 50 basis points." Fisher notes credit "is more expensive than it was at the time of your last meeting." However, overall conditions are not described as severely tight — just tightened via rate hikes and market repricing. The Fed has been tightening (5 prior actions), and rates are moving toward 7-8%. Conditions are tighter than neutral but not crisis-level.
**Direction: Tighter than neutral. Score: 58-62.**

**TREASURY_STRESS:**
Fisher discusses Treasury markets in detail. He describes bid/ask spreads on the 10-year note as showing "a continuing widening" (bottom left chart). He notes the long end "is having a harder time making that adjustment" to supply changes. However, bill market "has made a reasonably smooth transition." This is NOT crisis-level dysfunction — it's elevated but manageable concerns primarily about reduced Treasury supply and on-the-run/off-the-run spreads. Fisher also notes "Treasury is acting as somewhat of a magnet, pulling the long end of these yield curves down." The description is of somewhat strained but not dysfunctional markets. Some unusual dynamics from Treasury debt reduction.
**Bright-line check: Yield movements alone are not dysfunction. Score: 45-50 (slightly elevated but no systemic dysfunction).**

**CREDIT_SPREADS:**
Fisher notes non-Treasury yield curves have "backed up by around 50 basis points" and that "credit is more expensive than it was at the time of your last meeting. But even with credit spreads widening to quite wide levels by the standards of the last several years..." This phrasing "quite wide levels by the standards of the last several years" is significant — implying spreads have widened notably. However, by historical crisis standards, spreads are not at distressed levels. The 2000 credit market was stressed relative to the extremely compressed late-1990s levels but not at crisis widths.
**Direction: Somewhat above normal, wider than recent years. Score: 55-60.**

**EQUITY_VALUATION:**
Fisher and Prell discuss equity markets. The NASDAQ had its correction and the "stock market turmoil around mid-month." Prell specifically says: "by many conventional metrics, we've experienced a speculative bubble of extraordinary proportions. The market-correction scenario in the Greenbook — though involving a considerable, 25 percent decline from the March peak for the Wilshire — may well understate the downside potential here." Staff views equity valuations as still very elevated — a speculative bubble. Despite the NASDAQ correction, staff still sees overvaluation/bubble risk. This is high concern about overvaluation (not undervaluation), so score is LOW (= less stress from undervaluation perspective). Wait — polarity check: EQUITY_VALUATION: Higher = MORE equity market stress/concern. The staff's view is that equities remain in bubble territory with downside risk — this is elevated concern. Score: 18-25 (elevated overvaluation concern — the bubble is still there despite early correction).

**Polarity check:** "Higher = more stress/concern." Staff says equities are in a speculative bubble — that's high concern/stress from overvaluation. But the rubric says 1-15 = "extreme overvaluation concern / bubble / irrational exuberance / frothy" and 16-30 = "elevated valuations / stretched / rich by historical standards." Staff explicitly says "speculative bubble of extraordinary proportions" — that's clearly in the 10-20 range for overvaluation. Score: 15-20.

**BANKING_STRESS:**
Banking system not directly discussed by staff in terms of health or stress. Fisher discusses operations but not bank capital or lending conditions specifically. Prell briefly mentions "banks and other financial institutions have financed a construction boom" and potential for "credit crunches" in a downturn, but says "problems in this sphere aren't as great as they have been at some times in the past." This implies banking is relatively sound (low stress). But the discussion is brief.
**Direction: Not prominently discussed; brief mention suggests low stress. Score: 32-38 (healthy with minor concerns about potential future vulnerability).**

**DOLLAR_STRENGTH:**
Reinhart: "the dollar appreciated 3-1/2 percent relative to the major currencies over the intermeeting period." The dollar's strength is analyzed at length. The strong dollar is helping to contain domestic inflation by diverting demand abroad and keeping import prices down. However, staff projects the dollar will depreciate going forward, which would add to inflation pressures. Currently the dollar is strong and rising.
**Bright-line rule: Dollar appreciation → score MUST be > 50.** Score: 62-68 (notable appreciation, analyzed as meaningful force but not extreme).

### 4. Inflation Sub-Components

**INFLATION_GOODS:**
CPI overall was flat; core rose 0.2%. Energy prices in line with expectations. Some goods price pressure from oil (manufacturing inputs). Import prices stable due to strong dollar. Goods inflation modest but present. Score: 52-55.

**INFLATION_SERVICES:**
Wage pressures building, ECI rose sharply. Services inflation driven by tight labor markets. Prell warns of upward trend in core inflation. Score: 57-62.

**INFLATION_HOUSING:**
Housing discussed briefly. Starts up 3% in April but mixed signals. Some reports of slowing. Not directly addressed in terms of shelter inflation. N/A or near neutral.

**INFLATION_EXPECTATIONS:**
Prell is concerned about inflation expectations. Reinhart says "we anticipate that inflation expectations are going to be deteriorating somewhat in the environment that we are forecasting." Prell in response to questions notes the market is "comfortable with the policy of gradual" increases — which he finds too complacent. Broaddus (in go-around, not scoring) references TIPS spread widening. Staff sees expectations as at risk of moving up. Score: 58-63 (somewhat elevated/at risk).
