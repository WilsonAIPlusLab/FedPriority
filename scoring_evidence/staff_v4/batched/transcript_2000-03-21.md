### 2. Era Context

March 2000 is the late dot-com boom / peak of the late-1990s expansion. The U.S. economy is in its record-long expansion with very tight labor markets (unemployment ~4%), rapid GDP growth (Q4 1999 came in above 7%), strong productivity growth, and equity markets near historic highs. The Fed has been tightening rates. This is a period of above-potential growth, tight labor markets, and nascent but watched inflation pressures — consistent with scores above 50 for output gap, labor tightness, consumption/investment, and moderate-to-elevated inflation.

### 3. Directional Signals — Core Dimensions

**INFLATION_PRESSURE:**
Staff sees inflation as modestly above neutral and rising on concern. Prell reports CPI at 3-1/4% year-on-year (oil-driven). Core inflation remains "subdued" but Prell explicitly flags: "the reports of compensation pressures will be mirrored in an accelerating trend of wages and benefits in the coming months"; the Michigan SRC survey showed "a noticeable rise in average short- and long-run price expectations, may be a worrisome sign." Energy pass-through risk is highlighted. This is clearly ABOVE the midpoint — elevated but not yet unanchored. Score: **60–65**.

**OUTPUT_GAP:**
Staff sees the economy well above potential. Prell: "The now record-long expansion has continued to outstrip most analysts' expectations." Real GDP grew above 7% in Q4 1999. Staff raised productivity estimate to 3.2%. Domestic demand pressing on supply. Prell notes: "the economy is not to overheat more as a sequence of tightening of financial market conditions." Staff expects the economy to need "substantial further increase in the fed funds rate." BRIGHT-LINE: clearly above potential → score MUST be > 50. Score: **65–68**.

**LABOR_TIGHTNESS:**
Unemployment rate cited around 4–4.4% (Texas at 4.4%, District at 4.5%, nation near 4%). Prell: "We think the labor markets are unsustainably tight." Anecdotal reports from Beige Book: "it's costing employers more to hire and retain staff." Prell: "we would begin to see the real wage pressures creep into the actual outcomes for compensation growth." ANCHOR: below 4% unemployment → MUST be above 55, likely 65–70. Score: **68**.

**CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT:**
Prell: GDP grew above 7% in Q4 1999; "private domestic final purchases, a good measure of aggregate domestic demand, are now projected to grow fully 2 percentage points faster than was expected in the previous Greenbook." Real final sales at about 5% annual rate in H2 1999, same pace projected in H1 2000. Consumer sentiment high. Business investment robust. Score: **68**.

**PRODUCTIVITY:**
Explicitly discussed. Prell: "we've raised our sights on the pace of structural improvements in output per hour. We're now putting it at 3.2 percent per annum for the nonfarm business sector this year and next." This is well above the ~2% historical trend. Staff views this as largely sustainable: "we also think that the sustainable improvements in efficiency coming from the use of more and better capital equipment and from operational changes likely are coming at a faster pace." Score: **72**.

**FINANCIAL_CONDITIONS:**
Prell: "the moderation in domestic demand in our forecast thus continues to hinge largely on the influence of the less accommodative financial conditions that we've anticipated." Fisher has detailed financial conditions: credit spreads widened to and beyond 1998 highs (roughly doubled from pre-1998 levels); bid-ask spreads on Treasuries widened; yield curve inverted at long end. However, Prell also: "financial market conditions are reasonably supportive of an expansion of household and business spending. There is no shortage of credit available." Also: "the cost of equity capital seems to be very low for many firms." Overall, financial conditions are tighter than earlier in the expansion but not restrictive. The staff's own characterization is that conditions are "less accommodative" but still broadly supportive, and further tightening is needed/anticipated. BRIGHT-LINE: Not "tight" or "restrictive" overall; "less accommodative" → slightly above neutral. Score: **55**.

**TREASURY_STRESS:**
Fisher's entire presentation (pages 3–8) addresses unusual Treasury market conditions: yield curve inversion at the long end (30-year bond volatility exceeds 5- and 10-year); correlation of Treasury yield changes has broken down "extremely rapidly"; bid-ask spreads widened; repo rates showing Treasury scarcity. Fisher: "The suddenness of the breakdown needs to be understood as partly reflecting the fact that the market is not seeing this breakdown in relationships to the 30-year bond as a transient event." However, this is described as structural/supply-driven (Treasury buybacks, fiscal surplus), not market dysfunction in the liquidity-crisis sense. Fisher characterizes it as "a very dramatic altering of pricing relationships that the market has relied on for many years" — elevated concern, but orderly market functioning. Score: **55**.

**CREDIT_SPREADS:**
Fisher: "Ten-year credit spreads have widened to their 1998 highs and beyond; and ten-year spread relationships among private credits have become much less stable." "Since the fall of 1998, all three of these spreads have traded at roughly double the averages that had prevailed from 1994 to August 1998." This is clearly elevated — widened significantly, well above pre-1998 norms, and roughly doubled. BRIGHT-LINE: widened → score MUST be > 50. Score: **65**.

**EQUITY_VALUATION:**
Prell: "I still find the valuations of many so-called tech companies—and the rationales analysts give for them—rather wacky." The Wilshire 5000 was up 4% from the prior week, above the Greenbook's flat stock market assumption. This is a staff concern about elevated/overvalued equity markets. Johnson: tech stocks "have risen extremely rapidly." HIGHER score = MORE stress/concern. The staff clearly sees equities as elevated and concerning. Score: **20** (stretched/elevated valuations, concern about bubble characteristics, though not "unprecedented" bubble language).

**BANKING_STRESS:**
Not specifically discussed in staff presentations. Prell notes "no shortage of credit available" and "cost of equity capital seems to be very low for many firms." Jordan (participant) mentions banks having strong loan growth. No banking stress discussed by staff. Score: **N/A**.

**DOLLAR_STRENGTH:**
Johnson: The euro remains weak vs. dollar despite ECB rate hike ("this increase in their official rates had no perceptible effect on the value of the euro in terms of the dollar"). Yen strength is described as a risk (interventions by Japanese authorities). Johnson projects "almost flat... outcome for the dollar" going forward, with forces on both sides. The dollar appears broadly stable to modestly strong (Euro weakness implies dollar strength). Score: **55** — modestly above neutral reflecting euro weakness/dollar strength, but no dramatic appreciation signal.
