# FOMC Meeting Evidence Comparison: 1996-12-17

## Meeting Context
December 1996. The funds rate stood at 5.25%, unchanged since January 1996. The U.S. economy was in the midst of a sustained expansion, with GDP growth having decelerated from above-trend earlier in the year to near-trend by Q4. Unemployment stood at approximately 5.2–5.4%. Core CPI inflation was running just below 3%, with CPI bias adjustment discussions suggesting true inflation nearer 2%. Greenspan's "irrational exuberance" speech had been delivered December 5, 1996, twelve days before this meeting, and asset prices and financial stability received elevated attention. The policy decision was to hold at 5.25% with an asymmetric directive biased toward tightening.

### A1. INFLATION_PRESSURE

**Transcript: 47 ±7 [High confidence]**

Center-of-gravity: Current inflation is benign — core measures stable to slightly declining, with CPI bias adjustments suggesting true inflation near 2%. Most forecasts project a gradual uptick through 1997–98. Upside risk acknowledged but no current acceleration.

Representative quotes:
- MR. MEYER: "I read core measures of inflation as stable to still declining."
- MR. HOENIG: "both we and the Board staff are projecting rising core inflation, even allowing for the technical adjustments."
- MR. BROADDUS: "the underlying true inflation rate is in some sense closer to 2 percent"
- MR. PARRY: "only a very gradual upward trend in underlying inflation. Our forecast shows core CPI rising from under 2-3/4 percent this year to close to 3 percent in 1998."

Minority views: Melzer sees inflation risks as "somewhat greater now" and favors action; McTeer finds price indicators (dollar, gold, commodities) "fairly reassuring."

---

**Minutes: 46 ±7 [Moderate confidence]**

Center-of-gravity: Members agree inflation is currently subdued with risks tilted upward. Current core readings show little trend and even some decline, but rising labor compensation costs create credible forward risk.

Representative quotes:
- "Inflation had remained subdued, but the members continued to view the risks as tilted toward increases in the future."
- "Measures of core inflation had displayed little trend and even some decline over the past year."
- "Labor compensation costs clearly were rising at a faster pace in the context of persistently tight labor markets, and an upturn in core price inflation seemed quite possible at some point."

Minority views: None noted — broad agreement on this assessment.

### A3. LABOR_TIGHTNESS

**Transcript: 62 ±5 [High confidence]**

Center-of-gravity: Near-unanimous view that labor markets are tight. Unemployment at 5.2–5.4%, possibly below NAIRU. Widespread worker shortages reported across districts. Wage pressures emerging, particularly in skilled occupations, but not yet broad-based across all industries.

Representative quotes:
- MR. BROADDUS: "labor markets remain exceptionally tight in our region. We hear that from virtually everyone"
- MR. STERN: "how tight labor markets are and how difficult it is to find both skilled and unskilled workers. This is translating into higher wages."
- MS. MINEHAN: "A mutual fund company just instituted an across-the-board salary increase of between 5 and 10 percent on top of a merit increase of 5 percent"
- MR. BOEHNE: "Getting 'qualified workers' is becoming an increasingly familiar refrain... wage increases still remain in the 3 to 4 percent area"

Minority views: No dissent from the "tight" characterization. Debate centers on whether tightness will produce broad inflation pass-through.

**Minutes: 60 ±6 [High confidence]**

## Cross-Source Notes

**Discussion DISSENT_INTENSITY gap (Transcript 25 vs. Minutes 10)**: The transcript records verbatim dissent — Melzer explicitly stating he "would favor a modest increase in the degree of reserve restraint" and Broaddus indicating his opposition to a snugging "would have been less than heated." The minutes record only "Votes against this action: None" with no reference to Melzer's preference or near-dissent. The minutes systematically suppress the within-meeting range of preferences that were expressed on record.

**Discussion FINANCIAL_STABILITY_EMPHASIS gap (Transcript 38 vs. Minutes 25)**: The transcript preserves named individual statements from Minehan (extended discussion of leverage, liquidity ratios, mutual fund flows), Lindsey (three structural risks centered on irrational exuberance), Yellen ("significant continuing risk"), and Rivlin ("most worrisome problem on the horizon"). The minutes consolidate this into a single paragraph noting elevated valuations and a monitoring commitment, reducing the apparent weight of the topic.

**Discussion UNCERTAINTY_EXPRESSED gap (Transcript 35 vs. Minutes 45)**: Directional disagreement. The transcript's lower score reflects the actual near-term growth confidence expressed by participants — a very narrow GDP forecast range (2.0–2.4%), Kelley's "watchful waiting" characterization, and Meyer's observation that the range is "remarkably tight." The minutes' higher score reflects explicit uncertainty language ("a great deal of uncertainty," "good deal of uncertainty") that appears in synthesized form without the offsetting expressions of near-term confidence visible in the transcript.

**Staff FINANCIAL_CONDITIONS gap (Transcript 47 vs. Minutes 32)**: The staff transcript contains only brief Q&A fragments from Prell; the full Greenbook data review — with its extensive language about "ample availability of credit," declining yields, and wealth effects — appears only through the minutes' staff review summary. The transcript score (47) reflects near-absence of discussion and carries Low confidence; the minutes score (32) is the better-evidenced figure.

**Discussion and Staff convergence on core economic dimensions**: INFLATION_PRESSURE (Discussion T: 47, Discussion M: 46, Staff T: 48, Staff M: 47), OUTPUT_GAP (Discussion T: 52, Discussion M: 51, Staff T: 55, Staff M: 51), and CONSUMPTION_INVESTMENT (Discussion T: 54, Discussion M: 55, Staff T: 55, Staff M: 54) all show tight cross-source agreement. LABOR_TIGHTNESS shows a persistent gap between discussion (62) and staff (52–55), consistent with the transcript's record of nearly every participant devoting substantial commentary to labor market tightness, while the staff minutes present labor conditions more factually (5.4% unemployment, solid payroll growth) without the qualitative intensity of the district go-around reports.
